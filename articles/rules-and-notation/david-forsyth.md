# David Forsyth

David Forsyth (1854–1909) was a Scottish solicitor and chess organiser whose private shorthand for writing down positions became one of the most widely used conventions in chess. Every time a club player copies a position out of an analysis board, the first field of the resulting FEN string is, in essence, the scheme he published in Glasgow in 1883.[^s1][^s2] He was also a capable over-the-board player, a key figure in founding Scottish chess administration and, after emigrating, a New Zealand champion.[^s1][^s4]

## Life and chess career

Chess Scotland's biography records that Forsyth was born on 16 May 1854 in Alness, the son of a farmer, and that he only learned the game at about 25 but improved quickly.[^s1] He worked as a solicitor, joining the Glasgow Chess Club in 1883, where he served as secretary and later treasurer; after moving to Edinburgh in 1887 he became secretary of the Edinburgh Chess Club and won its club championship.[^s1] He played in the Scottish Championships of 1884, 1886, 1892, 1895 and 1896, and a later New Zealand report quoted in the biography says that at the first Scottish congress he scored well against the country's strongest players and for a time looked the favourite.[^s1] The same report credits him with notable skill at blindfold play, and the biography describes him as a strong problem solver though not a composer.[^s1]

Forsyth emigrated to New Zealand in early 1899, joined the Otago Chess Club in Dunedin and later became its secretary.[^s1] The New Zealand Chess Federation's list of national champions records him as the winner of the fourteenth championship, held in Christchurch in 1901, representing Dunedin.[^s4] He died at the end of December 1909.[^s1]

## Organiser and journalist

When the *Glasgow Weekly Herald* chess column proposed a national association in 1883, its 13 October column named "Mr D. Forsyth" as interim secretary and asked would-be members to write to him; by December he had received notice from 138 people intending to join.[^s3] The Scottish Chess Association was founded on 2 February 1884, and Forsyth was elected its Secretary and Treasurer, holding both posts until he left for New Zealand.[^s1][^s3] He helped with the *Herald* column, wrote the chess column of the *Weekly Scotsman* and arranged inter-city and Anglo-Scottish club matches; in New Zealand he edited a column in the *Southland Weekly Times* and compiled what may have been the first regular newspaper column on Go, in the *Otago Witness* from 1902 to 1903.[^s1]

## The notation

Writing in *The Chess Bouquet* (1897), F. R. Gittins said Forsyth had long used a notation of his own for recording positions and, because friends found it so useful, explained it in the *Glasgow Weekly Herald* of 10 February 1883; Edward Winter's Chess Notes reproduces that original proposal from page 7 of the issue.[^s2] The method reads the board rank by rank from a8 to h8 and downwards, uses capital letters for White's pieces and lower-case for Black's, and counts runs of empty squares with a number; Forsyth suggested vertical dividers between ranks, but slanting strokes were used in print.[^s1] Its value was economy: a position could be printed or sent by post as one short line instead of a diagram. Winter notes that usage was never fully standardised, and records a 1904 *British Chess Magazine* item that printed problems in Forsyth notation, which it recommended and explained, to get round typesetting difficulties.[^s2]

The Saavedra study shows how compact it is: Chess Scotland gives the piece placement as `8/8/1KP5/3r4/8/8/8/k7`, which this article's `fen` field completes with the side to move and the other computer-era fields.[^s1] A common pitfall when reading old sources is to assume the modern six-field record; Forsyth's original covered only the board itself.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Forsyth sits under **chess notation**, and **Forsyth–Edwards Notation** is **named after** him: Chess Scotland notes that Stephen J. Edwards adapted the scheme for computers, so the modern format links him to **Steven J. Edwards** and to Portable Game Notation.[^s1] The **Saavedra position** is the standard textbook example of his notation, and his reputation as a solver and blindfold player ties him to **chess problems** and **blindfold chess**.[^s1][^s2] He was a founding officer and **member of** the **Scottish Chess Association**, and **played in** the **New Zealand Chess Championship**, which he won in 1901.[^s3][^s4]

# FIDE Laws of Chess

The FIDE Laws of Chess are the rulebook for over-the-board chess published by FIDE, the International Chess Federation. As of November 2026 the text in force is the one that took effect on 1 January 2023; the Rules Commission keeps the previous 2018–2023 text only as a historical document.[^s1][^s2] The 2023 amendments were proposed by the Rules Commission and approved by the FIDE General Assembly at the 91st FIDE Congress in Chennai in 2022.[^s3] A game must be played under these Laws to be FIDE-rated, while FIDE only recommends them for other competitive games.[^s1]

## Structure

The Laws open with a short preface and then fall into two parts.[^s1] The **Basic Rules of Play** (Articles 1–5) describe the game itself: its nature and objective, the initial position, how each piece moves (including castling, en passant and promotion), the physical act of moving with the touch-move rule, and how a game is completed.[^s1] The **Competition Rules** (Articles 6–12) govern tournament play: the chessclock, irregularities such as illegal moves, recording the moves, drawn games, points, players' conduct and the role of the arbiter.[^s1] Appendices cover rapid chess, blitz, algebraic notation and play with blind and visually disabled players, and separate Guidelines, explicitly not part of the Laws, deal with adjourned games, Chess960 and games without increment.[^s1] A glossary closes the text; it defines rapid chess as a game with more than 10 but less than 60 minutes per player, and standard chess as at least 60 minutes.[^s1]

The preface states a principle club players often overlook: the Laws cannot foresee every situation, so where a case is not regulated the arbiter decides by analogy with similar regulated situations, relying on competence, sound judgement and objectivity rather than ever more detailed rules.[^s1]

## Key rules a club player should know

- **How a game ends.** Article 5.2 lists three kinds of draw within the Basic Rules: stalemate, a dead position in which neither player can checkmate with any series of legal moves, and agreement between the players.[^s1] The key position above (king and bishop against king) is a dead position, so the game is drawn at once.
- **Automatic draws.** Under Article 9.6 the arbiter ends the game as a draw if the same position appears at least five times, or after 75 moves by each player with no pawn move or capture; a checkmate on the last move still stands.[^s1] These sit beside the claim-based threefold repetition and fifty-move rules in Article 9.
- **Illegal moves.** Article 7.5 restores the position before the irregularity, gives the opponent two extra minutes for a first completed illegal move, and makes a second one lose the game unless checkmate has become impossible.[^s1]
- **Default time.** Unless event regulations say otherwise, the default time under Article 6.7 is zero: a player not at the board when the clock starts loses, unless the arbiter decides otherwise.[^s1]
- **Electronic devices.** Article 11.3 bans notes, sources of information and unapproved electronic devices in the playing venue; a device evidently on a player's person means a lost game unless the event sets a lighter penalty.[^s1]
- **Penalties.** Article 12.9 gives the arbiter a graded menu, from a warning or time adjustments through declaring a game lost, a fine, or exclusion and expulsion from the event.[^s1]

A common pitfall is assuming that casual or online conventions apply in a rated game: a second illegal move, an arrival a minute late or a phone in a pocket can each cost the game outright under the standard rules.[^s1]

## History

Unifying the rules was one of FIDE's founding aims when it was created in Paris in July 1924.[^s4][^s5] The FIDE Arbiters' Manual dates the first official rules to 1929, published in French, with a revised edition in 1952; Edward Winter records that a sub-committee of Folke Rogard, Berman, Golombek and Wade produced the final version of that revision, which was approved and then passed at the 1953 Schaffhausen Congress.[^s4][^s5] An annotated edition followed in 1966, and in 1974 the Permanent Rules Commission published the first English edition.[^s4] The 1997 restructuring introduced the split into Basic Rules, Competition Rules and appendices; in 2016 Guidelines and a glossary were added, and the 2018 text brought significant changes to illegal-move and draw rules.[^s4] From 1997 the Rules Commission adopted a four-year amendment cycle.[^s4]

The rulebook is not frozen. At its meeting of 23 August 2026 the FIDE Council resolved to discuss further a proposed draft of the Laws prepared by a working group led by FIDE Executive Director V. Bologan; no new text had been adopted as of November 2026.[^s6]

## Connections

The Laws are developed and published by **FIDE** through its Rules Commission.[^s2][^s4] Individual rule nodes in this graph, such as **en passant**, **castling**, **stalemate**, **threefold repetition** and **touch-move**, are parts of the Basic or Competition Rules, and the **fifty-move rule** sits in Article 9 next to the automatic 75-move draw.[^s1] **Algebraic notation** is defined in Appendix C, **Chess960** is handled by a Guideline outside the Laws proper, and the **arbiter** is the official who applies and interprets the text under Article 12.[^s1]

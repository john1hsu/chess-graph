# Alfred Binet

Alfred Binet (born 8 July 1857 in Nice; died 18 October 1911 in Paris) was a French psychologist and one of the founders of experimental psychology in France.[^s1][^s2] Outside chess he is remembered for the measurement of intelligence; inside chess he is remembered for an early attempt to find out, by asking the masters themselves, how a player can conduct games without seeing the board. His answer, that strong players hold an abstract, meaning-laden picture of the position rather than a photographic one, still frames research on chess memory and visualisation.[^s4][^s5]

## Career

Binet abandoned law in 1878 for medical and scientific study at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris, where he was drawn to Jean-Martin Charcot's work on hypnosis, and stayed until 1891.[^s1] He then joined the psychology laboratory at the Sorbonne and directed it from 1895 until his death.[^s1][^s2] He took a doctorate in natural science in 1894 and, with Henri Beaunis, founded and edited the journal *L'Année psychologique*.[^s2] His 1903 book on the experimental study of intelligence and, above all, the Binet–Simon scale (first version 1905, revised in 1908 and 1911) made him a central figure in mental testing.[^s2] Alongside that work he studied people with exceptional skills, including chess masters and expert mental calculators.[^s2]

## The blindfold chess inquiry

Binet's chess research appeared as an 1893 study of "mnemonic virtuosity" in chess players and in his 1894 book *Psychologie des grands calculateurs et joueurs d'échecs*.[^s5] Rather than test players in a laboratory, he sent a long questionnaire to well-known blindfold players, asking how they pictured the board, whether they saw pieces and colours, and whether they thought in words.[^s4][^s6] He organised the skill of blindfold play around three capacities: chess knowledge, imagination and memory.[^s6]

The replies, as summarised in later reviews, pointed in a consistent direction:

- **Abstract rather than concrete imagery.** Binet distinguished "concrete" visual memory, which keeps the actual shapes and colours of pieces and squares, from "abstract" visual memory, a schematic 64-square board in which attention falls only on the active part of the fight.[^s3] Skilled players did not seem to encode the physical look of pieces and board; they preferred the abstract kind.[^s4]
- **Memory of reasoning.** Binet concluded that blindfold memory is largely a memory of ideas and calculation: each move is retained as part of a plan of attack or defence, which is why a person ignorant of chess could not hold such a game in mind.[^s3]
- **Individual differences.** Not every respondent relied on imagery. One correspondent, Goetz, reported almost no visualisation and played by reason and calculation, while Binet described Siegbert Tarrasch as setting up the board mentally at the start and updating the picture after every move.[^s3]

Because it compared how different experts represented the same task, Gobet and Charness count Binet's chess work among the earliest systematic studies of individual differences in imagery.[^s5]

## Legacy in chess psychology

Binet's conclusions were taken up quickly. In 1907 Alfred Cleveland's article on the psychology of chess in the *American Journal of Psychology* leaned on Binet's book, agreeing that visual imagery matters but arguing it is not indispensable, and adding Harry Nelson Pillsbury's report that he remembered positions more as a record than as a picture.[^s3]

Modern experiments have broadly supported him. Campitelli and Gobet note that when Saariluoma and Kalakoski replaced pieces with dots, memory for positions barely suffered, for masters and medium-strength players alike, which they read as support for Binet's idea of abstract representation; Saariluoma's work also suggested that skill differences in blindfold play come mainly from knowledge stored in long-term memory rather than from raw imagery ability.[^s4] Template theory accounts for this by treating chunks as tied to locations on the board, so that a player can reconstruct details such as colour from where a piece stands.[^s4]

For the club player the practical lesson is the one Binet drew: blindfold and visualisation ability grows out of understanding. Positions are remembered as plans, threats and relationships, so building chess knowledge is itself the main way to improve the "mind's eye".[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Binet sits **under** the **cognitive psychology of chess**, as one of its pioneers before Adriaan de Groot's experimental work.[^s5] His subject was **blindfold chess**, and his findings on abstract imagery bear directly on **visualisation** and **chess expertise**.[^s4][^s6] Later research and **template theory** offered explanations of what he observed.[^s4] He drew on the testimony of masters, including **Siegbert Tarrasch**, whose blindfold method he described.[^s3]

# Opening repertoire

An opening repertoire is the set of openings a player specialises in and normally plays: in its simplest form one choice with White and one reply to each of the opponent's main tries with Black.[^s1] Because no one can know every opening well, the research literature and coaching advice agree that it pays to play openings one has studied in detail rather than to roam across many.[^s1][^s3] Building a repertoire is therefore a training task as much as a list of moves: choosing lines, learning their ideas, and keeping them up to date.

## How much a repertoire holds

Chassy and Gobet estimated opening knowledge from 76,562 games played in 2008 by players rated 1600–2399, treating the move at which a player first left the theory in a database as the edge of that player's knowledge.[^s1] Average known depth rose steadily with strength, from about 14 ply for Class B players to about 18 ply for masters, roughly 1.25 ply per rating class.[^s1] Turning those depths into a tree with three branches per move, their intermediate model put a master's repertoire at around 100,000 opening moves, with the authors noting the assumptions likely make it an underestimate.[^s1] They also report coaching advice that 25–50% of training time goes into building and refining a repertoire.[^s1]

## Why specialising works

Repertoire knowledge is not just stored moves; it changes how a player thinks in the resulting positions. Bilalić, McLeod and Gobet tested 24 players from candidate master to grandmaster, half of whom played the French Defence and half the Sicilian.[^s2] On positions from their own opening (Winawer French for one group, Najdorf Sicilian for the other) players recalled and solved better, by a margin comparable to being about one standard deviation stronger in general skill.[^s2] Inside their specialism they searched deeper and narrower; outside it, broader and shallower.[^s2] The authors conclude that familiarity with a problem area matters more than general problem-solving strategies, which supports studying a narrow set of structures thoroughly.[^s2]

## Choosing and building

Grivas stresses that choice is personal: no opening wins or loses by itself, preferences shift as a player develops, and a trainer can save years of trial and error.[^s3] Study should cover correct move orders, the reasons behind the moves and the middlegame plans each line leads to, and the repertoire needs constant refreshing.[^s3] Giddins's book on the subject, as reviewed by Silman, ranks understanding of positional and tactical ideas above memorisation, argues that style matters more the stronger the player, weighs offbeat lines that dodge preparation against sounder main lines, and treats move orders and transpositions as essential even below master level.[^s4] It also discusses universal systems, such as the Colle or Torre, that cut down the theory a player must learn.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

Common errors include fearing the opponent's preparation, which Grivas sees as a sign of insecurity about one's own lines, and expecting the opening alone to win games.[^s3] A Quality Chess blog post contrasts "principled" players who repeat and gradually improve the same lines with "opportunistic" ones who switch openings to out-prepare opponents; the first rarely win in the opening, the second risk unfamiliar positions.[^s5] It recommends a core repertoire with occasional deviations, so opponents cannot predict everything.[^s5] Giddins likewise discusses when to leave one's usual openings, for example against a well-prepared opponent or when a must-win situation arises.[^s4]

## Example

A typical club or master repertoire against 1.e4 commits to one main defence. The two specialisms in the Bilalić study start from 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 a6 (Najdorf, B90) and 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 Bb4 (Winawer French, C15).[^s2][^s6][^s7] Each defence covers only the main line, so either player must also prepare answers to White's other choices along the way, together with the middlegame plans that follow, because a repertoire is only as complete as its answer to each opponent choice.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

An opening repertoire is a personal selection from the body of chess openings and a practical expression of chess expertise.[^s1] It is the main practical source of the familiar structures that template theory describes, and the specialisation effect shows that knowledge at work.[^s2] It relates to particular systems often chosen as repertoire cores, such as the Najdorf Variation and the French Defence, and to the engine opening book, which plays the same role for a program.[^s6][^s7] It depends on handling transpositions and move orders, and stands in tension with the opening surprise as a competitive tactic.[^s4][^s5]

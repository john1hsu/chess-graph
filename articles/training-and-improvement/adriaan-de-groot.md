# Adriaan de Groot

Adriaan Dingeman de Groot (born 26 October 1914 in Santpoort, near Amsterdam; died August 2006 on the Frisian island of Schiermonnikoog) was a Dutch psychologist and master-strength chess player.[^s1][^s3][^s4] He is remembered in chess as the researcher who first asked, with experimental care, what actually happens in a strong player's head when choosing a move. Herbert Simon called him one of the fathers of cognitive psychology.[^s1]

## Chess career

De Groot played for the Netherlands at two official Chess Olympiads. At Stockholm 1937 he was the team's reserve and scored 9 points from 15 games (+6 =6 −3); at Buenos Aires 1939 he played third board and scored 9½ from 17 (+6 =7 −4), for 18½ from 32 overall (57.8%).[^s5] He also took part in the unofficial Munich event of 1936, finished fourth in the 1938 Dutch Championship, and worked as a chess journalist while a student.[^s6] His over-the-board results were uneven, and after his doctorate a professional chess career was no longer a realistic option.[^s6]

## Thought and Choice in Chess

De Groot studied mathematics, physics and psychology at the University of Amsterdam, and was shaped by the problem-solving theory of the German psychologist Otto Selz.[^s1] He began studying chess thinking around the 1938 AVRO tournament.[^s6] His doctorate, *Het denken van den schaker: een experimenteel-psychologische studie*, was awarded with distinction on 23 October 1946, under the supervision of Géza Révész.[^s3] The English edition, *Thought and Choice in Chess*, appeared from Mouton in The Hague in 1965 and became his most influential work internationally.[^s1][^s4]

His main method was the **think-aloud protocol**: players, including grandmasters, were given an unfamiliar position and asked to say everything they thought while choosing a move.[^s1] Most of his results came from a single test position, now known as "position A".[^s2] The findings surprised the expectations of the time:

- Grandmasters chose clearly better moves, oriented themselves faster and reached decisions sooner, but the overall shape of their search (how many moves and positions they considered, and how deep they went) differed little from that of strong amateurs.[^s1][^s2]
- All players were highly selective, typically looking at only three or four of the roughly thirty legal moves.[^s1]
- Players kept returning to the same continuations, re-examining them with fresh information. De Groot called this **progressive deepening** and saw in it a cycle of orientation, checking and evaluation that he believed typified complex goal-directed thinking in general.[^s1]

A second experiment pointed to perception rather than search as the key. Players saw a position for a few seconds and then rebuilt it from memory; masters were clearly superior, and the participants included Alexander Alekhine, Max Euwe, Reuben Fine, Salo Flohr and Paul Keres.[^s1] This recall task became the standard paradigm of chess-expertise research.

## Later reassessment

The qualitative side of de Groot's protocols was largely neglected; his quantitative results were what endured.[^s1] Because his samples were small, critics such as Holding argued he lacked the statistical power to detect differences in search depth.[^s2] Fernand Gobet's 1998 replication with 48 Swiss players on position A found some skill effects (masters searched about 5.0 plies deep on average, Class B players about 2.9), yet pooled across levels his sample did not differ significantly from de Groot's on any variable tested. Gobet concluded that search and pattern recognition should be integrated rather than set against each other.[^s2] De Groot himself returned to chess in the 1960s, using eye-movement recordings with his student Riekent Jongman, and in 1996 co-authored *Perception and Memory in Chess* with Gobet.[^s1]

## Career outside chess

De Groot held chairs at the University of Amsterdam from 1949 to 1980, first in applied psychology and statistical methods and later in the methodology and foundations of the social sciences, before moving to Groningen.[^s3][^s4] His *Methodology* (English edition 1969) taught generations of Dutch social scientists the "empirical cycle", and his advocacy of objective testing contributed to the founding of the Dutch national testing institute CITO in 1968.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

De Groot is a founder of the **cognitive psychology of chess** and of the wider study of **chess expertise**.[^s1][^s4] He was **influenced by** Otto Selz.[^s1] **Herbert Simon** brought his work to English-speaking researchers, and his recall experiments became the starting point for **chunking theory** and its revision, **template theory**.[^s1][^s2][^s4] His findings on selective, progressively deepening search remain central to the study of **calculation**.[^s1][^s2] As a player he **played in** the **Chess Olympiad** in 1937 and 1939.[^s5]

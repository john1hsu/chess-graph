# Fernand Gobet

Fernand Gobet is a cognitive scientist whose research on expertise, memory and learning sits at the centre of the modern psychology of chess. He is also a strong player in his own right: an International Master who has played many times for the Swiss national team.[^s3] He earned his Ph.D. in psychology at the University of Fribourg in 1992 and then spent six years at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh working with Herbert Simon.[^s1] He later worked at the University of Nottingham (as Senior Research Fellow, then Reader), took a chair in cognitive psychology at Brunel University from 2003, and became Professor of Cognitive Psychology at the University of Liverpool in 2013.[^s1] As of November 2026 his Liverpool profile lists him as a Professorial Research Fellow at the London School of Economics and an Honorary Senior Research Fellow at Liverpool.[^s2]

## Chess career

Gobet played for Switzerland at the Chess Olympiad. At Thessaloniki 1984, as a FIDE Master rated 2360, he played fourth board and scored 5 points from 10 games.[^s4] At Thessaloniki 1988, now an International Master, he again played board four, in a team led by Viktor Korchnoi, scoring 2½ from 7.[^s5] His playing strength matters to his research: like Adriaan de Groot before him, he studies a skill he knows from the inside.[^s3]

## Template theory

Gobet's best-known contribution to chess psychology is **template theory**, set out with Simon in a 1996 paper in *Cognitive Psychology*.[^s6] The starting point was Chase and Simon's chunking theory, which held that expert memory is limited by a short-term store of about seven chunks. Gobet and Simon showed players ranging from Class A to grandmaster up to five positions, each for five seconds. Recall declined as boards were added, but experts remembered far more pieces than the original theory allowed; longer pauses between boards helped; and one master, with practice, recalled nine boards at better than 70% accuracy, placing up to 160 pieces correctly.[^s6] To explain this, the authors proposed that experts build **templates**: large, frequently met long-term memory structures with slots that can be filled quickly, alongside ordinary short-term chunks.[^s6]

## CHREST and the idea of a chunk

Gobet is the main architect of **CHREST** (Chunk Hierarchy and REtrieval STructures), a computational cognitive architecture that turns template theory into a running model.[^s1] In CHREST a chunk is a node in long-term memory, created either by discrimination (adding a new node) or by familiarisation (adding detail to an existing one), following the earlier EPAM tradition.[^s8] The same machinery has been applied well beyond chess, to concept formation, implicit learning, children's language acquisition and problem gambling.[^s1] Gobet has also argued that "chunk" is used loosely in psychology, and that expert chess memory is the textbook case of automatic, unconscious chunking.[^s8]

## Practice and talent

Gobet has been a prominent voice in the debate over **deliberate practice**. In a 2007 study with Guillermo Campitelli of 104 Argentinian players, from weak amateurs to grandmasters, practice clearly mattered for reaching high skill, but its effect varied enormously: one player needed eight times as much practice as another to reach master level. Skill also correlated with the age at which players started, and chess players were more often mixed-handed than the general population.[^s7] The authors concluded that practice is necessary but not sufficient, and that starting age matters.[^s7]

## For the improving player

Several practical lessons follow from this work. Mastery rests on a large stock of familiar patterns and structures, so studying typical positions and pawn structures pays off; long hours alone do not guarantee the same return for everyone; and starting young helps.[^s6][^s7] His book *The Psychology of Chess* (Routledge, 2018) presents these findings for a general audience, covering intelligence, intuition, practice and the gender gap in ratings.[^s3]

## Connections

Gobet is a central figure in the **cognitive psychology of chess** and the study of **chess expertise**.[^s1][^s3] He was **influenced by** **Herbert Simon**, his collaborator at Carnegie Mellon, and his work extends the recall paradigm of **Adriaan de Groot**.[^s1][^s6] Template theory revised **chunking theory**, and his findings bear on **deliberate practice** and on how players **visualize** positions.[^s6][^s7] As an **International Master** he **played in** the **Chess Olympiad** for Switzerland.[^s4][^s5]

# Cognitive psychology of chess

The cognitive psychology of chess is the research field that asks how people perceive chess positions, remember them, imagine moves and choose between them. Chess suits the laboratory unusually well: the task is well defined, players' strength is measured precisely by ratings, and skill ranges from beginner to world champion. Neil Charness called it a "Drosophila" for psychology, after the fruit fly that served genetics as a model organism, and many general theories of expertise were first tested on chess players.[^s1]

## A short history

The field is older than most of experimental psychology. Alfred Binet's 1894 book on great calculators and chess players included a long study of blindfold chess; questionnaires answered by leading players showed that they did not picture the board with physical detail such as the colour or style of the pieces, but held a more abstract representation.[^s1][^s2] In 1907 Cleveland already stressed that players learn complex units, the idea later called chunks, and doubted that general intelligence predicts chess skill well.[^s1] Djakow, Petrowski and Rudik tested leading masters in 1927 and found no general advantage on non-chess visuo-spatial tasks, an early hint that chess skill is specific to chess.[^s1]

The modern field begins with Adriaan de Groot's 1946 thesis, translated into English in 1965. Using think-aloud protocols with grandmasters and experts, he found that the strongest players did not search deeper or wider than weaker ones; their advantage showed instead in remembering a position seen for only a few seconds, which pointed to knowledge and perception rather than raw calculation.[^s1] He also noticed that players often return to the same lines during their search, a pattern later work confirmed.[^s2] In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon showed that the memory advantage largely disappears for randomly arranged pieces, and built chunking theory on that contrast.[^s1]

## Main lines of research

**Perception.** Eye-tracking studies show how quickly experts read a board. In Reingold and colleagues' 2001 experiments, experts had a much larger visual span than intermediates and novices on real chess configurations but not on random ones. On a small check-detection task they were faster (861 ms against 1,207 ms for novices), made fewer fixations, and on about 16% of trials answered without moving their eyes at all.[^s3] Gobet and Charness summarise this work: experts fixate the relevant squares within the first second.[^s1]

**Memory and imagery.** Recall of briefly shown positions became the standard measure, explained first by chunking theory and later by template theory; the computer model CHREST needs about 300,000 chunks to reach grandmaster level.[^s1] Blindfold play turned out to depend on visuo-spatial working memory and above all on chess knowledge in long-term memory, not on any special imagery talent: masters recall real games and legal positions far better than illegal random ones.[^s2]

**Search and decision making.** Expert search is highly selective. Strong players look at similar depths to weaker ones but use pattern recognition to narrow the candidates. Supporting evidence includes Chabris and Hearst's finding that blunder rates rise only modestly when time is cut, from 5.02 to 6.85 per 1,000 moves between classical and rapid games.[^s1]

**Brain imaging.** A 2025 systematic review of 18 fMRI, fNIRS and EEG studies found that experts show more activity than novices in the fusiform gyrus and posterior middle temporal gyrus, visual and spatial regions, and stronger connectivity in control and decision networks. It also reported lower grey-matter volume in some regions, which the authors suggest may reflect neural efficiency.[^s4]

## What it means for players

The field's most consistent message is that chess skill is knowledge-based and domain-specific. General visual or memory ability transfers little, so a large store of chess patterns does most of the work.[^s1][^s2] Practical consequences follow: serious study builds the patterns that make positions readable at a glance, and few players reach master level with under 1,000 hours of it.[^s1] Calculation still matters, but it works on the few moves that recognition proposes.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

This node is the parent field for several training articles. It has as parts **chess expertise**, the study of individual differences in skill, and **chunking theory**, its best-known theory of memory.[^s1] It was shaped by **Alfred Binet**, **Adriaan de Groot** and **Herbert Simon**.[^s1] It relates to **calculation** and the **Einstellung effect**, which concern how players search and where familiar patterns mislead, and to **blindfold chess**, one of its oldest test cases.[^s2] More broadly it belongs to **cognitive science**, where chess serves as a model task.[^s1]

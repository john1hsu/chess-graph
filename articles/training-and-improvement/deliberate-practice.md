# Deliberate practice

Deliberate practice is the idea that expert skill is built by a particular kind of training: structured activity designed to improve performance, requiring full concentration, ideally with feedback, and not especially enjoyable for its own sake.[^s2] Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer set out the framework in 1993, mainly from a study of violin students at the Music Academy of West Berlin, where the best students had accumulated far more hours of practice alone by age 18 than the least accomplished group.[^s4] Their strong claim was that accumulated practice, not innate ability, explains expert performance; they wrote that they "reject any important role for innate ability", allowing exceptions only for things such as height in some sports.[^s4] The framework became popular well beyond psychology, including the slogan that ten years or 10,000 hours make an expert.[^s2]

## How it is measured in chess

Chess researchers usually turn the idea into a number: hours per week of serious chess study alone, summed over a player's career.[^s2] That covers analysing positions, studying games and books, and working through problems on one's own, as distinct from simply playing. Fernand Gobet and Guillermo Campitelli also counted group practice, which in their study included tournament games and study with other players.[^s1]

Their study of 104 Argentinian players, from weak amateurs to three grandmasters, found that both kinds of practice go with strength: individual practice correlated .42 with national rating and group practice .54, and the two figures did not differ reliably.[^s1] Coaching also helped: a reduced model using group practice and coaching explained about 36% of the variance in rating.[^s1]

## Necessary but not sufficient

The same data show how much players differ. Taking 2200 as master level, the players who reached it had needed on average about 11,000 hours of total practice, but the range ran from about 3,000 to about 23,600 hours, roughly an eightfold spread; for individual study alone the spread was about 22 to 1.[^s1] Some players logged more than 25,000 hours without reaching master strength.[^s1] The authors concluded that practice is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for expertise.[^s1]

David Hambrick and colleagues pooled six chess studies with 1,083 players in all. On average deliberate practice explained about 34% of the reliable variance in rating, leaving about two-thirds unexplained.[^s2] In one sample a player took 26 years to reach master level while another took under two, which led the authors to call a fixed ten-year or 10,000-hour threshold a myth.[^s2] A 2019 replication of the original violin study found that practice alone explained 26% of the variance rather than the original 48%, and that the best violinists had not practised more than the merely good ones.[^s4] The status of the strong version of the theory is therefore contested.[^s2][^s4]

## Starting age

When it starts seems to matter as well as how much. Controlling for total practice, players who began serious study or joined a club earlier were stronger, and almost all titled players in the Argentinian sample had started serious work by age 12.[^s1] About one in four of those who began seriously at 12 or younger reached international level (IM or GM), against about one in 55 of those who started later.[^s1] A longitudinal analysis of the same players found that masters began serious study at about 10 to 12, experts around 14, and that masters' ratings rose faster in the years after they began.[^s3] Hambrick and colleagues read this as a possible critical period, and also point to intelligence as another factor studied alongside practice.[^s2]

## Practical lessons

For an adult club player the research is more encouraging than its debates suggest. Hours of focused study remain the strongest single measured predictor of chess skill in these studies, even if they are not the whole story.[^s2] Since individual and group practice both mattered, a balanced routine of serious solitary work (analysing your own games, studying master games, solving positions) plus competitive play and, where possible, coaching fits the evidence.[^s1] The common mistake is to count hours rather than quality: what defines deliberate practice is effort and feedback, so passive browsing or blitz alone is not what the studies measured.[^s2]

## Connections

Deliberate practice is a topic within chess expertise and the cognitive psychology of chess.[^s2] It was developed by Ericsson with Krampe and Tesch-Römer, and stands in contrast with talent-based explanations of skill, which its critics argue cannot be dismissed.[^s2][^s4] It depends partly on starting age, as the Argentinian studies show.[^s1][^s3] It relates to chunking theory, which explains what long study builds in memory, and to calculation and puzzle training as typical forms of solitary study; the Einstellung effect is a reminder that knowledge built this way also has costs.[^s2]

# Anders Ericsson

K. Anders Ericsson (23 October 1947 – 17 June 2020) was a Swedish-born psychologist who became the best-known researcher on how people acquire expert performance.[^s1] He took his doctorate at the University of Stockholm in 1976, then held a postdoctoral fellowship at Carnegie Mellon University with William Chase and Herbert Simon, the founders of chunking theory.[^s1] After a period on the faculty of the University of Colorado at Boulder he moved in 1992 to Florida State University as Conradi Eminent Scholar and Professor of Psychology; among his colleagues there was the psychologist Neil Charness.[^s2] He wrote some 275 articles, chapters and books, cited more than 80,000 times, and in 2010 was elected a foreign member of the Royal Swedish Academy of Engineering Sciences.[^s1][^s2]

## Memory and thinking aloud

Ericsson's early work was on memory. In one well-known training study a student practised for about 200 hours and became able to repeat around 80 random digits, showing how far practice can stretch an apparently fixed capacity.[^s2] With Simon he wrote *Protocol Analysis: Verbal Reports as Data*, which put think-aloud methods on a firm theoretical basis; a revised edition appeared in 1993.[^s4] Chess was a model case for that method. Ericsson and Simon pointed to Adriaan de Groot's experiments, in which strong players chose the best move in unfamiliar positions while thinking aloud, as proof that superior expert thinking can be reproduced in the laboratory.[^s3] They noted that performance on this choose-a-move task tracks individual differences in tournament strength, and that asking players to think aloud during a real game would be unworkable because the opponent would gain an advantage.[^s3]

## The expert-performance approach and deliberate practice

From these studies Ericsson built what he called the expert-performance approach: find representative tasks that capture real skill, measure performance on them, and then ask what kind of training produced the difference.[^s3] His answer was deliberate practice, set out with Krampe and Tesch-Römer in a 1993 paper that has been cited more than 11,000 times and that Malcolm Gladwell later popularised as the "10,000-hour rule".[^s1] In a 2007 summary for a general audience, Ericsson and co-authors argued that experts are made rather than born, that effective practice targets tasks just beyond current ability, usually with a coach, and that reaching expert level typically takes about ten years of intense training.[^s6] They used chess examples freely: the Polgár sisters, trained from early childhood, and the observation that in think-aloud studies strong players go back over earlier analysis to find where a line went wrong.[^s6] They also stressed that truly concentrated practice is limited to a few hours a day.[^s6] With Robert Pool he later wrote the popular book *Peak* (2016), and he was lead editor of the *Cambridge Handbook of Expertise and Expert Performance* (2006; second edition 2018).[^s1][^s2]

## Long-term working memory

In 1995 Ericsson and Walter Kintsch proposed long-term working memory, the idea that experts encode information rapidly into long-term memory and keep only retrieval cues active.[^s1] For chess they proposed a retrieval structure based on the 64 squares, which would let a master store individual piece locations, update a position mentally during blindfold play, and plan move sequences.[^s5] Fernand Gobet argued that the theory was too vague to test and fitted chess data worse than chunking and template theory, citing masters' modest recall of random positions, their fast encoding of game positions shown for a few seconds, and the drop in performance when several boards must be remembered.[^s5]

## Legacy for chess players

For club players, Ericsson's influence is visible in the ordinary vocabulary of improvement: focused solitary study, feedback from a coach, problems pitched just above one's level, and suspicion of hours spent only playing casual games.[^s6] His strongest claim, that accumulated practice rather than talent explains expert performance, remains disputed, but even his critics took his research methods as their starting point.[^s1][^s5] A practical reading is to judge study by its difficulty and feedback rather than by hours logged, and to analyse one's own thinking after a game the way think-aloud studies do.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Ericsson sits within the cognitive psychology of chess and the wider study of chess expertise.[^s3] He was trained by and influenced by Herbert Simon, and his methods descend from de Groot's choose-a-move experiments.[^s1][^s3] He developed the deliberate-practice framework and long-term working memory, the latter standing in contrast with Gobet's template theory and the chunking theory it extends.[^s1][^s5] The debate over deliberate practice versus talent is his most lasting legacy in chess research.[^s1]

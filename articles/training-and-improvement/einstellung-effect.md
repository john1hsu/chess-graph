# Einstellung effect

The Einstellung (German for "set" or "attitude") effect is what happens when the first good idea a position suggests blocks the search for a better one. A familiar pattern comes to mind, it works, and the player stops looking, or keeps "looking" without really leaving the first idea behind.[^s1] The term comes from Abraham Luchins's 1942 experiments with water-jug problems: participants who had practised one fixed method went on to judge an "extinction" problem, which that method could not solve, to be insoluble.[^s3] It matters in chess because it strikes experts in their own field, not only beginners facing something new.[^s1]

## The chess experiments

Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet built the classic chess demonstration. Their "two-solution" problem allowed a well-known five-move smothered mate (queen check on e6, knight checks on f7 and h6, a queen sacrifice on g8 and a final knight mate on f7) but also a less familiar three-move mate with the queen on h6 and h7 or g7.[^s1] A "one-solution" version moved a black bishop from c6 to h5, where it covers f7 and kills the smothered mate, leaving only the short route.[^s1]

The results were striking. On the two-solution problem, International Masters found the shorter mate half the time, Masters 18% of the time and Candidate Masters never; on the one-solution problem every one of these stronger players found it.[^s2] Measured against weaker players who saw only the one-solution problem, the familiar idea cut experts' performance to the level of players about three standard deviations weaker: International Masters behaved like Class A players, Masters like Class B and Candidate Masters like Class C. Grandmasters were not affected by these particular problems.[^s1][^s2] Popular accounts by the authors add that grandmasters, too, failed once a problem was made tricky enough.[^s4]

## How it works: attention, not just memory

Eye tracking explained the mechanism. Ten Candidate Masters (mean Elo about 2090) were split into two groups. Those shown the two-solution problem reported the smothered mate after about 37 seconds and said they were still searching for something shorter, yet their gaze stayed on the squares of the familiar solution (f7, g5, g8) throughout. Those shown the one-solution problem drifted steadily towards the squares that matter for the short mate (b2, h6, h7, g7).[^s1] The authors conclude that the first idea biases which features of the board receive attention, so information that would point elsewhere is barely examined; they link this to confirmation bias more generally.[^s1][^s2]

Heather Sheridan and Eyal Reingold replicated this with 17 experts (mean Canadian rating 2223) and 17 novices, and found a boundary condition. When the familiar move was merely second-best, eight experts and eight novices still chose it, and those players never disengaged from the region of the board linked to it. When the familiar move was an outright blunder, all experts and most novices looked away and found the better move.[^s3] A refutation the player can see seems to free the search; a familiar move that is good enough does not.[^s3]

## Practical lessons

For club players the warning is uncomfortable: the more patterns you know, the more often a pattern will arrive first. The research suggests that the greatest risk comes when a familiar idea clearly works, because nothing then forces a re-examination.[^s3] The authors advise extra suspicion at exactly the moment you feel you already have the answer.[^s4] In practice that means, after finding a winning idea, deliberately checking other forcing moves and other areas of the board before committing; the old advice "when you see a good move, look for a better one" is a direct remedy. It also helps to remember that "still looking" is not the same as looking afresh: the experts in the eye-tracking study believed they were searching while their eyes stayed put.[^s1]

## Connections

The Einstellung effect belongs to the cognitive psychology of chess and the study of chess expertise.[^s1] It goes back to Abraham Luchins's water-jug studies.[^s3] It is closely tied to chunking theory: the same recognised patterns that make experts fast are what trigger the familiar first idea, which is why it is described as a cost of expertise.[^s1][^s2] Merim Bilalić and colleagues developed its chess version.[^s1] It relates to template theory, a successor of chunking theory, to calculation as the skill it distorts, and to the smothered mate, its best-known test pattern.[^s1]

# Abraham Luchins

Abraham S. Luchins (8 March 1914 – 27 December 2005) was an American psychologist in the Gestalt tradition. He was never a chess researcher, but his experiments on how a practised method blinds people to a simpler one gave chess psychology one of its most useful ideas: the Einstellung, or "set", effect, which explains why a strong player who has found a good move often stops seeing a better one.[^s1][^s5]

## Career

Born in Brooklyn, Luchins studied at Brooklyn College, took a master's degree at Columbia University and a doctorate at New York University.[^s1] The decisive influence on his work was Max Wertheimer, a founder of Gestalt psychology, at the New School for Social Research; the Society for Gestalt Theory and its Applications lists him as Wertheimer's research assistant from 1936 to 1943.[^s1] In his own recollection, Wertheimer supervised the Einstellung research, kept pushing him to redesign experiments, and insisted that the data rather than the hypothesis must decide.[^s2][^s3] He taught at Yeshiva University, McGill University and the universities of Oregon and Miami, and was a professor of psychology at the State University of New York at Albany.[^s1] He wrote widely on thinking, learning, perception and social psychology, often with Edith H. Luchins, and was an honorary member of the Gestalt society and a fellow of several divisions of the American Psychological Association.[^s1]

## The water-jar experiments

The Einstellung work began at the New School as a study of "learning by repetition" and became his NYU dissertation; Wertheimer then held back publication until it was rewritten as a monograph.[^s2][^s3] It appeared in 1942 as "Mechanization in problem solving: The effect of Einstellung" in *Psychological Monographs*.[^s5]

The design is simple. Subjects measure out a quantity of water with three jars. A run of practice problems can all be solved by one roundabout formula, written B − A − 2C. Then come "critical" problems that also yield to a much shorter method such as A + C, and an "extinction" problem that only the short method solves.[^s4] Most subjects kept using the long formula on the critical problems, and many declared the extinction problem insoluble, although a control group that saw it fresh solved it easily.[^s5] In one of Luchins's college groups, about 77% used the roundabout method on the first two critical problems and about 62% on the last two.[^s4] Looking back, the Luchinses noted that Wertheimer discussed these experiments in his New School seminars, and that the extinction problem often went unsolved simply because the much-repeated method could not reach it.[^s3]

## Why chess players should know the name

When Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet built a chess test of the effect, they copied Luchins's structure directly. Their "two-solution" position offered a familiar smothered mate and a shorter, less familiar mate, like Luchins's critical problems; their "one-solution" version disabled the smothered mate, like his extinction problem. Expert players fixed on the familiar idea in the first position but solved the second without trouble.[^s5] The authors treat the effect as a general source of cognitive bias rather than a quirk of puzzles, which is why it matters for training.[^s5]

For a club player the lesson is Luchins's, not chess-specific: a method that has worked several times in a row is precisely the one to distrust when the position changes. A practical habit is to re-check forcing moves after you have found a winning idea, rather than repeating the routine that found it.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Luchins belongs to the cognitive psychology of chess as the originator of the experimental paradigm behind the Einstellung effect.[^s5] He was influenced by Max Wertheimer, who supervised the work.[^s2][^s3] Merim Bilalić and colleagues adapted his design to chess expertise, and the effect bears directly on calculation, where an early familiar line can crowd out the search for better candidates.[^s5]

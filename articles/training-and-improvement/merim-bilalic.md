# Merim Bilalić

Merim Bilalić is a cognitive psychologist best known among chess players for showing, with eye-tracking, why strong players settle for a good move when a better one is on the board. He took his DPhil in experimental psychology at the University of Oxford and held research and teaching posts at Brunel University, the Humboldt University of Berlin, the University of Tübingen and the University of Klagenfurt before moving to Northumbria University in Newcastle, where, as of November 2026, he is Professor of Cognitive Psychology.[^s1][^s2] His doctoral work on problem-solving biases in experts won the British Psychological Society's 2008 award for an outstanding doctoral research contribution.[^s1]

## Research focus

Bilalić studies outstanding performance: how domain knowledge changes perception, attention and memory, what the brain does differently in experts, and which individual factors (intelligence, personality, motivation) shape who becomes one.[^s1] Chess is a recurring test domain in this work, alongside skills such as radiology and sport.[^s2][^s7] His research-portal profile lists pattern recognition, deliberate practice and skill acquisition among his topics, and a 2026 *British Journal of Psychology* paper on how computers have changed elite human chess performance.[^s2]

## The Einstellung effect in chess

His central chess contribution, with Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet, revived Abraham Luchins's 1942 idea of *Einstellung*, or mental set, in which a familiar solution blocks a better one.[^s3][^s4] Players were shown a position with two routes to mate: a well-known five-move smothered mate and a shorter, less familiar three-move mate. When only the familiar idea was available they solved it easily; with both present, most missed the shorter line. Half of the International Masters found it, but only 18% of masters and none of the candidate masters did.[^s4] When the smothered mate was made impossible, every master-level group found the short mate. The authors estimated that the familiar idea dragged experts down to roughly the level of players three standard deviations weaker.[^s4]

The eye-tracking study supplied the mechanism. Candidate masters said they were looking for something better, yet their gaze kept returning to squares that belonged to the smothered mate and rarely visited the squares needed for the faster win.[^s3][^s4] Bilalić and colleagues concluded that the first idea to come to mind quietly directs attention towards information that fits it, without the player noticing, and linked the same mechanism to confirmation bias in science and everyday reasoning.[^s3][^s4]

## Participation and the gender gap

In a 2009 paper in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, Bilalić, Smallbone, McLeod and Gobet compared the 100 best male and the 100 best female players in Germany. Men did score higher at the top, but the authors estimated that about 96% of the gap would be expected simply from the far larger number of men who play.[^s5] They argued that participation rates leave little room for biological or cultural explanations of the difference at the top and may also explain why fewer women reach the top in science.[^s5]

## The neuroscience of expertise

Bilalić's book *The Neuroscience of Expertise* (Cambridge University Press, 2017) brings together research on perception, attention and memory in experts and proposes a common framework across fields such as chess, radiology and tennis.[^s7] In a 2018 review he argued against the view that experts simply use less of the brain. In chess object- and pattern-recognition tasks, he reported, experts recruit areas in both hemispheres, which he calls the "double take of expertise", letting them recognise a piece, retrieve its function and relate it to other pieces at the same time.[^s6]

## For the improving player

The Einstellung findings give practical advice. A familiar pattern found early is not proof that it is best: after spotting a good move, deliberately look for a better one and check the squares your first idea ignores.[^s3][^s4] Bilalić's work also suggests that expertise reduces the effect but does not remove it, so even strong players are not immune.[^s4]

## Connections

Bilalić is a figure in the **cognitive psychology of chess** and the study of **chess expertise**.[^s1][^s4] He was **influenced by** **Abraham Luchins**, whose water-jar experiments defined the **Einstellung effect** he extended to chess, and worked closely with **Fernand Gobet**, co-author of his main chess papers.[^s3][^s4][^s5] His experiments used the **smothered mate** as the familiar idea, and his participation study bears on debates about **talent** and on **women in chess**.[^s4][^s5]

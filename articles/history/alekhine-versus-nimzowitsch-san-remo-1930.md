# Alekhine versus Nimzowitsch, San Remo 1930

**Alekhine versus Nimzowitsch** is the third-round game of the San Remo tournament of 1930, played on 18 January, in which the reigning world champion Alexander Alekhine beat Aron Nimzowitsch in 30 moves without any direct attack on the black king.[^s1][^s5] ChessBase calls it perhaps the most famous game of Alekhine's San Remo triumph, and Alekhine put it among the seven games from the event that he chose for his own best-games collection.[^s3] Its final position, with White's queen and both rooks lined up on the c-file, later gave its name to the formation known as Alekhine's gun, and the game has been argued over ever since as an example of zugzwang.[^s1][^s2]

## The setting

San Remo was a sixteen-player round robin staged at the casino, organised by Stefano Rosselli del Turco of *L'Italia Scacchistica*; the draw was made on 15 January 1930 and play began the next day.[^s1][^s6] The field included Nimzowitsch, Rubinstein, Bogoljubow, Tartakower, Spielmann, Maróczy, Vidmar and Yates, though Capablanca, Euwe and Lasker were absent.[^s3][^s6] Alekhine scored 14 from 15 (13 wins, two draws) and finished three and a half points clear; Nimzowitsch was second with 10½, so this game decided the head-to-head between the top two.[^s3][^s4]

## How the game unfolded

Nimzowitsch chose the Winawer French (ECO C17), and after 6.Nb5 Bxd2+ White kept a space-gaining pawn chain on d4–e5–f4.[^s1][^s5] Alekhine then advanced on the queenside with 11.a4 and 12.b4, opening the c-file after 12...cxb4 13.cxb4.[^s5] In the notes reproduced in Enrico Cecchelli's Italian tournament book, Alekhine calls 14...f5, which fixed the centre for good, Black's decisive strategic mistake, and suggests 14...a5 as a more practical try; the editor adds analysis arguing that ...a5 gives real counterplay.[^s1]

After 14.Nd6 f5 15.a5! White exchanged off Black's light-squared bishop with 16.Nxb7 and drove the a-pawn to a6, so that the squares c6 and c7 became permanent targets, a sequence Alekhine marked as the start of the attack.[^s1] From move 20 onwards both sides doubled on the c-file, but White's queen arrived behind the rooks with 26.Qc1, while Black had to hold c7 and c6 with pieces that had no other work.[^s1][^s5] The book's commentary describes the result as a model of blockade and of play on the open file and seventh rank, and quotes Tartakower praising a blockade worthy of Nimzowitsch's own principles; part of the game's fame is that the theorist of the blockade lost to his own weapons.[^s1]

## The final position and the ending

The key move in Alekhine's notes is 27.Ba4!: the bishop pins the c6-knight against the black queen, and the king walk to d8 only covers the critical squares without freeing anything.[^s1] After 30.h4 (the `fen` above) Black has only a few harmless pawn moves left; Alekhine's note says Black will soon have to play ...Qe8, after which the push b4–b5 wins at once.[^s1] Sources disagree on where the score stops. The Italian book and chessgames.com give the game as 30 moves with resignation after 30.h4, but the book's note indicates that the German text adds the two half-moves 30...Qe8 31.b5, and that is the version some databases, including Lichess, carry.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## Was it really zugzwang?

Gerald Abrahams, in *Technique in Chess* (1961), set the game beside Nimzowitsch's own famous win over Sämisch as a short game in which Alekhine reduced Nimzowitsch to zugzwang.[^s2] Wolfgang Heidenfeld, writing in the *British Chess Magazine* in January 1972, rejected it as an example, notwithstanding Alekhine's own claim in his second best-games volume.[^s2] The disagreement is partly about definitions: Black was not obliged to move into a lost position at once, since pawn moves remained, but every piece move lost material, which is why ChessBase still describes the finish as a middlegame zugzwang.[^s1][^s3]

For a club player the lessons are concrete: an early pawn advance can fix weak squares for good, a blocked centre lets the side with more space shift everything to one wing, and tripled heavy pieces work best when the defender's pieces are tied to fixed targets.[^s1]

## Connections

The game is **played by** **Alexander Alekhine** and **Aron Nimzowitsch** and was **played in** **San Remo 1930**.[^s3][^s4] Its opening is a Winawer **French Defence**, and it **exemplifies** both **Alekhine's gun** and the **blockade**, the strategic idea Nimzowitsch himself had codified.[^s1][^s5] It is a stock example in discussions of **zugzwang**, of pressure on an **open file**, of **weak squares** on c6 and c7, and of how a **space advantage** is converted.[^s1][^s2]

# Lewis chessmen

The Lewis chessmen are the best-known surviving medieval chess pieces: a hoard of carved gaming pieces found on the Isle of Lewis, in Scotland's Outer Hebrides, in 1831.[^s1][^s7] National Museums Scotland counts 93 gaming pieces in the hoard, including pieces from at least four chess sets as well as pieces for other games.[^s1] The local Historic Environment Record lists 78 walrus-ivory chessmen, 14 tablemen and a buckle, and notes that no set is complete.[^s6] For a chess player they are a rare physical window onto the game as it was played in northern Europe some three centuries before the modern queen and bishop existed.[^s7]

## Discovery

The pieces turned up in 1831 near Uig on the west coast of Lewis, but where exactly is uncertain: published accounts conflict, and NMS notes that none of their authors seems to have visited the island.[^s1] The record for the traditional find spot near Ardroil describes the sea undermining a sandbank in spring 1831 and exposing a small stone structure "like an oven" holding the pieces.[^s6] Some accounts name a local man, Malcolm MacLeod, as the finder.[^s1][^s5] A 2009 study led by David Caldwell of National Museums Scotland argued that the accepted story rests on a later storyteller's account, and that the hoard more likely came from Mèalasta, a few miles south of Uig Strand.[^s5]

The hoard was shown to the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland in Edinburgh in 1831, and within months it had been broken up and sold.[^s1] The British Museum bought most of it in 1831–32; the eleven pieces now in Edinburgh passed through private collections before National Museums Scotland acquired them in 1888.[^s1] As of the museum's 2024 redisplay, it held 11 pieces and the British Museum 82, six of which were on loan to Museum nan Eilean on Lewis.[^s2]

## Date, material and origin

Most pieces are carved from walrus ivory, probably from Greenland and traded through Norway; a few are sperm-whale teeth, and a single tusk could yield up to four pieces.[^s1] Traces of mercury suggest some were once stained red with cinnabar, so the two sides may have been told apart by colour, as on a modern board.[^s1] Dates vary by source: the British Museum gives 1150–1175, NMS the late 12th or early 13th century, and Britannica the 11th or 12th century.[^s3][^s1][^s7] NMS favours Trondheim in Norway as the likely workshop because of stylistic links such as the carved thrones.[^s1] When they were made, Lewis belonged to the Norse-Gaelic Kingdom of the Isles, at times under Norwegian control.[^s2] An older view, recorded in the find-spot record, argued for Scottish-Norman workmanship instead.[^s6]

## The pieces

The set's cast of figures is recognisably that of modern chess. Kings and queens sit on thrones carved with vine scrolls and interlace, and one queen rests her chin in her hand; bishops hold crosiers; knights ride small horses with spear and shield; and the rooks, called warders, stand as foot soldiers with sword and shield.[^s1][^s2] Some warders bite the tops of their shields, and museums describe them as berserkers, the frenzied warriors of Norse tradition.[^s1][^s3] If the hoard held four full sets, NMS calculates it is missing one knight, four warders and 44 pawns.[^s1]

These pieces belong to the medieval game, not today's. Before the reforms of the late 15th century, the piece that became the queen moved only one square diagonally, and the forerunner of the bishop only jumped two squares diagonally.[^s7] The queen and bishop figures on Lewis therefore show the European, Christian renaming of the old Persian–Arabic counsellor and elephant, not the powerful long-range pieces of modern chess.[^s7]

## Interpretation and pitfalls

Nobody knows who buried the hoard or why.[^s1][^s2] The British Museum repeats the idea that it belonged to a merchant travelling from Norway to Ireland.[^s3] The 2009 study in *Medieval Archaeology* argued instead that at least four sets from one workshop may have been made for use on Lewis, perhaps for a local leader such as a bishop or chief, and saw the hoard's make-up as more fluid than a merchant's stock.[^s4][^s5] Facial analysis by Caroline Wilkinson grouped the figures into sets that may reflect up to five carvers.[^s5]

A common mistake is to treat every piece as a chessman. Some may have served in *hnefatafl*, a Norse game in which a king and his guards try to reach a corner, and the hoard also contains pieces for tables, a backgammon-like game.[^s1][^s5] Treat precise counts with care too: totals differ slightly between catalogues depending on whether tablemen and the buckle are included.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

The Lewis chessmen sit within the **history of chess** as an **instance of** a **chess set** from the period when European chess still used **shatranj**-style moves for the counsellor and elephant.[^s7] Their link to **hnefatafl** reflects the overlap of chess with older Norse board games.[^s1][^s5] Their carved human figures, with each piece shown as a king, queen, bishop, knight or foot soldier, invite comparison with later standard designs such as the **Staunton chess set**.[^s1][^s2]

# World Chess Championship 1886

The World Chess Championship 1886 was the match between Wilhelm Steinitz and Johannes Zukertort, played from 11 January to 29 March 1886 in three American cities: New York, Saint Louis and New Orleans. Steinitz won with ten wins, five losses and five draws, a score of 12½–7½.[^s1] No international body regulated chess then, since FIDE was only founded in 1924, but the FIDE Historical Committee regards the match as, without dispute, the first official world championship.[^s2]

## Why it counts as the first

Earlier "champions" such as Staunton or Morphy were recognised informally. What made 1886 different was the wording. Edward Winter traces how Steinitz's *International Chess Magazine* first wrote only of "the championship", then by December 1885 of the championship of the world, and how the players' agreement, published in the *Chess Monthly* in January 1886, opened by stating that the match was "for the Championship of the World".[^s3] By then the two men were the obvious pair: Steinitz had been seen as the strongest player since beating Anderssen in 1866, while Zukertort had won London 1883 ahead of him.[^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame describes Zukertort as the world's second-best player from about 1871 to 1886, and London 1883, where he finished three points clear of Steinitz, as a high point of his career.[^s4]

## Format and setting

The winner was the first to ten wins, draws not counting; at nine wins each the match would have ended without a title decision.[^s1] The time control was 30 moves in two hours, then 15 moves an hour.[^s1][^s2] The stakes were $2,000 a side, a very large sum at the time, and the match was held in the United States because Steinitz refused to play in London, which he regarded as hostile.[^s5] The Manhattan Chess Club organised the opening leg at Cartier's Academy Hall; the Saint Louis games were played at the Harmonie Club and the final leg at the New Orleans Chess, Checkers and Whist Club.[^s1]

## Course of the match

Steinitz won the first game with Black, but Zukertort then won four in a row, so the New York leg ended with him leading 4–1 in wins.[^s2][^s5] In Saint Louis Steinitz recovered, and his win with Black in game 9 levelled the score at four wins each; the FIDE historian sees this game as the moment Steinitz took control of the match.[^s2][^s5] In New Orleans he outscored Zukertort 6–1 in decisive games, finishing with three straight wins in games 18–20.[^s1][^s5]

From a Slav-type set-up with Black, Steinitz gained space with ...e5–e4, sent his knight to h4 and g2, and sacrificed it on e3 to tear open the white king; his rooks then swung across the third rank. ## Interpretation and aftermath

The traditional reading is that Steinitz's positional method beat Zukertort's romantic attacking chess, and the FIDE historian notes the claim that modern chess was born with game 9.[^s2] Willy Hendriks has challenged this, arguing that Zukertort already played in a modern way and that Steinitz was simply stronger and steadier; Zukertort's poor health and strained nerves are also cited, though their effect is unclear.[^s5] Zukertort never got a rematch: his results declined, and he died in London in June 1888.[^s5][^s2]

## Connections

The match is **part of** the **World Chess Championship** lineage, of which it is the first official instance.[^s2] It was **played by** **Wilhelm Steinitz**, who **won** it, and **Johannes Zukertort**, and it was **organised by** the **Manhattan Chess Club** in its New York leg.[^s1] It is often read as the end of the **Romantic era**.[^s2] Its qualifying context was the **London 1883** tournament, where Zukertort finished ahead of Steinitz and so earned his claim to a title match.[^s2][^s4] In game 1 Steinitz answered the Queen's Gambit with an early form of the **Slav Defence** (ECO D10), combined with the space-gaining ...e5–e4.[^s6]

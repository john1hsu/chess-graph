# London 1883

London 1883 was an international tournament played in London between 26 April and 23 June 1883. Fourteen of the leading masters of the day met twice each, and Johannes Zukertort won with 22 points from 26 games, three points clear of Wilhelm Steinitz.[^s1] The event is remembered for three things: the most convincing tournament victory of Zukertort's career, the first use of a double-faced chess clock, and the way its result made a Steinitz–Zukertort match all but inevitable.[^s1][^s2]

## Organisation and format

The official tournament book, edited by James Innes Minchin and published in London by Jas Wade in 1883, records Marmaduke Wyvill among the organisers and is dedicated to Prince Leopold, Duke of Albany, as patron.[^s2] The games were annotated for the book by masters including Zukertort, Steinitz, Mason and Bird.[^s2]

The format was a double round-robin, but draws did not simply count as half-points: a drawn game was replayed, and only a third drawn result between the same players in a cycle stood as a draw.[^s1][^s5] The time limit was fifteen moves per hour.[^s2][^s5] Play ran in two sessions on fixed days, from noon to five and again from seven to eleven in the evening.[^s5]

## The clock

Time had previously been measured with sand-glasses or with a separate clock for each player. At London 1883 the players used a double-faced mechanical clock made by Thomas Bright Wilson of Manchester, with a tumbling mechanism linking the two movements. It is generally credited as the first tournament use of this kind of linked clock, the ancestor of the modern chess clock.[^s1][^s2]

## Participants and result

The field was Zukertort, Steinitz, Joseph Henry Blackburne, Mikhail Chigorin, George Henry Mackenzie, Berthold Englisch, James Mason, Samuel Rosenthal, Szymon Winawer, Henry Bird, Josef Noa, Alexander Sellman, James Mortimer and Arthur Skipworth.[^s1] The leading final scores were:[^s1]

| Place | Player | Score[^s1] |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Johannes Zukertort | 22/26 |
| 2 | Wilhelm Steinitz | 19 |
| 3 | Joseph Henry Blackburne | 16½ |
| 4 | Mikhail Chigorin | 16 |
| 5–7 | Mackenzie, Englisch, Mason | 15½ |

Zukertort had first place secured with three rounds to spare and then lost his last three games.[^s1][^s2] Writers have offered different explanations: the Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 blamed medication he took for his nerves, while Willy Hendriks argues the late losses owed more to tactical misjudgments and bad luck than to a collapse.[^s4][^s5] First prize was £300.[^s4] Chigorin's narrow miss of third place mattered financially, since the gap between third and fourth prize was thirty pounds.[^s5]

## Zukertort–Blackburne

The tournament's most celebrated game is Zukertort's win over Blackburne on 5 May 1883, often called his "immortal game".[^s1][^s3] From a quiet queen's-flank opening White built a kingside pawn storm, and the finish turned on the offer of the queen with 28.Qb4, a square Black's queen on e7 could take, followed by the rook sacrifice 29.Rf8+. Each sacrifice deflected a defender from the long diagonal so that White's dark-squared bishop and queen could strike at the king.[^s1][^s3] Edward Winter notes that the tournament book carries annotations by both Zukertort and Minchin.[^s3]

```pgn
15. Bxc4 d5 16. Bd3 Rfc8 17. Rae1 Rc7 18. e4 Rac8 19. e5 Ne8 20. f4 g6 21. Re3 f5
22. exf6 Nxf6 23. f5 Ne4 24. Bxe4 dxe4 25. fxg6 Rc2 26. gxh7+ Kh8 27. d5+ e5
28. Qb4 R8c5 29. Rf8+ Kxh7 30. Qxe4+ Kg7 31. Bxe5+ Kxf8 32. Bg7+ Kg8 33. Qxe7 1-0
```

For a club player the lesson is how the bishop on b2, quiet for most of the game, decided it once the e5-pawn and the f-file were opened: the sacrifices were justified by the long diagonal, not by the rook or queen themselves.[^s3]

## Connections

London 1883 was a successor to the first international tournament, `london-1851`, and like it was a `chess-tournament` of the late `romantic-era`, though in the `round-robin` rather than knockout form.[^s1] Its winner was `johannes-zukertort` and its runner-up `wilhelm-steinitz`; the margin between them and the rest of the field led directly to the `world-chess-championship-1886`, the first official title match, which Steinitz won.[^s1] Its Wilson clock is an early landmark in the history of the `chess-clock`.[^s2] The decisive game against `joseph-henry-blackburne` is the event's best-known example.[^s3]

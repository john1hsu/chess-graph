# Adams versus Torre, New Orleans 1920

**Adams versus Torre** is a 23-move game in which Edwin Ziegler Adams, a New Orleans amateur, is credited with beating the teenage Mexican talent Carlos Torre by offering his queen again and again to a black queen that could not accept without dropping the back rank.[^s1][^s4] It is dated New Orleans 1920 and appears in countless tactics books as the model back-rank combination.[^s1] It is also one of the most argued-over "games" in chess history, because the evidence that it was ever played as a real game is thin.[^s1]

## Players and record

Torre lived in New Orleans for several years as a youngster before returning to Mérida; Edward Winter's sources put him at about 16 in November 1921.[^s2] He went on to win the Western Chess Association tournament at Detroit in 1924 by two and a half points and to share second place with Maróczy behind Marshall at Chicago 1926.[^s2] Adams was a local player who presented himself as Torre's sponsor; a 1927 profile of him in the *American Chess Bulletin* carried that title.[^s1] Database records give him as born and died in New Orleans (1885–1944).[^s3]

No score of the game is known from 1920. It first appeared in the *American Chess Bulletin* of November 1925, under the heading "Chess in New Orleans", with notes supplied by Torre himself, the loser.[^s1] A French magazine printed it the same year without a source, and Chernev and Reinfeld later made it famous; Reinfeld even called it the finest win ever scored by an amateur against a master, a label Winter thinks is hard to justify for Torre in 1920.[^s1]

This is the score as Winter reproduces it from the 1925 publication; databases class the opening as a Philidor Defence (C41).[^s1][^s3]

## How the combination works

After 17...Bxf6 White's rooks are doubled on the e-file against Black's rook on e8, which is held only by the rook on c8 and the queen on d7, and Black's king has no luft.[^s1] In his notes Torre said Black should have made an escape square for the king a move earlier.[^s1] From here every white queen move is a deflection offer: 18.Qg4 cannot be taken, because 18...Qxg4 19.Rxe8+ Rxe8 20.Rxe8 is mate, and 19.Qc4 and 20.Qc7 repeat the trick against the queen and then the c8-rook, since either capture leaves e8 short of a defender.[^s1] With 21.a4 White deflects the queen along its diagonal, and after 22.Re4 Qb5 the final 23.Qxb7 attacks the black queen, which cannot leave the a4–e8 diagonal without exposing e8, and Black resigned.[^s1]

For the club player the lesson is about overloading: a piece that guards a mating square cannot also take on another square, and a rook pair on an open file makes every back-rank defender a target.[^s1][^s4] Torre's own remark that the later moves were "a study" fits how the finish is usually taught.[^s1]

## Was it really played?

Doubts were first aired in 1983 in Chess Notes, citing the collector Dale Brandreth and a theory of David Hooper that Torre credited Adams with a game that was partly analysis.[^s1] Brandreth later called it spurious, at best post-mortem analysis: the first printing came five years late and from the loser; Adams's two-page letter about Torre to Hermann Helms of 22 November 1920 enclosed what Adams called the only Torre game he could supply, and Brandreth argued that a win of this kind would surely have been mentioned; and Torre's friends Alejandro Báez and Pedro Busto Undiano told the biographer Gabriel Velasco that the game grew out of analysis of a skittles game.[^s1][^s2] Against that, Adams thanked *Chess Review* for praising the game in 1943, a year before his death, though Brandreth gave this little weight.[^s1] Winter sets out the evidence without a final verdict, and modern commentators still treat it as possibly brilliant analysis rather than a played game, which is why its status here is contested.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

The game is the classic **example of** the **back-rank mate** and of **overloading**, and its queen offers are textbook **deflection** inside a **combination**.[^s1][^s4] It was **played by** (or at least credited to) **Edwin Z. Adams** and **Carlos Torre**, and its opening is a **Philidor Defence**.[^s1][^s3]

# Steinitz versus von Bardeleben, Hastings 1895

**Steinitz versus von Bardeleben** is the game Wilhelm Steinitz won with White against Curt von Bardeleben in the tenth round of the Hastings tournament on 17 August 1895.[^s1][^s2] It is remembered for two things: a rook that sat undefended on Black's second rank, could not be taken, and kept checking along it; and the way it ended, with von Bardeleben leaving the hall rather than resigning.[^s1][^s3] Steinitz regarded it as one of the best games of his career, and Capablanca later described it as one of his last masterpieces.[^s1][^s6]

## Record and prize

The tournament book edited by Horace F. Cheshire gives the score as an Italian Game (Giuoco Piano, ECO C54) ending with 25.Rxh7+ and a win for White.[^s2][^s1] The tournament's brilliancy sub-committee, signed by John G. Colborne and C. D. Locock, awarded it the first brilliancy prize ahead of Tarrasch–Walbrodt, and wrote that the whole of the play deserved to be called artistic and beautiful as well as brilliant.[^s5][^s3] According to the prize list reproduced from the book, the first brilliancy prize was worth five pounds.[^s5][^s3]

## How the game went

The opening was a quiet Giuoco Piano with an early 4.c3 and 5.d4. After a run of exchanges on d5 and e7, White had an isolated d-pawn, and Black had played ...f6 to blunt the e-file, leaving his king in the centre.[^s4][^s2] Kavalek's note is that Steinitz then gave up that pawn with **17.d5** on a square Black seemed to control, to open lines while the black king was still uncastled.[^s3][^s4] After 17...cxd5 18.Nd4 Kf7 19.Ne6 the knight jumped forward, and 21.Ng5+ drove the king back to e8.[^s4]

The key position arises after **22.Rxe7+** (see the `fen`). Black cannot take: 22...Qxe7 loses to 23.Rxc8+ Rxc8 24.Qxc8+, and 22...Kxe7 23.Re1+ Kd6 24.Qb4+ Rc5 25.Re6+ also wins for White.[^s4] So von Bardeleben played 22...Kf8, which left all four white pieces under attack, among them the queen on g4 and the rook on c1.[^s4] White's reply was to keep checking with the rook, 23.Rf7+ Kg8 24.Rg7+ Kh8 25.Rxh7+, since any slower move such as 23.Qxd7 would allow 23...Rxc1+.[^s4][^s3]

## The finish that was never played

The record stops at 25.Rxh7+ because von Bardeleben left the room and let his time run out.[^s1][^s3] After he had gone, Steinitz showed the onlookers the forced win: 25...Kg8 26.Rg7+ Kh8 27.Qh4+ Kxg7 28.Qh7+ Kf8 29.Qh8+ Ke7 30.Qg7+ Ke8 31.Qg8+ Ke7 32.Qf7+ Kd8 33.Qf8+ Qe8 34.Nf7+ Kd7 35.Qd6#.[^s4][^s1] The steward R. C. Griffith recalled decades later that Steinitz sat for about 50 minutes before claiming the game.[^s1]

Why Black walked out is less clear than the legend. Edward Winter's survey of the contemporary record finds that W. H. K. Pollock, writing in *La Stratégie* in October 1895, linked it to the applause that greeted winners, and that the committee then asked visitors not to applaud, as the tournament book notes; other reports of the time do not mention any incident.[^s1] Winter treats several later retellings as unsupported, including a vivid scene of Black pacing in anger and a claim that he sent his resignation by special delivery.[^s1] Kavalek likewise asks how far the newspaper stories can be trusted.[^s3]

## Lessons for the club player

The game is a clean model of attacking an uncastled king. White opened the position with a pawn sacrifice rather than waiting, used the knight's jump to e6 and g5 to pin the king in place, and exploited a defender who was tied to more than one job.[^s3][^s4] The rook on the seventh shows that an attacked piece need not be saved if every move it makes is a check.[^s4] Kavalek adds that the timing mattered: one move later Black might have blockaded the d-pawn and stood well, so the sacrifice was a move of urgency as much as of brilliance.[^s3] Igor Zaitsev later used the game as a model in his book on attacking strong points.[^s3]

## Connections

The game was **played in** **Hastings 1895**, the tournament it is most closely associated with, and was **played by** **Wilhelm Steinitz** and **Curt von Bardeleben**.[^s1][^s2] It **exemplifies** the **sacrifice** and the **combination** against a king left in the centre, and its opening is the **Italian Game**.[^s4][^s2] The rook's march is a classic case of a **rook on the seventh rank**, and with the **Evergreen Game** it belongs to the small set of 19th-century brilliancies that every chess collection reprints.[^s3][^s6]

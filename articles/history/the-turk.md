# The Turk

**The Turk** was the most famous chess "automaton" in history: a life-size figure in a turban and oriental robes, seated behind a wooden cabinet with a chessboard on top, which appeared to play chess against visitors by clockwork.[^s1][^s6] It was built by Wolfgang von Kempelen, a Hungarian official trained in physics and mathematics, and first shown at the Viennese court of Empress Maria Theresa. The sources date the debut to 1769 or 1770.[^s1][^s4][^s7] In reality it was a pseudo-automaton: a strong human player hidden inside the cabinet chose and made the moves.[^s2][^s3] Over a career of more than seven decades it played at royal courts, fairs and exhibitions in Europe and America, until it was destroyed by fire in Philadelphia in 1854.[^s3][^s4][^s6]

## Origin and the illusion

Britannica relates that Kempelen had been invited to judge a magic show at the court in 1769, criticised it, and was given six months to produce something better; the Turk was the result.[^s1] JSTOR Daily gives the same story, naming the earlier act as a French inventor's magnetic demonstration.[^s6] The performance followed a fixed routine. The exhibitor opened the cabinet's doors one after another to show wheels, levers and gearing and apparently nothing else, wound the machine up, and then invited a volunteer to play.[^s1][^s6] The Turk usually won.[^s6]

The secret was a concealed operator. According to the Linda Hall Library, the operator sat on a sliding chair below the desk, out of sight of the audience.[^s3] The hidden player followed the game on a small board of his own and worked the figure's arm with a system of levers, a pantograph, that copied his hand movements onto the board above.[^s1][^s5] Because the Turk's strength was really that of whoever sat inside, the operators were capable players; many masters were rumoured to have done the job.[^s3]

## Tours, owners and famous opponents

From the 1780s Kempelen toured the Turk across Europe. In Paris it played Benjamin Franklin, and also François-André Philidor, the strongest player of the age, who won but admitted the game was hard work.[^s1] The Heinz Nixdorf MuseumsForum lists Napoleon among the opponents of its long career.[^s4] After Kempelen's death in 1804 the machine passed to the engineer Johann Nepomuk Maelzel, who kept exhibiting it and in 1826 took it to the United States.[^s1][^s3][^s6] In 1819 Charles Babbage watched it, saw through the trick, and began to think about how a machine might really play chess.[^s1]

Explanations multiplied while it was on show. Observers proposed magnetism, hidden strings, a trained monkey, evil spirits, or a dwarf or child inside the cabinet.[^s1][^s4][^s7] Britannica notes that the mystery inspired more than a dozen books.[^s2] The best-known essay was Edgar Allan Poe's "Maelzel's Chess-Player", published in Richmond's *Southern Literary Messenger* in April 1836 after Poe saw the Turk there in 1835. Poe argued correctly that a man was hidden inside, though the Linda Hall Library notes that his account of how it was done was wrong.[^s3][^s6]

## Philadelphia and the fire

Maelzel died at sea in 1838 and the Turk was auctioned in Philadelphia.[^s5] It was bought by a club of about 75 subscribers organised by John Kearsley Mitchell, a professor at Jefferson Medical College. Mitchell reassembled the machine from five crates of mixed parts in 1840 and placed it on display in the Chinese Museum at Ninth and Sansom Streets, where it drew few visitors.[^s5] On 5 July 1854 a fire that began at the nearby National Theatre spread to the museum and consumed the Turk.[^s3][^s5] Mitchell's son, Silas Weir Mitchell, later described the operating method in *The Chess Monthly*. He published his account three years after the fire.[^s5]

## Reconstruction

To mark the 200th anniversary of Kempelen's death, the Heinz Nixdorf MuseumsForum in Paderborn built a full-size working copy, which played its first game on 25 March 2004.[^s4] At the presentation an actor playing Kempelen opened every compartment before the figure began to play. Afterwards the museum revealed that a player had been hidden in the cabinet, following the moves on a board under the playing surface and steering the arm by pantograph.[^s7] A separate reconstruction was built in the United States by the illusionist John Gaughan.[^s3]

## Significance and common misconceptions

For chess history the Turk matters less for its games than for what it made people ask: could a machine play chess at all? Britannica places it at the start of the story of chess and artificial intelligence, as the first of the pseudo-automatons. The first real chess machine was Leonardo Torres y Quevedo's electromagnetic endgame device of 1890, which could mate with king and rook against king.[^s2] A common misconception is that the Turk fooled everyone. In fact most observers suspected an illusion, and the Linda Hall Library notes that the secret was widely known among chess players; what remained unclear for decades was exactly how the operator stayed hidden.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The Turk is **part of** the **history of chess** and was **developed by** Wolfgang von Kempelen.[^s1][^s3] It relates to **computer chess** and the **history of computer chess** as their best-known forerunner: a sham machine that raised the real question of mechanical play, a question Babbage took up after watching it.[^s1][^s2] It is related to **François-André Philidor**, its most celebrated opponent, and to **Charles Babbage**, whom it inspired.[^s1]

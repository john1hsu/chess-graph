# Hypermodernism

Hypermodernism is the school of chess thought that took shape among central European masters after the First World War.[^s1] Its leading exponents, Aron Nimzowitsch and Richard Réti, attacked Wilhelm Steinitz's approach to the centre and the strict rules that Siegbert Tarrasch had laid down, and took pleasure in showing that those guidelines could be broken with profit.[^s1][^s2] Savielly Tartakower is credited with giving the movement its name.[^s6] Réti summed up the attitude by saying that the new generation was interested less in rules than in their exceptions.[^s2]

## Main ideas

**Control rather than occupation of the centre.** Classical theory, going back to Philidor, treated a broad pawn centre as the goal of the opening. The hypermoderns argued that such a centre could be a liability, because it gives the opponent a fixed target.[^s2] Instead of rushing pawns to e4 and d4, they posted pieces on the flanks, above all fianchettoed bishops on b2, g2, b7 or g7, to bear on the central squares from a distance.[^s2]

**Provoking advances.** A hypermodern player often invites the opponent to advance central pawns, intending to attack them later once they are overextended.[^s2] Alexander Alekhine explored this in the 1920s with 1.e4 Nf6, tempting White to chase the knight with 2.e5 and so create a pawn that can later be undermined.[^s2]

**Surrendering the centre on purpose.** Nimzowitsch developed the idea, which Steinitz had claimed as his own, of exchanging central pawns voluntarily and occupying the vacated squares with minor pieces.[^s2] He also built up the theory of prophylaxis, the anticipation and restraint of the opponent's plans, which became one of the movement's most durable legacies.[^s2] His book *My System* (1925), regarded as a classic, set out the school's ideas, and Britannica credits him with a new vocabulary that made master strategy easier to understand.[^s5]

## Openings and players

Against 1.d4 Nimzowitsch preferred 1...Nf6 to the traditional 1...d5, aiming to control e4 with pieces; his systems grew into the Queen's Indian and the Nimzo-Indian (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4), which uses the pin on the c3 knight.[^s2][^s5] Réti often opened with 1.Nf3 and, in Soltis's description, advanced no more than one pawn beyond the third rank before the middlegame.[^s2] The Réti Opening, which begins 1.Nf3 d5 2.c4, is named after him and is characterised by fianchettoing both of White's bishops and playing c4.[^s4][^s6] Gyula Breyer added his own provocative joke that after 1.e4 White's game is already in its death throes.[^s2]

Réti, born in 1889 in Pezinok and died in Prague in 1929, was also a composer of problems and studies.[^s3] His book *Modern Ideas in Chess* (1923) and his newspaper columns made him a respected writer.[^s3][^s4] At New York 1924 he beat Capablanca, ending a run of 63 games without defeat, and won the brilliancy prize.[^s3][^s4] Keene notes the cost of the style, too: at that event Réti scored well with White but lost seven games with Black.[^s6]

## Example: Réti–Bogoljubow, New York 1924

Réti's brilliancy-prize game shows the method: a double fianchetto and a delayed central advance, then a sudden opening of lines when Black's centre gives way.[^s6] Keene's notes single out 17...exd4 as the error that opened the position for White's better-placed pieces, and the finish 25.Be8 exploits the weak back rank.[^s6]

## Legacy and pitfalls

By the late 1920s the hypermodern ideas had been absorbed into the mainstream, and most leading masters, Capablanca and Tarrasch among them, had tried hypermodern openings.[^s2] The next generation, led by the Soviet school, sought exceptions to other rules, prizing the initiative and unbalanced positions.[^s2] For a club player the lesson cuts both ways: a big pawn centre is a target as well as an asset, but "control from a distance" is not passivity. If the flank pieces do not actually press on the centre, the opponent's pawns simply gain space for free.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

Hypermodernism sits under **chess strategy** and the **history of chess**, as a reaction to the classical doctrine of **Wilhelm Steinitz** and **Siegbert Tarrasch**.[^s1][^s2] It was **championed by** **Aron Nimzowitsch**, whose ideas on **prophylaxis** and the **blockade** became core strategy, and by **Richard Réti**.[^s2][^s4][^s5] It **leads to** the **Nimzo-Indian Defence**, the **Réti Opening** and **Alekhine's Defence**, is **exemplified by** Réti–Bogoljubow, New York 1924, and was followed by the **Soviet chess school**.[^s2][^s4][^s6]

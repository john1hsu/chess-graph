# History of chess

The history of chess is the story of how a war game from northern India became the standardized international sport of today. Britannica finds no credible evidence of a game close to modern chess before the 6th century CE, and treats chaturanga, flourishing in northwestern India by the 7th century, as the earliest clear precursor.[^s1] The game then changed shape several times as it travelled, and the board of the 15th century is the first that a modern player would recognize as fully their own.[^s1]

## From India to medieval Europe

After about 600 CE the game spread west as shatranj, which added the counsellor (*firzān*), and east to China by about 750 and to Korea and Japan by the 11th century.[^s1] It reached Europe through Persia, Byzantium and the Arab world: Muslims carried it to North Africa, Sicily and Spain by the 10th century, Eastern Slavs took it to Kievan Rus at about the same time, and Vikings carried it to Iceland and England.[^s1] The Lewis chessmen, made in the 11th or 12th century and found in 1831, survive from this northern route.[^s1] The game was popular enough that Louis IX tried to ban it in France in 1254, and by the 15th century it was called the "royal game".[^s1]

## The modern game, after 1475

Medieval chess was slow because only the rook moved far. After about 1475 the counsellor became the queen, the strongest piece, and the bishop's range more than doubled; with these changes a game could end in mate within two moves, and the leisurely medieval style gave way.[^s1] The pawn's optional double step had appeared by 1300 but took centuries to be accepted, and castling and en passant were known in the 15th century but not widely used until the 18th.[^s1]

The key position records the shortest possible mate under the new rules, 1.f3 e5 2.g4 Qh4#: the long diagonal move of the queen, impossible for the old counsellor, decides the game at once. Printed manuals followed the new game quickly. Lucena's *Repetición de amores, y Arte de ajedres*, printed at Salamanca by Leonardus Hutz and Lupus Sanz, carries an imprint of about 1496, with October 1497 the most likely date; many surviving copies contain only the chess treatise.[^s3]

## From books to theory, 1600–1850

Britannica names Gioacchino Greco, around 1620, as the first chess professional; his composed games contrasting material greed with sacrificial attacks on the king helped make the game popular.[^s2] In 1749 Philidor's *L'Analyze des échecs* stressed planning and the role of pawns, ran through more than 100 editions, and first showed how pawn structure makes a bishop good or bad.[^s2] The Modena masters Del Rio, Lolli and Ponziani answered with rapid development and gambits aimed at the king, and the 19th-century Romantic era carried that taste for sacrifice to its height in Adolf Anderssen.[^s2]

## Competition and the world title

Organized competition grew in the 19th century. Staunton beat Saint-Amant in 1843 and organized the first international tournament, London 1851, which Anderssen won; Paul Morphy won the First American Chess Congress in 1857 and beat Anderssen in 1858; Steinitz beat Anderssen in 1866.[^s1] The Staunton pattern of pieces, designed by Nathaniel Cook about 1835 and registered in 1849, became the only style allowed in international play.[^s1] In 1886 Steinitz met Zukertort in the first match officially styled a world championship, played in New York, St Louis and New Orleans; after trailing 4–0, Steinitz won 10 games to 5 with 5 draws.[^s4][^s1] Steinitz also founded the positional school, accumulating small advantages before attacking.[^s2]

FIDE was founded in Paris in 1924 by representatives of 15 countries, and after Alekhine died in 1946 it took control of the world championship, organizing it from 1948.[^s1] Every champion and challenger from 1951 to 1969 was a Soviet citizen; Bobby Fischer broke the run in 1972, and Kasparov dethroned Karpov in 1985.[^s1] In theory, the hypermodern school after the First World War and the Soviet school's love of the initiative and unbalanced positions followed the classical teaching of Tarrasch, Lasker and Capablanca.[^s2]

## The computer era

In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat Kasparov 3.5–2.5 in a six-game rematch in New York, the first time a computer had beaten a reigning world champion in a match at standard tournament time controls.[^s5] The match is the best-known milestone in the contest between human champions and machines that the computer chess node covers in depth.[^s5]

## Pitfalls

Avoid two common errors. Do not treat legends of a single inventor as history: Britannica rejects claims for a game close to chess before the 6th century.[^s1] And remember that "unofficial" champions such as Staunton and Morphy are later labels; the first match officially billed as for the world title came only in 1886.[^s1][^s4]

# Carlsen versus Anand, Sochi 2014, game 6

**Carlsen versus Anand, game 6** is the win by Magnus Carlsen, with White, over Viswanathan Anand in the sixth game of their 2014 World Chess Championship match in Sochi, played on 15 November 2014.[^s1][^s3] It is remembered less for the winner's technique than for one moment: on move 26 Carlsen made a king move that allowed a winning tactic, and Anand replied within about a minute without seeing it.[^s1][^s2][^s3] FIDE's Open Chess Museum holds Carlsen's handwritten scoresheet of the game and notes that the win gave him a one-point lead.[^s4]

## The match setting

The Sochi match was a rematch: Anand had lost the title to Carlsen a year earlier and earned another chance by winning the 2014 Candidates Tournament.[^s6] After five games the score was level, and game 6 put Carlsen ahead 3½–2½.[^s1][^s2] He went on to retain the title by 6½–4½.[^s5] Carlsen was rated 2863 and Anand 2792 at the time.[^s1]

## Opening and middlegame plan

Carlsen chose an Open Sicilian, and the queens came off as early as move 9, leaving White a small but lasting edge in the ending.[^s2][^s3] His plan was slow and instructive: he advanced the h-pawn to h5 to fix Black's kingside, castled long, and swung a rook along the third rank with Rd1–d3–g3 to press on g6 and g7, while the pawn on e5 cramped Black's pieces.[^s3] Anand had spent a lot of clock time by move 18, a point the commentators remarked on during the broadcast.[^s1]

## The double blunder

The diagram shows the position after **26.Kd2**, a move Carlsen made in about a minute and later admitted was a serious mistake; he said he saw the problem as soon as he had played it.[^s2][^s3] Black could have played **26...Nxe5!**, and after 27.Rxg8 the natural recapture is delayed by the in-between check **27...Nxc4+**; only after 28.Kd3 Nb2+ 29.Kd2 does Black take back with 29...Rxg8, emerging two pawns up with the g2-pawn also under attack.[^s1][^s2] The Week in Chess suggests 26.Kd1 or 26.Rg3 as safer moves.[^s1]

Anand instead played **26...a4** after about a minute, intent on his own queenside plan of ...a4–a3, and said he realised his error immediately.[^s1][^s2][^s3] After 27.Ke2 White was fine again.[^s2] At the press conference Anand observed that when one is not expecting a gift, one sometimes fails to take it.[^s1]

## How Carlsen won

The miss seemed to unsettle Anand, and the rest of the game was short.[^s3] ChessBase and The Week in Chess regard 31...Ba4 as dubious and 32...Bc6 as the decisive error; Carlsen then traded on g6, collected the kingside and central pawns with his rook, picked up the a3-pawn and forced resignation after 38.Re7+.[^s1][^s2] Carlsen, for his part, said he felt "massively relieved" after the game.[^s1]

## Lessons for club players

The game shows that a world-class player can play the move he has been planning without a fresh blunder check, and that an opponent's sudden error is easy to miss when one is focused on one's own plan.[^s2][^s3] Both mistakes took about a minute each, which is why commentators linked them to hurried decisions rather than to lack of understanding.[^s1][^s3] Carlsen's scoresheet in the FIDE museum records both players' clock times every five moves, a habit the museum points to as good time-management practice.[^s4]

## Connections

The game was **played by** **Magnus Carlsen** and **Viswanathan Anand** and **played in** the 2014 **World Chess Championship** match in Sochi.[^s1][^s5][^s6] Its opening is a **Sicilian Defence** that turned into a queenless **endgame** early on.[^s2][^s3] The missed refutation **exemplifies** the **zwischenzug**, since 27...Nxc4+ is an in-between check before the recapture on g8, and it is a standard illustration of the role of blunder checks in **calculation** and of hurried moves in **time management**.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

# Immortal Game

The **Immortal Game** is the informal game Adolf Anderssen won against Lionel Kieseritzky in London in 1851, the year Anderssen won the first modern international tournament.[^s1][^s3] Anderssen gave away a bishop, both rooks and his queen and then mated with his three remaining minor pieces, while Kieseritzky still had almost his whole army.[^s3][^s4] Britannica explains the nickname simply: players believed the game's fame would last forever.[^s1] It is the best-known product of the Romantic era, though its exact date, venue and final moves are less certain than its reputation suggests.[^s2][^s4]

## Circumstances and record

The game was not part of the knockout tournament that Howard Staunton organised for the Great Exhibition. It was one of many casual games played alongside it.[^s3][^s4] The loser published it himself: Kieseritzky printed the score in his Paris magazine *La Régence* in July 1851, praising Anderssen, and *The Chess Player* of 19 July 1851 also gave it.[^s2][^s4]

Many details are disputed. *La Régence* dates the game 21 June 1851, the date most modern accounts repeat.[^s2][^s3] A German source instead places it on the eve of the tournament, and the historian John Townsend argues that 26 May 1851 is the likeliest date.[^s2] The venue is often given as Simpson's Divan in the Strand,[^s5] but Edward Winter found no contemporary source for this, and the evidence he collects points rather to the St George's Chess Club.[^s2] Colours were also loose in that period: *The Chess Player* seems to have given Anderssen Black, although he moved first, and later publications show him as White.[^s2][^s3] Nor is it clear who coined the name. ChessBase credits the *Wiener Schachzeitung* in 1855, while reference books variously name Ernst Falkbeer or Philipp Hirschfeld, and Winter traced no early use of it.[^s2][^s4]

## How the game went

The opening is a King's Gambit Accepted with 3.Bc4 rather than the more common 3.Nf3, which would have prevented the check. Black's check on h4 cost White the right to castle.[^s5][^s6] Kieseritzky then offered a pawn with 4...b5 to deflect the bishop from f7. His queen was chased round the board, and his 7...Nh5 put a knight on the rim.[^s5][^s6] On move 11 Anderssen left his bishop to be taken and played for the initiative instead. Rather than defend material, he kept bringing pieces into play with tempo.[^s5][^s6]

The decisive idea is **18.Bd6**. White leaves both rooks en prise, because the bishop takes f8 away from Black's king and controls the dark squares around it. White's knights on d5 and f5 meanwhile close in on g7, c7 and e7.[^s5][^s6] After Black has taken both rooks, 22.Qf6+ forces the knight to capture the queen, and the bishop mates on e7.[^s5][^s6]

Moves 18–20 follow Kieseritzky's own version in *La Régence*, which ends 19.e5 Qxa1+ 20.Ke2.[^s2][^s6] Some modern accounts give the order 18...Qxa1+ 19.Ke2 Bxg1 20.e5, which reaches the same position. Winter notes that there is no consensus on the exact final moves.[^s2][^s5]

## Errors and lessons

Engines and annotators are hard on the game. Robert Hübner's analysis for ChessBase found many errors and possible improvements, and ChessBase describes it as full of mistakes, many of them Anderssen's.[^s3][^s4] It was a casual game, played at speed for pleasure, so it should be judged as such.[^s4] The useful lessons are still real ones. A king stuck in the centre is a target. A lead in development and well-placed knights can outweigh a large material deficit. And a defender who grabs every piece offered, as Kieseritzky did with his queen raid on b2 and a1, can leave his own king without defenders.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

The Immortal Game is the standard **example of** the **Romantic era**, the period of open gambits and sacrificial attacks that Anderssen led, and of the **sacrifice** and **combination** as attacking weapons.[^s1][^s4] It was **played by** Adolf Anderssen and Lionel Kieseritzky. It is related to the **London 1851** tournament, which formed its backdrop, and to the **King's Gambit**, its opening.[^s3][^s5]

# World Chess Championship

The World Chess Championship is the contest that decides who holds the title of world chess champion. Since 1886 it has almost always been settled by a head-to-head match between the reigning champion and a challenger, and since 1948 it has been organised by FIDE, the International Chess Federation.[^s1] Its history mirrors the history of competitive chess: private challenges between masters, a long period of Soviet dominance, a schism in the 1990s and a reunified, regulated cycle today.[^s1][^s2]

## Origins: the champions' era (1886–1946)

The first match recognised as being for the world title was played in 1886 between Wilhelm Steinitz and Johannes Zukertort, in New York, Saint Louis and New Orleans. Zukertort led early on, but Steinitz recovered and won.[^s1] For the next six decades the title effectively belonged to its holder, who negotiated with challengers over terms, stakes and venues. Emanuel Lasker took it from Steinitz in 1894, José Raúl Capablanca ended Lasker's long reign in Havana in 1921, and Alexander Alekhine beat Capablanca in Buenos Aires in 1927. Max Euwe's victory over Alekhine in 1935 was the biggest upset of the era, and Alekhine won the title back in 1937.[^s1]

## FIDE takes control (1948–1993)

When Alekhine died in 1946 as champion, the title was vacant, and FIDE stepped in to set rules so that it no longer depended on a private owner.[^s1] In 1948 FIDE held a five-player match-tournament, split between The Hague and Moscow, which Mikhail Botvinnik won ahead of Keres, Smyslov, Reshevsky and Euwe.[^s1] From 1951 to 1969 every title match was an all-Soviet affair held in Moscow, with Botvinnik, Smyslov, Tal, Petrosian and then Spassky holding the crown.[^s1] Bobby Fischer broke the sequence by beating Spassky in Reykjavík in 1972; Anatoly Karpov became champion in 1975 when Fischer did not defend, and Garry Kasparov defeated Karpov in 1985.[^s1]

## The split and reunification (1993–2006)

In 1993 Kasparov and his challenger Nigel Short broke away from FIDE and played their London match under a new body, the Professional Chess Association; Kasparov won, while FIDE staged its own title match in which Karpov beat Jan Timman.[^s1] For thirteen years there were two "world champions". FIDE also experimented with large knockout events around 2000–2002, one of which made Ruslan Ponomariov champion at eighteen.[^s1] The schism ended with a reunification match announced by FIDE in 2006: Vladimir Kramnik met Veselin Topalov in Elista, Kalmykia, and won after a tiebreak to become undisputed champion.[^s2]

## The modern cycle

Today the challenger earns the right to play by winning the Candidates Tournament. For the 2026 cycle this was an eight-player double round robin of 14 rounds held in Cyprus from 27 March to 17 April 2026, with rapid and blitz play-offs only to break a tie for first place; the winner qualifies for the next title match.[^s4]

The match itself, as set out in FIDE's 2024 match regulations, consists of 14 classical games, and the first player to reach 7½ points wins.[^s3] The classical time control is 120 minutes for 40 moves, then 30 minutes for the rest of the game with a 30-second increment from move 41, and players may not agree a draw before Black's 40th move.[^s3] A 7–7 tie goes to a ladder of faster games: four rapid games, then two faster rapid games, then two blitz games, and finally Armageddon-style games until one is decisive.[^s3] The 2024 prize fund was 2.5 million US dollars, with 200,000 dollars paid per game won and the balance split equally.[^s3]

These rules decided the 2024 match in Singapore, where Gukesh D beat Ding Liren 7½–6½ to become, at 18, the youngest world champion in history and the 18th in FIDE's list.[^s5] As of November 2026 Gukesh is the reigning champion, and he is due to defend the title against Javokhir Sindarov of Uzbekistan, who won the 2026 Candidates Tournament with a round to spare, in Geneva from 25 November to 15 December 2026, again over 14 games with 7½ points needed to win.[^s6]

## Why it matters to players

For a club player the championship is the best-documented thread through chess history: each match is a snapshot of the strategic and opening ideas of its time, from Steinitz's positional principles to the deep computer-assisted preparation of modern matches. The format also shapes how matches are played. Because a single loss can be decisive over 14 games, champions and challengers tend to prepare solid repertoires with Black and save their sharpest ideas for key moments, while the ban on early draw agreements forces most games to be played out.[^s3] Studying title matches in order, game by game, is a classic way to see how elite understanding of openings, middlegames and endgames has changed.[^s1]

## Connections

The championship is sanctioned by **FIDE**, which has run it since 1948, and its games are played under the **FIDE Laws of Chess** supplemented by match regulations.[^s1][^s3] Its parts include the first official match, **World Chess Championship 1886**, won by **Wilhelm Steinitz**, and the 1972 **Fischer–Spassky** match.[^s1] The challenger route runs through the **Candidates Tournament**, and the winner of the match holds the title of **world chess champion**, currently **Gukesh D** as of November 2026.[^s4][^s6] More broadly it is the central thread of the **history of chess**.[^s1]

# Shatranj

Shatranj is the form of chess played across the medieval Islamic world, and the game from which European chess grew. Britannica describes it as a two-player game that took shape after about 600 CE, developing gradually out of the Indian game on the same 64-square board and adding a new piece, the counsellor or *firzān*.[^s1] It is the earliest member of the chess family whose rules are well documented, and the first to have professional players and a written literature.[^s5]

## From chatrang to shatranj

In Sasanian Persia the Indian game became *chatrang*, with pieces called shah, farzin, pil, asp, rukh and piyadah.[^s4] When Arab armies conquered the Sasanian Empire in the mid-7th century, the game passed into the Islamic world as *shatranj*, an Arabic adaptation of the Persian name, and its piece names were Arabicized.[^s4][^s5] The Persian cry *shah mat*, roughly "the king is helpless", is the origin of the word checkmate.[^s4]

The game spread under the Umayyads, and around the 8th century the pieces became more standardized and abstract.[^s4] A nearly complete 12th-century glazed set from Nishapur in Iran, one of the earliest surviving chess sets, shows this style: the king is a throne, the vizier a smaller throne, the elephant a block with two tusk-like points, the horse a knob, and the chariot a rectangle with a wedge on top.[^s2]

## Rules

The modern player recognises most of the army. Following the reconstruction collected on the Chess Variants Pages:[^s5]

- **Shāh (king)** moves one square in any direction; there is no castling.[^s5]
- **Rukh (rook)** and **faras (knight)** move exactly as today.[^s5]
- **Firzān (counsellor)** moves only one square diagonally.[^s5]
- **Fil (elephant)** leaps exactly two squares diagonally, jumping over anything in between.[^s5]
- **Baidaq (pawn)** moves one square forward and captures one square diagonally, with no double step and no en passant; on the last rank it can promote only to a counsellor.[^s5]

A player won by checkmate, by stalemating the opponent, or by stripping the enemy king of every other piece (a "bare king"), unless the opponent could bare the winner's king on the very next move, in which case the game was drawn.[^s5] Sources disagree on details such as repetition and on regional customs, so no single rulebook covers every period.[^s5]

The short-range pieces shape the whole game. Because the fil always jumps two squares diagonally, each one can visit only eight squares of the board, and the counsellor creeps one diagonal step at a time, so there is no long-range piece except the rook.[^s5][^s6] With so few long-range pieces, play was slow, which helps explain why the bare-king and stalemate wins mattered: they offered ways to win besides checkmate.[^s5][^s6]

## Masters and literature

The game flourished under the Abbasid Caliphate from 750, and Arabic literature records expert players, some of whom played blindfold.[^s4] In the 9th and 10th centuries, al-Adli, ar-Razi and as-Suli wrote books that were copied for centuries; they preserve the moves and rules and include *mansubat*, composed positions used to train tactics and strategy.[^s4] As-Suli, a favourite of Caliph al-Muktafi in Baghdad, belonged to the top class of players known as *aliyat* and wrote what Raymond Keene calls the first systematic book on playing the game.[^s6] Keene also reports that one of as-Suli's problems, which its author called extremely difficult, was only fully solved in modern times with computer help.[^s6]

## Into Europe

Shatranj reached Christian Europe through contact zones, chiefly Italy and Spain, no later than the early 10th century.[^s4] The Spanish *ajedrez* and Portuguese *xadrez* both derive from the Arabic name.[^s5] The best European witness is the *Libro de los juegos* made for Alfonso X of Castile in 1283, whose roughly 150 illustrations cover chess, dice and table games and place chess, as the game of reason, above games of chance.[^s3] It draws heavily on Arabic *mansubat*, and in Alfonso's Spain the board was already chequered.[^s4][^s5] In the late 15th century the bishop gained its long diagonal move and the queen became the strongest piece; this "queen's chess" displaced shatranj within a generation or two.[^s4]

## Pitfalls for modern readers

Do not read modern values back into the old pieces: the counsellor and elephant were minor units, and promotion to a counsellor did not decide games the way queening does today.[^s5] Treat attributions of famous medieval games and problems with care, since some survive only in later copies or reconstructions.[^s6]

## Connections

Shatranj sits in the **history of chess** and the **chess variants** family. It was **influenced by** **chaturanga**, its Indian ancestor, and **leads to** modern **chess** through the late-medieval reforms.[^s1][^s4] Its *mansubat* are forerunners of **chess problems**, and its treatment of **stalemate** as a win and its weak **pawn promotion** show how those rules have changed.[^s4][^s5] As-Suli is its best-known **champion**.[^s6]

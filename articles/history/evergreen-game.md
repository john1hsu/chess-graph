# Evergreen Game

The **Evergreen Game** is Adolf Anderssen's win with White against Jean Dufresne, an Evans Gambit in which Anderssen gave up a knight, a rook and his queen and then mated with his two bishops.[^s1][^s3] Next to the Immortal Game of 1851 it is the most famous product of Romantic chess; Capablanca in 1913 even called it "the other immortal".[^s1] Club players meet it in almost every collection of classic games, because its final combination is short, forcing and easy to remember.[^s3][^s4]

## Record, date and name

The earliest known printing is the *Deutsche Schachzeitung* of September 1852, with a further note in the October issue; the *Chess Player's Chronicle* reprinted it in 1853 without naming a venue.[^s1] Berlin 1852 is the conventional heading, used for example by Collijn in 1918, but Edward Winter points out that no early source proves the venue, and he raises Breslau, Anderssen's home city, as an open possibility.[^s1] Later datings to 1853 or 1856 cannot be right, since the game was already in print in 1852.[^s1]

The name comes from Wilhelm Steinitz. Annotating the game in *The Field* in March 1879 in a tribute to the departed Anderssen, he began his note to 19.Rad1 by calling it an evergreen in the dead master's laurel crown.[^s1] Winter found no use of the name in the first printings of 1852–53.[^s1] Lasker's *Common Sense in Chess*, from his London lectures of 1895, already refers to it as the "evergreen partie" and gives the full score.[^s2]

Winter also corrects a long-repeated error about the loser: Jean Dufresne, a chess writer and co-author with Zukertort of a large chess handbook, was a real person, and "E. S. Freund" was a pen name he used, not the other way round.[^s1]

## How the game went

Dufresne accepted the gambit and met 7.O-O with 7...d3, a line Lasker already called obsolete in his lectures.[^s2] Anderssen castled early, pushed e4–e5 to drive the queen and cut Black off from f6, aimed his bishop from a3 at f8, and brought the queen's knight to e4.[^s3][^s4] When Black's king was still on e8, **17.Nf6+** gave up the knight to open the g-file and plant a pawn on f6, and Black answered with a counterattack on g2.[^s3]

The key position arises after **19.Rad1**, a quiet rook move that ignores the threats against White's own king. Lasker called it one of the most subtle and profound moves on record.[^s2] Black took the knight with 19...Qxf3, and White finished with 20.Rxe7+ Nxe7 21.Qxd7+!! Kxd7 22.Bf5+, a double check from bishop and rook, after which the bishops mate.[^s2][^s3]

This is the usual modern score.[^s3] Lasker's version ends one move sooner, with 22...Kc6 23.Bd7#, and he adds that 20...Kd8 also loses to 21.Rxd7+ Kc8 22.Rd8+ Nxd8 23.Qd7+ and mate in two more moves.[^s2] The notes credited to Kasparov treat 22...Kc6 23.Bd7# as the alternative finish.[^s3]

## Was it sound?

Generations of annotators have argued over 19.Rad1. In the ChessBase notes, Lipke's 19...Rg4 is called much stronger than the game's 19...Qxf3, and Zaitsev's 19...Bd4! leads to a draw; Lasker's alternative 19.Be4 is examined at length without a clear verdict.[^s3] Winter cites a 1958 report that a team analysing the game concluded Anderssen's 20-minute decision was sound after all, which shows how unsettled the question has stayed.[^s1] The annotator's point is that the game is a masterpiece of attacking play even though, like most brilliancies of the era, it needed some help from the loser.[^s3]

## Lessons for the club player

The instructive part is the build-up. The gambit bought White open lines and a lead in development, the bishop on a3 kept Black's king in the centre, and every white piece joined the attack before the sacrifices began.[^s3][^s4] Black spent his time on grabbing and holding material and on a direct counterattack, and never got his king to safety.[^s4] The finish is a model of a double check: a king attacked by two pieces must move, so even the strongest defender cannot block or capture.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The Evergreen Game is the second classic **example of** the **Romantic era** after the **Immortal Game**, and of the **sacrifice** and the **combination** as weapons against an uncastled king.[^s1][^s3] It was **played by** Adolf Anderssen and Jean Dufresne. Its opening is the **Evans Gambit**, and it is linked to **Wilhelm Steinitz**, whose 1879 tribute named it, and to **Emanuel Lasker**, who used it in his lectures and analysed an alternative to 19.Rad1.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

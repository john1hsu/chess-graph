# Botvinnik versus Capablanca, AVRO 1938

**Botvinnik versus Capablanca** is the win by Mikhail Botvinnik, with White, over the former world champion José Raúl Capablanca in the 11th round of the AVRO tournament, played in Rotterdam on 22 November 1938.[^s1][^s3] It became Botvinnik's best-known game, chiefly for the combination that starts with 30.Ba3, which Botvinnik himself described as the opening of a twelve-move combination.[^s1] The World Chess Hall of Fame singles it out as a notable game in its profile of Botvinnik, the first of the Soviet world champions.[^s2]

## The tournament setting

AVRO, a Dutch radio broadcaster, staged an eight-player double round-robin from 6 to 27 November 1938, moving between Amsterdam, Haarlem, Arnhem, Rotterdam and other Dutch towns.[^s1][^s3] The field was Alekhine, Botvinnik, Capablanca, Euwe, Fine, Flohr, Keres and Reshevsky.[^s1] Keres and Fine shared first on 8½ points, with Keres placed first on tie-break; Botvinnik was third on 7½ and Capablanca seventh on 6.[^s3] Their first-cycle game in round 4 had been drawn.[^s3] This loss was one of four Capablanca suffered in the event; he later blamed his physical condition and said he had played mechanically after the third hour.[^s1] After round 11 Botvinnik had climbed into a share of second place.[^s5]

## Ideas behind the win

The structure is typical of the Nimzo-Indian with doubled c-pawns: once Black played ...c4, the queenside was fixed and White's chances lay in the centre and on the kingside.[^s4][^s5] Botvinnik let Black's queen and knight wander off to win the a-pawn while he pushed e4, e5, f4 and f5, and the exchanges on the e-file left him a protected passed pawn on e6 deep in Black's camp.[^s4][^s5]

In the diagram position before move 30, Black's queen on e7 both blockades the e-pawn and helps guard the kingside. 30.Ba3 offers the bishop to deflect the queen from that double duty.[^s4] After 30...Qxa3, the knight sacrifice 31.Nh5+ removed the knight on f6 from the defence by way of 32.Qg5+ and 33.Qxf6+, and 34.e7 threatened to promote.[^s4] Black's only hope was perpetual check, and White's king had to walk up the board to h5 to escape it; the ChessBase commentary notes it is hard to say which part was harder to calculate, the sacrifices or the king's escape.[^s4][^s5]

## How much did Botvinnik see?

Annotators have often written that Botvinnik foresaw everything when he played 30.Ba3.[^s1] Edward Winter points out that Botvinnik's own notes in *Half a Century of Chess* say the opposite: he admitted he could not calculate the line to the end, worked in two stages, and at first only satisfied himself that he had at least a draw by perpetual check.[^s1] The lesson for club players is practical: a sacrifice can be sound when the worst case is a draw and the best case is a win, even if the whole line cannot be seen at once.[^s1]

## Connections

The game was **played by** **Mikhail Botvinnik** and **José Raúl Capablanca** and **played in** the **AVRO 1938** tournament.[^s1][^s3] It **exemplifies** **deflection** and the **sacrifice** for an attack, it ends in the race between a **passed pawn** heading for **promotion** and Black's attempts at **perpetual check**, and its opening is a **Nimzo-Indian Defence**.[^s4][^s5] It also links to the **Soviet chess school**, since Botvinnik was the first of the long line of Soviet world champions.[^s2]

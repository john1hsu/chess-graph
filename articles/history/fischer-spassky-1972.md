# Fischer–Spassky 1972

The 1972 World Chess Championship was played in Reykjavík between the titleholder Boris Spassky of the USSR and the American challenger Bobby Fischer. It was organised by FIDE and the Icelandic Chess Federation in the Laugardalshöll arena from July to September, and Fischer won 12½–8½ after 21 of a scheduled 24 games.[^s1] The World Chess Hall of Fame calls it the most famous match of modern times, often billed as the "Match of the Century", because it broke 24 years of Soviet control of the title during the Cold War.[^s2]

## Background

Spassky had taken the title in 1969 by a narrow 12½–10½ win over Tigran Petrosian.[^s3] Fischer arrived as the clear favourite on form: in the 1970–71 qualifying events he won 20 consecutive games, a record, beat former champion Petrosian in the 1971 Candidates final, and afterwards stood 125 rating points above the world number two, Spassky, another record gap.[^s2] OlimpBase lists the players' ratings for the match as 2785 for Fischer and 2660 for Spassky.[^s1] The Hall of Fame also stresses the asymmetry of support: Fischer worked with far less state backing than his Soviet rivals and came from a much smaller pool of strong compatriots.[^s2]

## Format and officials

The match was over a maximum of 24 games, with 12½ points needed to win, at a time control of 2 hours 30 minutes for 40 moves followed by 1 hour for 16 moves. Lothar Schmid of West Germany was chief arbiter, assisted by Guðmundur Arnlaugsson of Iceland.[^s1]

## Course of the match

The start was turbulent. In game 1, played on 11 July, Fischer grabbed a pawn with 29...Bxh2 in a position most observers considered drawn, and he resigned on move 56, later blaming the filming cameras.[^s4] He then forfeited game 2 in protest over the cameras, leaving him 0–2 down.[^s4][^s1] Game 3 was moved to a small back room away from the audience, and Fischer won it with Black.[^s4][^s1]

From there the match turned. Fischer won games 5, 6 and 8, and after game 10 he led 6½–3½.[^s1][^s4] Game 6 was a shock: Fischer, an almost exclusive 1.e4 player, opened 1.c4 and steered into a Queen's Gambit Declined, and Spassky lost for the first time with his favourite Tartakower Variation.[^s5] Spassky hit back in game 11 by crushing Fischer's Poisoned Pawn Najdorf, but Fischer won game 13 with the Alekhine's Defence, which he had never before played at top level.[^s6] Games 14 to 20 were all drawn.[^s1] Game 21, a Sicilian, was adjourned in a lost position for Spassky, who resigned by telephone on 1 September; Schmid then announced Fischer as champion in the hall.[^s6] The final tally was seven wins to Fischer, three to Spassky (one by forfeit) and eleven draws.[^s1]

## Significance and aftermath

The Hall of Fame credits the match with greatly increasing the popularity of chess in the United States, and marked its fiftieth anniversary in 2022 with an exhibition of more than 500 artifacts, including the pieces used in game 3.[^s2] Twenty years later the two played a privately organised rematch in Yugoslavia, then under United Nations sanctions, which Fischer won.[^s3]

## Connections

The match is **part of** the **world chess championship** lineage and was **sanctioned by FIDE**.[^s1] It was **played by** **Bobby Fischer**, who **won** it, and **Boris Spassky**.[^s1] It ended the run of the **Soviet chess school** at the top.[^s2] Its openings make it a reference point for the **Queen's Gambit** (game 6), the **Najdorf Variation** (game 11) and **Alekhine's Defence** (game 13).[^s5][^s6]

# Hastings 1895

Hastings 1895 was an international master tournament held in the English seaside town of Hastings from 5 August to 2 September 1895. Twenty-two players met in a single round robin of 21 rounds, so every competitor played every other once, with the usual scoring of one point for a win and a half for a draw.[^s1][^s5] It is remembered as one of the strongest tournaments of the nineteenth century and above all for its surprise winner, the young American Harry Nelson Pillsbury.[^s4][^s5]

## The field and the format

The congress was organised by the Hastings and St Leonards Chess Club and played at the Brassey Institute, the building that now houses the town's public library. Pairings were drawn by lot and announced on the morning of each playing day, and the time control was 30 moves in the first two hours followed by 15 moves per hour.[^s5] The entry brought together almost the whole of the world elite: the reigning world champion Emanuel Lasker, his predecessor Wilhelm Steinitz, and Mikhail Chigorin, Siegbert Tarrasch, Isidor Gunsberg, Dawid Janowski and Carl Schlechter, all of whom played or would play for the world title.[^s5] Veterans such as Joseph Henry Blackburne, Henry Bird and Amos Burn sat alongside younger masters including Richard Teichmann and Jacques Mieses.[^s1] Of the leading names, only Schlechter was younger than Pillsbury.[^s6]

## The result

Pillsbury, who was representing the Brooklyn Chess Club and playing his first international tournament, finished first with 16½ points, half a point ahead of Chigorin on 16 and a full point ahead of Lasker on 15½. Tarrasch took fourth with 14 and Steinitz fifth with 13; Emanuel Schiffers was sixth with 12, and Teichmann and Curt von Bardeleben shared seventh on 11½.[^s1][^s5] The race went to the final round, in which Pillsbury beat Gunsberg in a knight ending to secure first place.[^s5][^s6] The World Chess Hall of Fame describes him as a previously unknown American who won one of the strongest events held up to that time.[^s4]

## Games and ideas

The tournament produced several games still studied today. The best known is Steinitz's tenth-round win over von Bardeleben, a sacrificial attack against the uncastled king.[^s5] Pillsbury's second-round victory over Tarrasch, then the highest-rated player in the field by later historical estimates, showed a modern plan in the Queen's Gambit Declined (ECO D63): White established a knight on e5, advanced the f-pawn, and switched his forces to the kingside while Black pushed his queenside pawns.[^s3][^s5] The final position, after 52.Qxh7#, is given as this node's `fen`. Pillsbury's dynamic handling of the Queen's Gambit helped make that opening fashionable at master level.[^s4] Tarrasch's eighteenth-round win against Janowski is likewise cited as a model treatment of an isolated-pawn position.[^s5]

## Legacy and records

All 231 games survive in the official tournament book, edited by Horace F. Cheshire and published in 1896 by Chatto & Windus in London and G. P. Putnam's Sons in New York; its annotated scores remain the primary record of the event.[^s2] The result unsettled the established order at the top of chess and led directly to the four-player St Petersburg tournament of 1895–96, where Pillsbury, weakened by illness, finished third.[^s4][^s5] Pillsbury went on to win the US championship from Jackson Showalter in 1897.[^s4] A popular legend that Sherlock Holmes impersonated Pillsbury at Hastings comes from a 1964 article and is not taken seriously by chess historians.[^s6]

## Connections

Hastings 1895 is a classic `chess-tournament` of the round-robin type, and it is the event in which Steinitz, Lasker and Tarrasch all `playedIn`, as their own articles record.[^s1] It was `wonBy` Pillsbury, and its most famous brilliancy, Steinitz versus von Bardeleben, has its own node. The Pillsbury–Tarrasch game `exemplifies` the period's shift from purely tactical play towards positional Queen's Gambit plans, a bridge between the `romantic-era` and the modern game.[^s3][^s4] Its sequel was St Petersburg 1895–96.[^s5]

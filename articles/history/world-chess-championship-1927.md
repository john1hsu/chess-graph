# World Chess Championship 1927

The World Chess Championship 1927 was the title match between the champion José Raúl Capablanca and the challenger Alexander Alekhine, played in Buenos Aires from 16 September to 29 November 1927. The winner was the first to six wins, and after 34 games Alekhine had won six, Capablanca three, with 25 draws, a final score of 18½–15½.[^s1][^s3] Newspapers of the time reported that it had set a new record for the length of a world championship match.[^s4]

## The London Rules and the purse

Capablanca had held the title since beating Emanuel Lasker in 1921. In 1922 he proposed the so-called London Rules, under which a challenger had to raise a purse of $10,000; the FIDE museum notes that Alekhine was the one who managed to find the money.[^s3] Edward Winter describes the 1927 match as the only world title match actually played under those rules, at the Club Argentino de Ajedrez, which put up the $10,000. The champion took 20% as a fee, and the remaining 80% was split 60–40 between winner and loser.[^s2] Draws did not count and there was no limit on the number of games, which explains why the match lasted more than two months.[^s2]

A long-running story holds that a 5–5 score would have ended the match drawn, leaving Capablanca champion. Winter found no such clause in the London Rules or in the Argentine press of the time, and traces the claim to later writers.[^s2]

## Course of the match

Alekhine won the first game with Black, from a French Defence, and the openings that followed were almost all forms of the Queen's Gambit Declined.[^s5] Capablanca struck back in games 3 and 7; the FIDE museum keeps his own scoresheet of game 7, after which he led by two wins to one.[^s3][^s1] Alekhine then won games 11 and 12, and by mid-October he led 3–2.[^s1][^s2] A long run of draws followed, broken by Alekhine's win with Black in game 21. Game 22, at 86 moves the longest of the match, was drawn, as Alekhine could not convert what Winter calls a potential fifth win that would have all but settled the result.[^s1][^s4][^s2] Capablanca's win in game 29 brought him back to within one, but Alekhine won games 32 and 34, the last of them a long rook ending that ended with 82.Re7.[^s1][^s2]

The press of the day noted the slow pace. The *Hawke's Bay Tribune*, reprinting wire copy, reported that Alekhine once spent nineteen minutes on a single move in game 16, and that Capablanca had to be woken when it was his turn.[^s4]

## Example: game 1

The first game set the tone. Against Capablanca's French Winawer, Delayed Exchange (ECO C01), Alekhine used the open e-file, struck with 16...Nxc2 to win material, and after a series of exchanges finished the game with his queen and rook against the exposed white king. Capablanca resigned after 43...Rf2.[^s6][^s7] The score is from the chessgames.com record; its finish from move 16 matches the sequence given by FM Paul Whitehead.[^s6][^s7]

## Aftermath

Alekhine became the fourth world champion. Capablanca soon wrote proposing a return match for 1929, and Alekhine answered that he would play one on the same terms once formally challenged, but no rematch was ever agreed.[^s2] Whitehead, writing for the Mechanics' Institute, judges that Alekhine prepared very hard while Capablanca's play was somewhat lacklustre, and that Alekhine later avoided a return match.[^s7]

## Connections

The match is **part of** the **World Chess Championship** lineage, a line that began with the **World Chess Championship 1886**.[^s1] It was **played by** **José Raúl Capablanca** and **Alexander Alekhine**, and **won by** Alekhine.[^s1] It was **organised by** the **Club Argentino de Ajedrez**, under the **London Rules** that Capablanca himself had drafted.[^s2][^s3] Its first game is a **French Defence**, while nearly all the rest were variations of the **Queen's Gambit Declined**.[^s5]

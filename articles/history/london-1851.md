# London 1851

London 1851 was a chess tournament held in London from late May to July 1851, timed to coincide with the Great Exhibition, and it is generally treated as the first international chess tournament.[^s1][^s5] Howard Staunton, then widely considered the strongest player in the world, took the lead in organising it.[^s3] The German master Adolf Anderssen won it, and the result moved the centre of the chess world away from Staunton and towards the attacking style that Anderssen came to represent.[^s2][^s4]

## Organisation and field

Staunton hoped the Great Exhibition, which was already drawing visitors from across Europe, would make it easier for foreign masters to travel to London. He raised a prize fund of about £500 from English and overseas clubs, with the Calcutta Chess Club alone contributing £100.[^s2] Invitations went to leading continental players including Szén, Löwenthal, Anderssen, Horwitz, Mayet, von der Lasa, Saint-Amant, Kieseritzky, Jaenisch and Petrov, but several could not or did not come: von der Lasa and Petrov stayed away, Saint-Amant was abroad on diplomatic service, and Jaenisch and Shumov arrived late. A feud between the St George's Club and the London Chess Club also kept some London players, notably Harrwitz, out of the event.[^s2] Anderssen, a schoolteacher, nearly declined because of the travel costs, and Staunton paid his expenses.[^s2]

## Format

The sixteen entrants played a knockout series of mini-matches, paired by lot without seeding.[^s1][^s2] First-round matches were best of three games, and later rounds required four wins; draws did not count towards the score.[^s1][^s5] Losers of the later rounds played further matches to settle the places below the winner, so eight players received a final ranking.[^s1][^s5] Staunton proposed a "chess parliament" to settle questions such as notation and time limits, because some players were known to out-sit their opponents.[^s2]

## Results

In the first round Anderssen beat Lionel Kieseritzky 2–0, Staunton beat Brodie 2–0, and Elijah Williams beat Löwenthal 2–1, while Bird and Löwenthal were among those eliminated.[^s1][^s2] In the quarter-finals Anderssen defeated József Szén 4–2 and Staunton beat Bernhard Horwitz 4–2.[^s1] The decisive semi-final saw Anderssen beat Staunton 4–1; GM Julio Becerra singles out the third game, in which Staunton outplayed Anderssen positionally and then missed a simple win.[^s1][^s5] In the other semi-final, Marmaduke Wyvill, a Member of Parliament and amateur player, beat Williams 4–3.[^s1][^s6]

Anderssen won the final against Wyvill 4–2 (four wins, two losses and a draw that did not count).[^s1][^s6] Williams then beat Staunton 4–3 for third place, so the organiser and favourite finished only fourth.[^s1][^s3] Szén took fifth place ahead of Hugh Alexander Kennedy, Horwitz and James Mucklow.[^s1]

## Aftermath and the tournament book

Under the event's rules, Staunton challenged Anderssen to a longer match of 21 games; Anderssen accepted in principle but had to return to his teaching post in Germany, and the match never took place.[^s5] Staunton wrote the official tournament book, *The Chess Tournament*, which records the games and his commentary; it was reissued by Hardinge Simpole in 2003.[^s4] The book is remembered for its ungracious tone towards the winner, and Becerra argues that Staunton's organising duties kept him from playing at his usual strength.[^s2][^s5]

## Significance

The tournament was only a moderate success at the time, but historians regard it as a watershed: it showed that a multinational competition could be run and paved the way for the international tournaments that followed.[^s2] Its weaknesses were also instructive. With unseeded pairings drawn by lot, strong players could meet early, and a first-round match of only three games was a small sample on which to eliminate a master; Kieseritzky and Löwenthal both went out at that stage.[^s1][^s2] Anderssen's victory also ended Staunton's claim to be the leading player of the day.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

London 1851 is an early instance of a `chess-tournament` and a landmark of the `romantic-era`. It was `wonBy` Adolf Anderssen and `organizedBy` Howard Staunton, who also `playedIn` it, as did Wyvill and Kieseritzky.[^s1][^s3] The Immortal Game, Anderssen's famous casual win over Kieseritzky in London that year, was not a tournament game and has its own node. Hastings 1895 is one of the later international tournaments for which London 1851 is credited with paving the way.[^s2]

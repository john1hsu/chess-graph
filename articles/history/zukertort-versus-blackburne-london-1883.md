# Zukertort versus Blackburne, London 1883

Zukertort–Blackburne is the best-known game of the London international tournament of 1883, played on 5 May 1883 and won by Johannes Zukertort with the white pieces in 33 moves.[^s1] ChessBase places it in the sixth round and repeats the long-standing description of it as Zukertort's "immortal game", praising its attack and its two deflecting sacrifices.[^s2] It is a fixture of anthologies because the finish is spectacular and yet grows out of a calm, positional opening.[^s1][^s2]

## Setting

London 1883 was a double round-robin of fourteen masters, with drawn games replayed; Zukertort won it three points ahead of Wilhelm Steinitz, the high point of his career.[^s2][^s3] Blackburne, nicknamed "the Black Death", finished third.[^s4] The two men knew each other's play well: Zukertort had beaten Blackburne decisively in a match two years earlier, a result that led to a bitter press quarrel.[^s4]

## Record and publication

The score was in print almost at once. Edward Winter lists notes by Leopold Hoffer and Zukertort in the *London Evening Standard* of 7 May 1883, two days after the game, followed during that summer by commentary from C.E. Ranken, from Steinitz and from the German press; the official tournament book carries two sets of notes, Zukertort's own and J.I. Minchin's, and Blackburne later annotated the game himself.[^s1] The score below follows Winter's page.[^s1]

## How the game unfolds

Both sides develop a bishop on the long diagonal, and the opening looks like a slow positional struggle. Zukertort exchanges his queen's knight for Black's dark-squared bishop on d6, leaving Black without the piece that best guards the dark squares around his king.[^s1] White then gains space in the centre with e4–e5 and turns the f-pawn into a battering ram: f4, f5 and the capture on g6 strip the black king, while the pawn that reaches h7 forces Black's king into the corner.[^s1] Black's counterplay is real; his rooks double on the c-file and invade on c2, so White has to keep forcing matters.[^s1]

The key position arises after 27.d5+ e5, shown in the `fen` field. The d-pawn check has cut the Bb7 off from White's king, and White's own b2-bishop is blocked only by the pawn on e5. Zukertort now plays 28.Qb4, putting his queen where Black's queen on e7 can capture it, and follows with 29.Rf8+, giving up the rook as well.[^s1] Each offer pulls a black piece away from the dark squares; after 30.Qxe4+ and 31.Bxe5+ the long diagonal is open and the bishop and queen finish together, winning Black's queen at the end.[^s1][^s2]

## Was the finish best?

Winter's page is devoted largely to a point the anthologies missed. After 30...Kg7 White has a quicker win, 31.Rg8+, forcing mate in seven, first published by Edgard Tchélébi in the *British Chess Magazine* of March 1957.[^s1] Winter notes that later writers still gave the game move 31.Bxe5+ double exclamation marks, and that some modern books and biographies say nothing of Tchélébi's discovery, though software confirms it at once.[^s1] The game remains a model attack; the lesson is that even celebrated combinations deserve re-checking.

## Lessons for the club player

The game shows how a quiet bishop can decide an attack once the pawns in front of it move: the b2-bishop does little for twenty-seven moves and then delivers the decisive blows. It also shows why trading off the opponent's bishop of the colour around his king matters long before any sacrifice, and how a pawn storm aims to open lines rather than to win material. When a sacrifice seems to fail to a simple capture, check what that capture costs the defender in coordination.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The game was **played in** **London 1883** and is **played by** **Johannes Zukertort** and **Joseph Henry Blackburne**.[^s1][^s2] It sits under the **romantic era** of attacking chess, in the period just before the first world championship match.[^s2] It **exemplifies** **deflection**, since both sacrifices lure defenders away from key squares, and is related to the **combination** and **sacrifice** in general and to Anderssen's **Immortal Game**, whose nickname it echoes.[^s2]

# Chaturanga

Chaturanga is the Indian war game that historians treat as the earliest precursor of modern chess. The word is Sanskrit for a battle formation mentioned in the *Mahabharata*, and the game was flourishing in northwestern India by the 7th century CE.[^s1] It already had the two features that define every later member of the chess family: pieces with different powers, and a result decided by what happens to the king.[^s2]

## Dating and evidence

Britannica finds no credible evidence of a game approaching chess before the 6th century CE, and treats older pieces found across Asia as belonging to more distantly related board games that often used dice.[^s2] Other historians place the game's beginnings in northern India during or shortly before the 5th century CE, and treat a passage in Bānabhatta's *Harshacharita*, a biography of King Harsha, as the conventional starting point of the literary record.[^s4] H. J. R. Murray's *A History of Chess* (1913), still the standard reference, put the invention between 455 and 543 CE.[^s5] Claims of much older Indian chess, such as pieces from the Indus Valley site of Lothal, have never been confirmed.[^s5]

No Sanskrit text sets out the complete rules. Modern reconstructions rely mainly on later Muslim sources describing shatranj, so details differ between historians.[^s6]

## The board and the army

The name means "four-limbed", after the four arms of an Indian army: foot soldiers, cavalry, elephants and chariots, led by a king and his minister.[^s4] The game was played on an uncheckered 8×8 board, the *ashtāpada*, with the aim of capturing the enemy king.[^s4] Britannica notes it was perhaps played with dice on its 64 squares.[^s2]

In the reconstruction of Murray and Harry Golombek the pieces moved as follows:[^s6]

- **Raja (king):** one square in any direction.[^s6]
- **Mantri (minister):** one square diagonally, the ancestor of the queen.[^s6][^s2]
- **Gaja (elephant):** a leap of exactly two squares diagonally, the ancestor of the bishop.[^s6][^s2]
- **Ashva (horse):** exactly like the modern knight.[^s6]
- **Ratha (chariot):** exactly like the modern rook.[^s6]
- **Padati (foot soldier):** one square forward, capturing one square diagonally forward, with no double step.[^s6]

A foot soldier reaching the last rank became a minister, and, in Murray's account, a player could also win by stalemating the opponent or by stripping the enemy king of all its other pieces (a "bare king").[^s6] For a modern player the striking point is how slow the game was: only the rook and knight would feel familiar, while the long-range queen and bishop did not yet exist.

## Transmission to Persia

From India the game moved west into the Sasanian Empire, where it became *chatrang*; Middle Persian texts list it among the accomplishments expected of nobles.[^s4] A Middle Persian story set in the reign of Khusrau I (531–579) tells of Indian envoys presenting the game as a challenge that the sage Wuzurgmihr solved, though its historicity has been questioned.[^s3] The Persian word *shatranj* was probably derived from *chaturanga*.[^s3] After about 600 CE the Indian game may have gradually turned into shatranj, which added the counsellor (*firzān*) and allowed a win by capturing the king or reducing the opponent to a bare king.[^s2]

The weak counsellor and the jumping elephant survived in Europe until roughly 1475, when the new rules turned them into the modern queen and bishop.[^s2]

## Common misconceptions

A popular nineteenth-century theory held that chaturanga was originally a four-player dice game, older than the two-player form. Murray rejected it, and in a debate published by Edward Winter the historian Hugh Myers pointed out that *chaturanga* first meant an army, not a game.[^s5] Myers argued that Murray's conclusions should be refuted with evidence, not ignored.[^s5]

## Connections

Chaturanga sits at the root of the **history of chess** and is the oldest well-attested member of the **chess variants** family.[^s1] It leads directly to **shatranj**, the Persian and Arabic form through which chess reached Europe.[^s2] Its last-rank rule anticipates **pawn promotion**, though only to the weak minister, and its treatment of **stalemate** as a win shows that this rule changed over the centuries.[^s6] The disputed **four-handed chaturanga** theory is a contrasting account of the **origins of chess**.[^s5]

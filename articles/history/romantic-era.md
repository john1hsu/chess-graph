# Romantic era

The **Romantic era** (or Romantic school) is the name given to the style that dominated master chess in the mid-to-late nineteenth century.[^s1] Its players mobilised their pieces as fast as possible and went straight for the enemy king, offering pawns in the opening as gambits to open lines, and their games often ended in checkmating attacks crowned by startling sacrifices.[^s1] Britannica calls Adolf Anderssen the greatest of the Romantics.[^s1][^s2] The era was not a formal movement with a manifesto; the label describes a shared taste for initiative and brilliance that later masters, led by Wilhelm Steinitz, replaced with a positional theory of the game.[^s1][^s4]

## Roots and main ideas

The Romantic approach grew out of the eighteenth-century Modena school of Ercole del Rio, Giambattista Lolli and Domenico Ponziani. They argued against Philidor that he overrated pawns and underrated direct attack on the king, and pointed out that quick mobilisation of the pieces could win in fewer than 20 moves.[^s1] Nineteenth-century masters took that lesson to its limit. The typical Romantic game opened the centre early, sacrificed a pawn or more for development, and aimed every piece at the king.[^s1] Romantic players gave up pawns and pieces freely to attack. Later writers contrasted this with a modern style that clings to every pawn.[^s5]

The openings of the period reflect this. Steinitz's early games, a fair sample of top-level play in the 1860s, are full of the King's Gambit, the Evans Gambit, the Giuoco Piano and Vienna Game lines with an early f4, and king hunts across the whole board.[^s5] Defence was a secondary skill. Britannica credits Steinitz, not any Romantic, with being the first master of defensive play.[^s1]

## Anderssen and Morphy

Anderssen, a mathematics teacher from Breslau, won the London tournament of 1851, the first modern international tournament, and was then regarded as the unofficial champion; his style relied on combinations meant to force an immediate decision.[^s2] During that event he played his casual "Immortal Game" against Lionel Kieseritzky on 21 June 1851, giving up both rooks before delivering mate.[^s6] Later analysis, notably by Robert Hübner, found many errors in it, yet the game still stands as the emblem of chess Romanticism.[^s6]

Paul Morphy, born in New Orleans in 1837, won the First American Chess Congress in 1857. On his European tour of 1858–59 he beat the leading players, Howard Staunton apart, since Staunton avoided a match.[^s1][^s3] Morphy calculated no better than his rivals, but he understood better when and how to attack. He saw that a lead in development counts for most in open positions, and he made seizing the initiative his first priority.[^s1] He beat Anderssen 7–2 in 1858. Asked why he had not sacrificed brilliantly, Anderssen is said to have replied that Morphy would not let him.[^s1][^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame describes Morphy as the first player to show the value of long-term development over premature attacks and traps. His spectacular sacrifices kept him tied to the Romantic tradition.[^s3] He stopped playing serious chess after 1859.[^s3]

## A short example

A small sign of the period's appetite for risk is the counter-gambit **1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 f5**, which Steinitz played as Black in his early, romantic years. Black meets a quiet bishop development by attacking at once, at the cost of weakening his own king. ChessBase marks it as dubious.[^s5]

## Decline

Once Morphy's games were widely studied, a development lead became much harder to obtain against a prepared opponent.[^s1] Steinitz himself began as a tactical, Morphy-style player. ChessBase dates his change of style to the Vienna tournament of 1873, which he won with a new, more cautious strategy, beating Anderssen twice.[^s5] He then argued that the forces are balanced so the natural result is a draw, that an attack is justified only when the balance has been upset, and that small advantages accumulate into decisive ones.[^s1] In matches he beat the leading Romantics, among them Anderssen, Blackburne, Zukertort and Chigorin. He became the first official world champion in 1886.[^s1][^s4] He had beaten Anderssen 8–6 in 1866, while still playing in the old style himself.[^s5]

## Pitfalls for the modern reader

Romantic games make excellent tactical training, but they can mislead. Many famous brilliancies succeeded because defenders accepted every sacrifice and neglected development, and engines and later annotators find holes in many of them.[^s6] The lasting lesson is Morphy's, not the gambits themselves: develop quickly, open lines when you are ahead in development, and attack only when the position justifies it.[^s1]

## Connections

The Romantic era is a **part of** the **history of chess**, which sits between the Modena school that **influenced** it and the positional revolution of Wilhelm Steinitz, whose approach **contrasts with** it.[^s1] It was **championed by** Adolf Anderssen and Paul Morphy, and it is **exemplified by** the Immortal Game.[^s2][^s3][^s6] It is related to the **combination** and the **sacrifice**, its favourite weapons, and to the **Evans Gambit**, one of its signature openings.[^s5] It also links to **hypermodernism**, the next great challenge to accepted doctrine, and to the **world chess championship** that began with Steinitz's 1886 title.[^s4]

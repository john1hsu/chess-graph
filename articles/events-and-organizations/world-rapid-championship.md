# World Rapid Championship

The World Rapid Championship is the world title event for rapid chess, organised by FIDE and today played every December in the same venue and week as the World Blitz Championship. It is a separate title from the classical world championship: there is no challenger match, and the champion is simply the winner of that year's tournament. For the 2025 edition in Doha the open rapid was held on 26–28 December, before the blitz.[^s1]

## History

FIDE's own history of the event traces it to Mazatlán, Mexico, in 1988, where 61 players contested a 13-round Swiss followed by a knockout of the top eight; Anatoly Karpov and Viktor Gavrikov drew their final 5–5 and Karpov was declared champion on additional criteria.[^s2] Rapid titles were then fragmented for years: a FIDE-supported World Cup in Cannes in 2001 and a Prague knockout in 2002 were followed by the Cap d'Agde event of 2003, which FIDE's page counts as a revival of the championship and lists Viswanathan Anand as its winner, while the Association of Chess Professionals ran a parallel rapid cup in Odessa from 2007 to 2010.[^s2]

The modern annual series began in Astana, Kazakhstan, in July 2012. That edition was a 16-player round robin at 15 minutes plus 10 seconds per move; Sergey Karjakin won with 11½/15, a point ahead of Magnus Carlsen, with Veselin Topalov and Shakhriyar Mamedyarov sharing third.[^s5] FIDE's list of later champions runs Mamedyarov (2013), Carlsen (2014 and 2015), Vassily Ivanchuk (2016), Anand (2017, in Riyadh) and Daniil Dubov (2018).[^s2] Carlsen has been the dominant figure: his win in Doha in 2025 was his sixth rapid title, after 2014, 2015, 2019, 2022 and 2023.[^s3]

## Format

The current event is an open Swiss rather than an invitational. Under the 2025 regulations, players qualified with a rating of at least 2550 on any FIDE list during the year, as a 2025 national champion, or by nomination of the FIDE President or organiser.[^s1] The open section is 13 rounds at 15 minutes plus 10 seconds per move from move one, with no default time for late arrival.[^s1] Tied scores are separated by Buchholz Cut 1, Buchholz, average rating of opponents cut 1, direct encounter and lots, but the title itself is settled by a play-off when first place is shared: two players meet in a two-game 3+2 blitz match and then a sudden-death game in which a draw counts as a win for Black; three or more tied players play a sudden-death knockout.[^s1] Level 1 fair-play protection measures apply, and the regulations set a dress code and fines for breaches of player duties.[^s1]

A women's World Rapid runs alongside, over 11 rounds; FIDE kept both rapid formats unchanged for 2025 while reworking the blitz knockout.[^s4] The 2025 prize fund for the whole event was €1 million, with €350,000 for the open rapid and €70,000 for its winner.[^s4][^s1]

## Recent results

In New York in December 2024 the 18-year-old Volodar Murzin won the open with an unbeaten 10/13 from a field of 180, ahead of Alexander Grischuk and Ian Nepomniachtchi on 9½; Humpy Koneru won the women's title with 8½/11, her second.[^s6] As of November 2026 the reigning champions are Carlsen, who scored 10½/13 in Doha to finish a full point clear of Vladislav Artemiev and Arjun Erigaisi, and Aleksandra Goryachkina, who won the women's title in a blitz play-off against Zhu Jiner.[^s3]

## What it rewards

Thirteen rapid games in three days punish long thinks and favour players who convert small edges quickly and stay steady when tired. The Swiss format means that a single early loss can be recovered from, but the tie-break order makes the strength of one's opponents matter, and the blitz play-off adds a different skill again.[^s1] Murzin's unbeaten run in 2024 shows that a solid, low-risk approach can beat a field full of stronger-rated players.[^s6]

## Connections

The championship is the flagship event of rapid chess and is sanctioned by FIDE, which sets its regulations each year.[^s1] It is a chess tournament run on the Swiss system with its own tie-break order, and it shares dates, venue and fair-play rules with the World Blitz Championship.[^s1][^s4] Its winners include Karpov (1988), Anand (2003, 2017), Karjakin (2012) and Carlsen, the most frequent champion.[^s2][^s5][^s3] It is distinct from the classical World Chess Championship, which is decided by a title match.[^s2]

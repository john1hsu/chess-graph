# US Chess

US Chess, formally the United States Chess Federation (USCF), is the national governing body for chess in the United States and the country's official federation within FIDE.[^s2] It was founded in 1939 through the merger of the American Chess Federation and the National Chess Federation, which together had roughly a thousand members.[^s1][^s4] Since 2014 it has been a 501(c)(3) non-profit, with the mission to empower people, enrich lives and enhance communities through chess.[^s3][^s4]

## What US Chess does

For an American club player, US Chess is the body behind almost every over-the-board tournament. It sanctions thousands of tournaments a year, oversees more than half a million rated games annually, and runs national championships for groups from elementary-school pupils to seniors.[^s1] Its services include membership, ratings and the organisation of events, among them some of the largest chess competitions in the world, alongside scholastic programmes, a Safe Play programme and support for state chapters.[^s2] As FIDE's member federation it represents the United States internationally and supports American participation in official FIDE championships.[^s1][^s2]

## Ratings

US Chess adopted Arpad Elo's method for adjusting ratings in 1960; Elo built his scale on the one the federation had used before, calibrated to an average player at a U.S. Open.[^s7] Statisticians Mark Glickman and Albyn Jones described the system in use in their survey, including a K-factor that fell from 32 to 24 to 16 as ratings rose past 2100 and 2400, and proposed revisions making K depend on how uncertain a player's rating is and on the number of games played.[^s7] The federation keeps its own rating list for the games it sanctions, which is distinct from the international list that FIDE maintains for its members.[^s1][^s2]

## The rulebook

US Chess publishes its own *Official Rules of Chess*, now in a 7th edition edited by Tim Just; the free online chapters cover the basic rules, tournament procedure, correspondence, online and blitz play, and were updated for 2026.[^s6] Under Rule 1B, US Chess play is governed by these rules, and FIDE rules may be used only when announced in advance.[^s5] Some differences matter in practice. The standard penalty is two minutes added to the opponent's clock, and directors may warn rather than penalise young or inexperienced players.[^s5] Delay clocks are standard for delay time controls, with 5 seconds the default delay for regular play.[^s5] The touch-move rule (Rule 10) requires a player who deliberately touches a piece to move it if it can legally move, while accidental contact does not count.[^s5]

## Membership and headquarters

Membership grew with American chess booms. Official tracking began in 1952 with 1,127 members; Bobby Fischer's world-championship run lifted it from 30,884 in 1972 to 59,779 in 1974; it fell to 51,777 in 2021 when play moved online, then passed 100,000 for the first time on 19 February 2023.[^s4] In September 2025 the federation reported more than 115,000 members.[^s2] The headquarters moved from New Windsor, New York, to Crossville, Tennessee, in 2003; in January 2022 the Executive Board voted 8–0 to relocate to the St. Louis area, citing recruitment difficulties and the city's chess culture.[^s3] As of its 2025 job posting, US Chess was headquartered in St. Louis, with an Executive Board setting policy and an Executive Director, reporting to the Board, running the staff.[^s2]

## Pitfalls

A common confusion is to treat a US Chess rating and a FIDE rating as interchangeable, or to assume US events follow the FIDE Laws. Unless an organiser announces otherwise, a US Chess event runs under the US Chess rulebook, so claims about clocks, illegal moves or penalties should be checked against that book.[^s5] Membership figures and staffing change often, so any count should carry a date.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

US Chess is one of the **chess organizations** and is **a member of FIDE**, representing the United States there.[^s1][^s2] It adopted the **Elo rating system** in 1960, and its rulebook has its own version of the **touch-move** rule.[^s5][^s7] The **Bobby Fischer** boom of 1972 is the clearest example of how a single player drove its growth.[^s4] Its move to St. Louis placed it near the **Saint Louis Chess Club**, which has hosted the **U.S. Championship** for about a decade.[^s3]

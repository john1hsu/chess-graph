# Women's World Chess Championship

The Women's World Chess Championship decides the women's world champion, a title that runs in parallel with the open **World Chess Championship**. It began with a tournament in 1927, which Vera Menchik won to become the first holder.[^s1][^s2] Women can and do enter the open cycle as well; this title is a separate, FIDE-run championship with its own qualifying events, its own Candidates and its own title match.[^s4][^s6]

## History

**The Menchik era (1927–1944).** Menchik won the inaugural championship in 1927 and kept the title until her death in 1944. She won it eight times, more than anyone else, and at one point won at least 59 consecutive games in championship tournaments. Pre-war titles were mostly decided by tournaments, but in 1937 she also beat Sonja Graf in an official title match.[^s2][^s1] FIDE's women's commission notes that her reign is the longest of any women's champion, and among all world champions only Emanuel Lasker held a title longer.[^s2]

**Restart and the match era.** Menchik's death left the title vacant. FIDE restarted it with a tournament in Moscow in the winter of 1949–50, run alongside a men's event to find a successor to Alexander Alekhine, who had died in 1946 as open champion. Lyudmila Rudenko won with nine wins, five draws and one loss.[^s3] From the 1950s the title usually passed through a Candidates event and then a match against the champion. Rudenko lost it to Elisaveta Bykova in 1953, and Bykova became the first woman to regain the title after losing it.[^s3][^s1] Georgia then supplied two long reigns: Nona Gaprindashvili (1962–1978) and Maia Chiburdanidze (1978–1991), who won the title young and defended it four times.[^s1]

**Knockouts and the return to matches.** Xie Jun became the first Chinese champion in 1991. Around 2000 FIDE began deciding the title with large knockout events, and several champions won through tiebreaks. Susan Polgar lost the title in 1999 by forfeit after a dispute over match conditions. Hou Yifan won it at 16 in 2010, the youngest champion so far, and regained it twice. Anna Ushenina (2012), Mariya Muzychuk (2015) and Tan Zhongyi (2017) all took the title in knockouts.[^s1] Ju Wenjun beat Tan in a 2018 match and has held the title since. She defended it in 2020 against Aleksandra Goryachkina and in 2023 against Lei Tingjie.[^s1][^s5] In April 2025 she beat Tan again, 6½–2½, decided by a draw in game 9, for her fifth title.[^s5]

## How the title match works

FIDE's regulations for the 2025 match give the current format. The match is 12 classical games, and the first player to reach 6½ points wins. The time control is 90 minutes for 40 moves, then 30 minutes for the rest of the game, with a 30-second increment from move 1.[^s4] At 6–6 the title goes to tiebreaks. First comes a four-game rapid match at 15 minutes plus 10 seconds. If that is level, two-game playoffs at 10+5 and then 3+2 follow, and finally single blitz games until one is decisive.[^s4] The challenger is the winner of the previous Women's Candidates Tournament. The recommended prize fund was €500,000, split 60–40, or 55–45 if tiebreaks decide the title.[^s4]

## Current cycle

As of November 2026, Ju Wenjun of China is the reigning champion. R. Vaishali of India won the 2026 Women's Candidates outright with 8½/14 in April 2026, earning the right to challenge her.[^s6][^s5] The women's cycle mirrors the open one: qualifiers from the Women's Grand Prix, Women's World Cup and Women's Grand Swiss met in an eight-player, 14-round double round robin in Cyprus, and its winner meets the champion.[^s7]

## Pitfalls in reading the record

Title counts depend on how you count. FIDE credits Menchik with eight championship wins across one continuous reign.[^s2] Ju's "fifth title" counts the 2018 knockout she won as defending champion as a separate title.[^s5][^s1] Champions from the knockout era did not always lose the crown at the board. Polgar forfeited, and Hou Yifan declined to defend in 2015 and again in 2017.[^s1] When comparing champions, keep the format of each era in mind. A 64-player knockout with rapid tiebreaks tests different qualities than a long match against a single opponent prepared for months.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

The championship is a branch of the **World Chess Championship** tradition, run and sanctioned by **FIDE**. Its games are played under the **FIDE Laws of Chess**, with match rules set by FIDE regulations.[^s4] The challenger comes from the women's section of the **Candidates Tournament**.[^s6] Vera Menchik, the first champion, also gives her name to the trophy for the women's **Chess Olympiad**, the Vera Menchik Cup.[^s2] The current holder, Ju Wenjun, is recorded as its most recent winner, and Hou Yifan, the youngest champion, is a related player node.[^s1][^s5]

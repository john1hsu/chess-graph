# Norway Chess

Norway Chess is an annual invitational super-tournament that grew out of an idea in 2012 and was first held in 2013, with the stated aim of bringing the world's best players together in Norway.[^s1] It is now one of the fixed points of the elite calendar, and since 2024 it has had a women's counterpart, Norway Chess Women, played alongside it.[^s1] For a club player it is above all the best-known experiment in tournament scoring: it was built to reward winning and to make sure that, in its own words, every game produces a winner.[^s2]

## Format and scoring

Each event has six players in a double round robin of ten rounds, so everyone meets everyone twice, once with each colour.[^s2] The classical time control is two hours per player with no increment, with a 10-second increment per move added only after move 40.[^s2] Points are unusual: a classical win is worth 3 points and a loss 0. A drawn classical game is followed at once by an Armageddon game, in which White has 10 minutes and must win, while Black has 7 minutes and wins the tie on a draw; the Armageddon winner takes 1½ points and the loser 1.[^s2][^s5]

Armageddon arrived in 2019, the same year the classical time control was changed.[^s1][^s3] The organisers' own count shows how balanced the 10-versus-7 split with draw odds has been in practice: across all editions to 2025, 194 Armageddon games had been played, with 101 won by White and 93 by Black.[^s3] The scoring makes a classical win worth twice an Armageddon win, so a player behind in the table often has to take risks with the long time control rather than wait for the quick game.[^s2]

## Venues and presentation

Early editions moved around the Stavanger region, using venues such as the University of Stavanger, Utstein Monastery and Stavanger Concert Hall, and the 13th edition in 2025 was still played in Stavanger.[^s1][^s5] The 2026 edition, from 25 May to 5 June, was held in Oslo.[^s4][^s6] The event is also known for its presentation: a "confessional booth" next to the playing hall, introduced in 2015, lets players record their thoughts on camera during the game without contact with anyone outside.[^s2]

## Equal events for women

Norway Chess Women began in 2024, billed by the organisers as the world's first equal super-tournament for women, and it returned on the same terms in 2025.[^s1] FIDE's reports describe both events as six-player double round robins with equal prize funds; in 2026 each fund was 1,690,000 NOK, about $182,000.[^s4][^s6] Ju Wenjun won the first women's edition, Anna Muzychuk won the 2025 edition unbeaten after finishing second the year before, and Bibisara Assaubayeva won in 2026 with a round to spare on 16½ points.[^s7][^s5][^s4]

## Winners

Magnus Carlsen, the home favourite, won his seventh Norway Chess title in 2025, drawing a lost position in the final round with seconds left while his rivals Fabiano Caruana and Gukesh Dommaraju both lost.[^s5][^s7] Other winners include Caruana in 2018 and Hikaru Nakamura in 2023.[^s8][^s7] In 2026 Praggnanandhaa Rameshbabu took the open title with 18 points, ahead of Wesley So on 17 and Alireza Firouzja on 15½; Carlsen was fourth and world champion Gukesh last.[^s4] Praggnanandhaa had been bottom of the table after losing to So in round six, then won his last four classical games, finishing with a final-round classical win over Vincent Keymer.[^s6][^s4] These results are as of November 2026.

## Pitfalls for readers

Standings from Norway Chess cannot be compared directly with a normal round robin: a score of 18 points from ten games means nothing under 1-½-0 scoring, and a player's classical record can look very different from their place in the table.[^s2][^s4] The scoring also changed in 2019, so tables from the first editions use a different system from later ones.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Norway Chess is an **instance of** the invitational **chess tournament**, organised as a **round robin** whose drawn games are settled by an **Armageddon** game, a form of **tie-break** applied game by game.[^s2] It is **won by** **Magnus Carlsen** a record seven times, by **Fabiano Caruana**, **Hikaru Nakamura** and, in 2026, **Praggnanandhaa Rameshbabu**, and its women's event was first **won by** **Ju Wenjun**.[^s7][^s8][^s4] With the **Tata Steel Chess Tournament** it is one of the annual invitationals that world champions such as **Gukesh Dommaraju** play outside the official FIDE cycle.[^s4]

# Chess organizations

Organised chess is run by a layered set of bodies rather than a single authority. At the top sits FIDE, a federation whose voting members are national federations; around it is a ring of "affiliated organisations" that the FIDE General Assembly admits without giving them a vote. The FIDE Charter (Article 14) sorts these into three kinds: organisations grouping member federations, associations representing regions or transnational territories, and associations representing people who share an interest in a specific chess activity.[^s1] Knowing which body is responsible for what explains why a correspondence title, a composition title and an over-the-board title come from different places.

## The layers

**National federations** are the base: they hold the vote at the FIDE General Assembly, register players and organise national championships. A body that covers only part of a country cannot become a FIDE affiliated organisation, though since November 2023 such a territory can be recognised as an "Affiliated Member" if its national federation consents, it has a geographical or historical basis for its identity and it has an active chess community.[^s1] Affiliated Members may send players to events such as the Olympiad where the event rules allow it, but still have no vote.[^s1]

**Continental and regional bodies** group federations. As of November 2026 FIDE's directory lists the four continental bodies (the African Chess Confederation, the Asian Chess Federation, the Confederation of Chess for Americas and the European Chess Union) among its affiliated organisations, alongside regional and cultural groupings such as the Arab Chess Federation, the ASEAN Chess Confederation, the Commonwealth Chess Association, the Francophone association AIDEF, the Iberoamerican federation FIBDA, the Mediterranean and MITROPA associations, the Oceania Chess Confederation and the Small Nations Chess Association.[^s2] The European Chess Union, founded in Graz in 1985, illustrates the role: it groups 54 national federations and runs the European Individual Championships, the European Club Cup and youth and team events.[^s3]

**Specialist organisations** cover parts of chess that FIDE does not run directly. The directory's list includes the International Correspondence Chess Federation (ICCF), the World Federation for Chess Composition (WFCC), the International Computer Games Association (ICGA), two school-chess bodies (ISCF and ISCU), and three bodies for players with disabilities: the International Braille Chess Association (IBCA), the International Chess Committee of the Deaf (ICCD) and the International Physically Disabled Chess Association (IPCA).[^s2] In total the directory listed 26 affiliated organisations when checked in November 2026.[^s2]

## Correspondence chess as an example

The ICCF shows how a specialist body works in practice. Its lineage runs from the Internationaler Fernschachbund (IFSB) of 1928, the first international correspondence league, through the International Correspondence Chess Association of 1945, to the ICCF in its present form from 1951.[^s4] Like FIDE it is a federation of national member organisations, and it runs its own individual and team championships, title-norm tournaments and promotion events, played both by post and on its webserver.[^s4] Its titles, championships and ratings are its own, recognised by FIDE but separate from over-the-board ones.[^s4]

## Rival bodies

Affiliation is cooperative, but chess has also seen breakaway organisations. In 1993 the challenger Nigel Short and champion Garry Kasparov chose to play their world championship match under a new Professional Chess Association rather than FIDE; FIDE responded by stripping Kasparov of its title and staging its own title match, won by Anatoly Karpov.[^s5][^s6] Kasparov defended the PCA title against Viswanathan Anand in 1995, and the PCA disbanded in 1996.[^s5] The episode left two rival world-title lineages, one under FIDE and one outside it.

## Pitfalls

Two confusions are common. Affiliation does not mean subordination: affiliated organisations have their own officers, rules and titles, and take part in FIDE congresses without voting.[^s1] And a title's issuing body matters: an ICCF correspondence title or a WFCC composition title is a genuine international title, but not the FIDE over-the-board grandmaster title.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

This node is the umbrella class for chess's governing and organising bodies. Its central member is **FIDE**, and **US Chess** is an example of a national federation.[^s1] It has as parts the **European Chess Union** (a continental body), the **International Correspondence Chess Federation**, which governs **correspondence chess**, and the **World Federation for Chess Composition**.[^s2][^s3][^s4] The historical **Professional Chess Association** is included as the best-known rival body.[^s5] The titles these bodies award are surveyed under **chess titles**.[^s2]

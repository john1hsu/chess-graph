# Arbiter

An arbiter is the official in charge of the rules at a chess competition. Under the FIDE Laws of Chess the arbiter's first duty is simply to see that the Laws are observed, and Article 12 adds a broader brief: ensure fair play, act in the best interest of the competition, keep a good playing environment, make sure players are not disturbed, supervise the event's progress, and take special measures for disabled players and those needing medical attention.[^s1] The Laws deliberately leave room for judgement: their preface assumes arbiters bring competence, sound judgement and absolute objectivity, and cases the text does not regulate are settled by analogy.[^s1] In US Chess events the equivalent official is the tournament director (TD), who works under a different rulebook.[^s5]

## What the arbiter does

Article 12 asks the arbiter to watch the games, above all when players are short of time, to enforce the decisions they or their assistants make, and to penalise players where appropriate; assistants may be appointed precisely for crowded time scrambles.[^s1] If a game is disturbed from outside, the arbiter may compensate one or both players with extra time.[^s1] Mobile phones and other communication devices may not be used in the venue, or a contiguous area the arbiter designates, without the arbiter's permission.[^s1]

Penalties are chosen from a ladder that the Laws list in approximate order of severity: a warning; adding time to the opponent's clock; removing time from the offender's; raising the opponent's score for the game to the maximum; reducing the offender's score; declaring the game lost; a fine announced in advance; exclusion from one or more rounds; and expulsion from the competition.[^s1] This gives the arbiter proportionate options rather than an all-or-nothing choice.

## When the arbiter intervenes, and when not

FIDE arbiters act on what they see. If an arbiter observes an infringement, the Laws say they must not wait for a player's claim, and a touch-move breach, for example, is corrected on the spot.[^s1] The arbiter also calls a fallen flag when they observe it.[^s1] Equally important is what the arbiter must not do: they may not intervene except as the Laws provide, must not tell a player how many moves have been completed (except when a flag has fallen and the count matters), and should not tell a player that the opponent has moved or that they forgot to press the clock.[^s1] The Arbiters' Manual treats a question such as "how many moves until the time control?" as a request for advice that must go unanswered.[^s1] Spectators and players in other games who notice an irregularity may tell only the arbiter, not the players, and may be expelled for interfering.[^s1]

US Chess draws the line differently. Its official comparison notes that only players may call a fallen flag, that the director corrects observed illegal moves unless either player has under five minutes (and only on claim under a widely used variation), and that touch-move and most other violations require a claim by the opponent.[^s5] For a club player who moves between FIDE-rated and US Chess events, this is one of the most practical differences: a FIDE arbiter will step in unasked, while a TD generally waits to be called.[^s5]

## Titles and qualification

FIDE recognises two arbiter titles, FIDE Arbiter (FA) and International Arbiter (IA), held for life; National Arbiters are appointed by federations and registered by FIDE's Arbiters' Commission (ARB).[^s2][^s3] The IA title can only go to someone who already holds the FA, and active arbiters are graded into categories A to D, with A and B reserved for International Arbiters.[^s3] In the version of the title regulations in force until 28 February 2026, an FA needed a FIDE Arbiters' Seminar with an exam score of at least 80% plus norms from rated events, four norms in all, and had to be at least 19 when applying; an IA needed five further norms, conversational English and, from 2024, an International Arbiters' Certification Seminar, with a minimum age of 21.[^s2] A revision effective 1 March 2026 kept norms and seminars but tightened the process: an IA norm must now be signed by an IA, only one norm per festival counts, and a candidate needs either time in the lower title or extra tournaments before applying.[^s4] Arbiters at FIDE-rated events must also be registered and pay FIDE's fee, or the event may go unrated.[^s2]

## Practical advice for players

- Raise any dispute with the arbiter calmly and promptly; under FIDE rules you may ask for a ruling, but do not expect advice about the game or the clock.[^s1]
- Check the event's rules: whether the event follows FIDE or US Chess rules decides whether the official calls flags and illegal moves unprompted.[^s5]
- Leave your phone switched off or outside, as the arbiter controls device use in the venue.[^s1]

## Connections

The arbiter's powers are **governed by** the **FIDE Laws of Chess**, chiefly Article 12, and the official is part of every rated **chess tournament**.[^s1] Arbiter titles are **sanctioned by** **FIDE** through its Arbiters' Commission, alongside FIDE's other **chess titles**.[^s2][^s3] In practice the arbiter enforces rules such as **touch-move** and the device and conduct rules behind the **fair-play regulations**.[^s1] The role is **related** to the tournament director of **US Chess**, whose rules leave more to players' claims.[^s5]

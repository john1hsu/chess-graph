# FIDE World Cup

The FIDE World Cup is a mass knockout tournament run by FIDE as one of the qualifying routes to the Candidates Tournament, and through it to a match for the world title. Its modern form dates from the 2005 edition in Khanty-Mansiysk, Russia, where 128 players met in knockout matches between late November and 18 December; Levon Aronian won it by drawing both regular games of the final against Ruslan Ponomariov and then winning the rapid tiebreak 2–0.[^s4] ChessBase described that event as a qualifier for the Candidates stage of the world championship, a role the World Cup has kept since.[^s4]

## Format

The 2025 edition, held in Goa, India, shows the current design. It had 206 players and eight knockout rounds, every round a two-game match.[^s1] Seeds 51 to 206 played in round 1, while the top 50 seeds had a bye into round 2; the eighth round consisted of the final and a match for third place.[^s1] Places were filled through nine categories: the reigning world champion, leading finishers of the 2023 World Cup, the women's world champion, the world junior champion, 80 continental qualifiers, 13 rating qualifiers from the June 2025 list, one player from each of 100 federations based on the 2024 Olympiad, and a few FIDE President and organiser nominees.[^s1]

Classical games were played at 90 minutes for 40 moves plus 30 minutes for the rest of the game, with 30 seconds added per move from move one.[^s1] If a match stood level after two classical games, the players went through two rapid games at 15+10, two more at 10+10, two blitz games at 5+3, two at 3+2, and finally a single Armageddon game in which Black bids time and a draw sends Black through.[^s1] Players could not agree a draw before Black's 30th move.[^s1] The 2025 prize fund was US$2 million, with US$120,000 for the winner.[^s1]

## Why it matters for the title cycle

The World Cup is the most open path to the Candidates: a strong grandmaster outside the elite can enter through a continental championship and, by surviving seven or eight short matches, reach the eight-player event. Under the 2025 regulations the top three finishers qualified for the 2026 Candidates, with any place freed by a player already qualified reallocated under the Candidates rules.[^s1] In 2023 at Baku the top three were Magnus Carlsen, R Praggnanandhaa and Fabiano Caruana; FIDE's report noted that the fourth-placed Nijat Abasov would take Carlsen's place if he declined, which is what the reallocation rule is for.[^s3]

## Playing a knockout

Two-game matches reward a different skill set from round robins. A single loss with White can end a campaign, so players often aim for a safe draw in one game and press in the other, and the rapid and blitz tiebreaks make speed and nerves part of the qualification process. The 2023 and 2025 finals both show this: in Baku the two classical games were drawn (the second agreed by move 22) and Carlsen won the rapid tiebreak, his first World Cup title.[^s3] In Goa the final between Javokhir Sindarov and Wei Yi was also drawn in classical play; Sindarov won the tiebreak 1½–½ after Wei Yi blundered with 57.Rxd4?? in the second rapid game.[^s2] A club player preparing for any knockout can take the same lessons: prepare for the tiebreak format, and judge risk by the match situation rather than the single game.

## Recent results

As of November 2026 the reigning World Cup champion is Sindarov, who won the 2025 event aged 19.[^s5] Chess.com reported him as the youngest World Cup winner, and with the runner-up Wei Yi and the third-place Andrey Esipenko he qualified for the 2026 Candidates.[^s6] Esipenko took third place by beating Nodirbek Yakubboev 2–0 in classical games.[^s2] A separate Women's World Cup runs alongside the open event; Divya Deshmukh won its 2025 edition.[^s6]

## Connections

The World Cup is part of the world championship cycle and sanctioned by FIDE, and it feeds the Candidates Tournament through its top three places.[^s1] It is a special case of the chess tournament, a knockout rather than a round robin or Swiss, played under the FIDE Laws of Chess with event-specific tiebreak and draw rules.[^s1] Its winners include Aronian (2005), Carlsen (2023) and Sindarov (2025), and its women's counterpart connects it to the Women's World Chess Championship cycle.[^s4][^s3][^s6]

# Candidates Tournament

The Candidates Tournament is the last qualifying stage of the world championship cycle: FIDE's regulations state plainly that its winner qualifies for the next World Championship Match.[^s1] It replaced the era in which a champion chose a challenger by private negotiation, and for most of its history it has been the hardest event in chess to win, because every player in it has already beaten a strong field to get there.[^s2] For a club player it is also the clearest showcase of elite preparation, nerves and tournament strategy over three weeks.

## History

The first Candidates was held in Budapest in 1950 as a double round robin. David Bronstein and Isaac Boleslavsky tied for first with no tie-break rule in place, and Bronstein became challenger only after a play-off in Moscow that was itself level until he won the first decisive game.[^s2] Vasily Smyslov won the next two editions, in 1953 and in Amsterdam in 1956, and Mikhail Tal took the 1959 event in Yugoslavia, an eight-player quadruple round robin.[^s2] Curaçao 1962 used the same format and was won by Tigran Petrosian without a single loss; Tal withdrew after 21 rounds.[^s2]

From 1965 FIDE replaced the tournament with knockout matches, and Boris Spassky won the match series of 1965 and 1968.[^s2] The tournament form returned only in London in 2013, fifty-one years after Curaçao, with eight players in a double round robin.[^s2] That edition was decided on the number of wins: Magnus Carlsen and Vladimir Kramnik both lost in the last round and finished level, and Carlsen took first on the tie-break.[^s2] Later winners were Viswanathan Anand (2014, unbeaten on 8½/14), Sergey Karjakin (2016), Fabiano Caruana (2018), Ian Nepomniachtchi twice (Yekaterinburg 2021, an event halted by the pandemic after seven rounds and resumed thirteen months later, and Madrid 2022 with 9½/14) and Gukesh D (Toronto 2024), the youngest player ever to win it.[^s2]

## How it works today

For the 2026 cycle the Open Candidates had eight players in a 14-round double round robin, played in Cyprus between 27 March and 17 April 2026.[^s1] The places came from several routes: the winner of the 2024 FIDE Circuit, the top two of the 2025 Grand Swiss, the top three of the 2025 World Cup, the 2025 Circuit winner, and one place for the highest average rating over six monthly lists.[^s1] The time control was 120 minutes for 40 moves, then 30 minutes with a 30-second increment from move 41, and draws by agreement were barred before Black's 40th move.[^s1]

Rapid and blitz play-offs are used only to break a tie for first place, ending if necessary in a sudden-death game; other places are separated by Sonneborn-Berger, then number of wins, then the head-to-head result.[^s1] The 2026 regulations also set a minimum prize fund of €700,000 and paid each player €5,000 per half point, a design that rewards fighting play rather than safe draws.[^s1] In 2024 and 2026 a Women's Candidates ran alongside with the same eight-player, 14-round format, deciding the challenger for the women's title; Tan Zhongyi won it in 2024 with 9½/14.[^s2]

## The 2026 edition

Javokhir Sindarov of Uzbekistan clinched the 2026 Open Candidates with a round to spare, securing first place by drawing with Anish Giri in round 13.[^s3] He finished on 10/14, unbeaten with six wins and eight draws, 1½ points ahead of Giri, and that total is the highest of the double-round-robin era that began in 2013.[^s5][^s6] Caruana was third on 7½.[^s5] Sindarov thereby earned the title match against Gukesh D, scheduled for the end of 2026.[^s3] In the Women's Candidates, Vaishali Rameshbabu won outright on 8½/14 by beating Kateryna Lagno in the last round, and qualified to play Ju Wenjun for the women's world title.[^s4] These results are as of November 2026.

## Pitfalls for readers

"Candidates" refers to different formats in different decades: a round robin in 1950–62 and again since 2013, but knockout matches in between, from 1965 until the tournament returned in London.[^s2] So the matches that Bobby Fischer and Anatoly Karpov won on their way to the title were Candidates matches, not tournaments. Tie-break rules have also changed: in 1950 there were none, while in 2013 the number of wins decided first place.[^s2] Modern regulations instead use play-offs only for first place.[^s1]

## Connections

The Candidates is **part of** the **World Chess Championship** cycle and is **sanctioned by FIDE**, which writes its regulations.[^s1] Its games follow the **FIDE Laws of Chess**, with event-specific rules such as the 40-move limit on agreed draws.[^s1] Its winners include **Gukesh D** in 2024 and **Javokhir Sindarov** in 2026, and the match era produced challengers such as **Bobby Fischer** and **Anatoly Karpov**.[^s2][^s3] The parallel Women's Candidates feeds the **Women's World Chess Championship**.[^s4]

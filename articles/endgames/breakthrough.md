# Breakthrough

A breakthrough is a pawn sacrifice, or a short chain of them, that tears open a pawn front which looks locked and balanced, so that one pawn slips through as an unstoppable passer. Its lesson runs against a common intuition. Many players assume that when both sides have the same number of pawns on a wing, nobody can make progress there; Daniel Naroditsky calls this assumption false and notes that breakthroughs are easy to overlook precisely because players do not expect them.[^s4] Capablanca put the underlying rule in general form a century ago: wherever three or more pawns stand opposed to each other, one side or the other may be able to obtain a passed pawn.[^s1]

## Where it sits in endgame theory

The breakthrough belongs to the basic toolkit of pawn endings, but it is not confined to them. Dvoretsky's manual gives it its own section in the opening chapter on pawn endgames, between shouldering and the outside passed pawn, and the author's preface lists it among the terms introduced there because they matter in many other kinds of ending.[^s2] John Nunn's endings handbook reflects the same breadth: it has separate sections called "Breakthrough" for pawn endings, knight endings, same-coloured bishop endings and opposite-coloured bishop endings.[^s3] Davorin Kuljasevic presents it as the first of the basic principles of passed-pawn play and illustrates it with a same-coloured bishop ending from Navara–Bartel, Jerusalem 2015, and with a rook ending won by the same device.[^s5] Karsten Müller's endgame column returns to the theme repeatedly, stressing that a pawn majority is often the platform from which a breakthrough is organised.[^s6]

## The classic pattern

The textbook case is Capablanca's Example 24, where three pawns face three on adjacent files with the kings far away (diagram: White pawns a5, b5, c5; Black pawns a7, b7, c7). With White to move, the middle pawn goes first: 1.b6! If 1...axb6, then 2.c6 bxc6 3.a6 and the a-pawn runs home; if 1...cxb6 instead, 2.a6 bxa6 3.c6 achieves the same on the c-file.[^s1] The point of the sacrifice is arithmetic of files. The first pawn forces a capture, the second deflects the remaining guard, and the third has a clear road, while Black's extra pawns are still several moves from queening. Capablanca stresses that the decisive factor is that White's new passer is nearer to its promotion square.[^s1]

With Black to move the picture flips. Black plays 1...b6 himself, and after 2.cxb6 cxb6 3.axb6 axb6 only a blocked pair remains and the position is drawn: the defender has used his tempo to break the formation before it could be exploited.[^s1] This mirror image is the defensive lesson of the pattern. Whoever is threatened by a breakthrough should check whether a pawn move of his own can dismantle the attacking chain, or at least keep the pawns from reaching the rank where the sacrifice works.

## How to judge a breakthrough

A breakthrough is a calculation, not a slogan. Before sacrificing, the attacker must count three things: whether the new passed pawn can be caught by the enemy king (the rule of the square applies to it like to any runner), whether the defender's own remaining pawns will promote first, and whether his own king is close enough to deal with them.[^s1][^s4] Capablanca's diagram assumes the kings are far away; with a defending king nearby, the new passer may simply be rounded up, so the count must include the kings.[^s1] The order of the moves matters as much as the idea: the sacrifice usually starts with the pawn in the middle of the group, because only that capture opens two files at once.

## Typical mistakes

The commonest error is the one Naroditsky describes: treating an even pawn count on a wing as a dead zone and, for example, trading into a pawn ending that looks drawn but contains a winning breakthrough for the opponent.[^s4] A second error is advancing one's pawns too far on a wing where the opponent has a compact majority, handing him the formation he needs. A third is starting the sacrifice with the wrong pawn or a tempo too late, so that the defender recaptures in a way that leaves no passer at all.[^s1]

## Connections

A breakthrough is a way to create a **passed pawn** and a characteristic method of **king and pawn endgames**; it **leads to** **pawn promotion** and **depends on** counting **tempo** exactly.[^s1][^s2] Its success is checked with the **rule of the square**, and it is usually prepared from a **pawn majority**.[^s1][^s6] It recurs in piece endings too, with its own sections for knight, same-coloured and **opposite-coloured bishops** endings in Nunn's handbook, and it is a form of **sacrifice** in which material is given for a decisive passer rather than for an attack on the king.[^s3][^s5]

# Endgame tablebases

An endgame tablebase is a complete, precomputed table of every legal position in a given material balance, such as king, rook and pawn against king and rook, recording the exact result with best play and how long that result takes.[^s1][^s4] Unlike an engine's evaluation, which is an estimate, a tablebase entry is a proof: if it says a position is drawn, no amount of search will find a win. For a club player, tablebases are the final judge of whether an ending was won, where the decisive mistake was, and which textbook verdicts were wrong.[^s5]

## How they are built

Tablebases are generated backwards by *retrograde analysis*. The insight is that an ending with few men has an astronomical number of move sequences but a manageable number of distinct positions, so every position can be enumerated and solved outright.[^s6] In outline, the generator starts from the mates and from positions that convert into an already-solved smaller ending, then works backwards move by move until no new results appear; whatever is left is drawn.[^s1][^s6] Ken Thompson's 1986 *ICCA Journal* paper, "Retrograde analysis of certain endgames", used standard retrograde analysis to produce tables that recorded distances to mate, to conversion and to the next zeroing move.[^s1] The approach scales only as far as memory and disk allow, which is why the frontier has moved one man at a time: Thompson's work reached five-piece and later six-piece endings, and a full seven-piece set was computed at Moscow State University on the Lomonosov supercomputer in 2012, and in the 2020s work on eight-piece endings.[^s2][^s5][^s6]

## What the numbers mean

A tablebase can be indexed by different distance *metrics*, and they answer different questions.[^s1][^s5]

- **DTM (depth to mate)** counts moves to checkmate. It is the most intuitive but can be inflated by long preambles before a capture or promotion.[^s5]
- **DTC (depth to conversion)** counts moves until mate or a change of material; Marc Bourzutschky, who has computed many eight-piece results, prefers it for that reason.[^s5]
- **DTZ (depth to zeroing move)** counts moves until the next capture or pawn move, the events that reset the **fifty-move rule** counter.[^s1][^s5]

The choice matters in practice. Following a DTM-optimal line can take longer than the fifty-move rule allows between zeroing moves, so a "won" position can be drawn over the board. Haworth formalised the remedy as *depth by the rule*, the smallest move limit under which a position is still a win, and showed that it lies between DTZ and DTM.[^s1] The Syzygy tables, the format most engines use today, store a win/draw/loss (WDL) file and a separate DTZ file, and their DTZ is defined so that following it wins under the fifty-move rule; engines that probe them therefore treat a win that needs too many moves as a fifty-move draw unless told otherwise.[^s3][^s4]

## Key position

The `fen` above is Haworth's illustration of why the metrics differ: White Ka1, Ra2, Rb1 against Black Kd1, Bc1, White to move.[^s1] The quickest *zeroing* move is 1.Rxc1+, capturing the bishop at once, but that does not mate; the quickest *mate* is 1.Rab2, after which Black's only legal move is 1...Ke1 and 2.Rxc1# follows.[^s1] A program that blindly minimises DTZ captures first and wins more slowly, while one that minimises DTM may take longer to reset the counter, so practical tables have to be designed with the rule in mind.[^s1]

## Sizes, records and uses

The completed seven-piece Syzygy set, generated by Bojun Guo with optimisations by the format's author Ronald de Man, covers about 424 trillion unique legal positions in 18.4 TB, and includes the six-against-one endings that the Lomonosov set had omitted.[^s4] The Lomonosov team reported the longest known forced mate in seven-man chess, 549 moves.[^s2] Among pawnless seven-man endings, the Syzygy generators found a position where the defender can hold out for 1,034 half-moves before the winning capture, and some pawn endings need 656 half-moves to set up a decisive pawn advance; as of a 2022 report, Bourzutschky's longest eight-piece pawnless win was 584 moves (DTC).[^s4][^s5] Such records are volatile and may be extended as eight-piece work continues.

Engines probe these files during search. Stockfish's `SyzygyPath` option points it at the tables, `SyzygyProbeLimit` caps the number of men (default 7), and `Syzygy50MoveRule` can be switched off so that tablebase wins beyond fifty moves count as wins, which the project notes is useful for ICCF correspondence chess.[^s3] Lichess makes the full seven-piece set available free on its analysis board and through a public API.[^s4]

## Pitfalls for players

Tablebases tell you *what* is true, not *why*. A DTM-optimal line in a seven-piece ending often looks random to a human, and the shortest win is rarely the simplest to learn. They are best used to check a verdict or locate a mistake, then studied with a human method such as the techniques of **rook endgames**. Remember also that the result depends on the rule set: correspondence players and over-the-board players can get different answers from the same position.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Endgame tablebases belong both to the **chess endgame** and to **computer chess**: they are produced by **retrograde analysis**, a method pioneered by **Ken Thompson**, and are consulted by engines such as **Stockfish**.[^s1][^s3] Their distance metrics exist largely because of the **fifty-move rule**.[^s1][^s4] Because they give exact verdicts, they are the natural tool for cataloguing **zugzwang** positions, re-checking the theory of **rook endgames** and testing **endgame studies**, all treated in their own nodes; their longest wins are themselves a kind of composed curiosity.[^s4][^s5]

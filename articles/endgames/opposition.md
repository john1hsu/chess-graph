# Opposition

The opposition is the most basic tool of king play in the endgame. Because a king may never step onto a square the enemy king attacks, two kings that face each other with a single square between them block one another: neither can advance through that square, and whichever side must move has to step aside.[^s4] The side that does not have to move is said to have the opposition, and in pawn endings that usually decides whether the stronger king gets through to escort its pawn or the weaker king holds the line.[^s3] Capablanca described it as being of paramount importance in nearly all king-and-pawn endings, the exception being positions where the pawns alone settle the result.[^s1]

## Forms of the opposition

Capablanca defined having the opposition as forcing the opponent's king, by its own move, into a position where it must give way. He counted the squares between the kings on a common file, rank or diagonal: with an odd number between them, the player who moved last holds the opposition, and he distinguished frontal, lateral and diagonal forms, close or distant, while noting that in practice they are one idea.[^s1] Modern teaching uses the same vocabulary. Direct opposition has one square between the kings on a rank or file; diagonal opposition has one square between them on a diagonal; distant opposition has a larger odd number of squares between them.[^s3] Edward Lasker gave the same rule of thumb in colour terms: in the basic case the kings stand on squares of the same colour, and the side that has just moved into that relation has the opposition.[^s2]

## Why it matters with king and pawn against king

In king and pawn against king, the attacker wants his king in front of the pawn and as far forward as possible, advancing the pawn only when necessary; the defender wants his king directly in front of the pawn or, failing that, in front of the attacking king.[^s1] Lasker explained that when the attacking king stands one rank ahead of its pawn, the result usually depends on whether the defender can take the opposition, while a king two ranks ahead wins regardless; a spare pawn move can be used to hand the move back and force the defender to give way.[^s2] The rook's pawn is the classic exception, because the defending king draws by reaching the corner.[^s2]

The key position above (White Ke5, Pe4; Black Ke7) shows the point. The kings face each other on the e-file with one square between them. With Black to move, Black has to yield a side: after 1...Kd7 2.Kf6 Ke8 3.Ke6 Kf8 4.Kd7 the white king controls the promotion path and the pawn walks through. With White to move, the roles are reversed: 1.Kd5 Kd7 keeps the opposition and Black holds the draw. Lasker likewise stressed that once the pawn reaches the sixth rank, the defender must take the opposition on the back rank, and that choosing the wrong square there throws the draw away.[^s2]

## Typical mistakes

The most common error is to treat the opposition as an end in itself. It only matters in positions where one side is forced to move, which is why it dominates pawn endings and rarely decides positions with other pieces on the board that can make waiting moves.[^s2] A second error is to take the wrong form: Capablanca shows a pawn-down defence in which the only drawing move takes the distant opposition, while the natural-looking alternative lets the enemy king through and loses.[^s1] Defenders are also taught to hold the opposition in front of the attacking king rather than chase the pawn, guarding the squares of their own territory.[^s4]

## Connections

The opposition is the core technique of king-and-pawn endgames, and it rests on zugzwang: by definition the side with the opposition forces the opponent to move and give way.[^s1] Endgame manuals present it alongside key squares, which describe where the attacking king must arrive, and alongside corresponding squares and triangulation, the more general methods for winning or losing a tempo when simple opposition is not enough.[^s5] Its practical purpose is promotion: winning the opposition is usually what lets the king clear the path for the pawn to queen.[^s4]

# Outflanking

Outflanking is the king manoeuvre of going around the opposing king rather than straight at it. Two kings can never stand next to each other, so a defending king placed directly in front of the attacker blocks its path; the attacker therefore steps diagonally to the side, towards the wing the defender has just left, and then turns forwards past it. ChessKid's coach describes it as walking around the enemy king, used together with the opposition to push that king back or stop it advancing.[^s6] Jesús de la Villa treats it as an advanced pawn-ending concept: in his 2026 primer *100 Basic Endgames You Must Know*, "Outflanking" is Ending 51 in the chapter on advanced pawn endings, next to triangulation, the dominant king and the tempo move.[^s1]

## How it works

Outflanking is the payoff of the **opposition**. De la Villa defines the opposition as the kings facing each other on squares of the same colour with one square between them; the side that has just moved holds it, and the other king, if it must move, has to step back or sideways, which lets the first king gain a rank.[^s1] He stresses that this works best combined with **zugzwang**, so it matters most with few or blocked pawns, and that it is a visual guide rather than a rule that names the right move in every position.[^s1] Outflanking is what turns that sideways step into progress: once the defending king has moved to one side, the attacker goes diagonally the other way and gets past it. In Bruce Pandolfini's teaching examples the attacking king uses the opposition to reach a critical square and the defending king is described as outflanked.[^s5]

The technique is the mirror image of **shouldering** (the "bodycheck"), which de la Villa lists among his basic definitions alongside opposition and triangulation.[^s1] Shouldering puts one's king in the other's way; outflanking finds the way round a king that is already in the way.

## Beyond pure pawn endings

The idea also decides endings with pieces. In *100 Endgames You Must Know*, de la Villa's winning plan for bishop and two connected pawns on the sixth rank against an **opposite-coloured** bishop has two stages: a bishop check that forces the defending king to commit to one side, and then the attacking king outflanking it on the other.[^s2] After 1.Bb5+ Kd8 White's king goes round with 2.Kf5 and reaches f7 via g6 two moves later, after which the e-pawn advances with check; had Black gone to f8 instead, White would run the same plan on the queenside via d5 and c6.[^s2]

In rook against pawn, Mark Dvoretsky's *Endgame Manual* gives outflanking its own heading in the chapter on rook versus one pawn, straight after shouldering.[^s3] Grandmaster Hovhannes Gabuzyan shows it from the rook's side. In his example, with a black pawn on f5 and White to move, the natural 1.Ke6 lets the pawn run down the f-file under checks and ends in a draw after promotion to a knight. The winning route is 1.Kf6!!, coming round the other side of the pawn: 1...f4 2.Kg5 f3 3.Kg4 f2 4.Rf5 Ke3 5.Kg3, and the pawn falls.[^s4]

## Typical uses and pitfalls

- **Converting simplification.** Several of Pandolfini's exercises win by trading into a pawn ending that is won because the defender's king is outflanked, for example 1.Rxc6 bxc6 2.Kxe4 Kb7 3.Ke5.[^s5]
- **Breaking a blockade.** The coach's column notes that outflanking is how a king escorts a pawn or breaks into a group of enemy pawns.[^s6]
- **Choosing the right side.** The common mistake is to approach directly: Gabuzyan's 1.Ke6 is the obvious move and only draws, while the correct king route looks like a detour.[^s4]
- **Taking the opposition for its own sake.** Opposition only helps if a way round follows; de la Villa warns that the opposition is not an automatic guide to the best move.[^s1]

## Connections

Outflanking belongs to **king and pawn endgames** and the wider **chess endgame**, where de la Villa groups it with **triangulation** and other advanced pawn-ending ideas.[^s1] It **depends on** the **opposition**, which forces the defending king aside, and on **zugzwang**, which gives the opposition its force; its target is usually the **key squares** in front of a pawn.[^s1][^s5] It **contrasts with** **shouldering**, the blocking manoeuvre, and both are **part of** the theory of **rook versus pawn**.[^s3][^s4] It also supplies the winning plan in some **opposite-coloured bishops** endings.[^s2]

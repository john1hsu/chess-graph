# Chess endgame

The endgame (or ending) is the last of the three phases of a chess game, after the opening and the middlegame. Encyclopaedia Britannica describes it as the stage that generally arrives after several exchanges of pieces, when pawn promotion becomes the dominant theme, and lists endgame technique, including the basic mates against a lone king, as one of the core components of chess theory alongside openings, tactics and strategy.[^s1] There is no exact boundary: a game has entered the endgame when the reduced material changes what the players are fighting for, from attacking the king to creating and stopping passed pawns.[^s1]

## What changes in the endgame

**The king becomes a fighting piece.** In the middlegame the king hides behind its pawns; with the queens and most pieces gone, it is rarely in danger and is often the strongest piece on the board. Capablanca's *Chess Fundamentals* (1921) repeatedly centralises the king in its endgame examples, stating that in such endings the king belongs in the middle of the board, and in one rook-and-minor-piece ending shows the black king walking in to attack White's pawns to decide the game.[^s2]

**Pawns matter more.** With fewer pieces to stop them, pawns become potential queens. Capablanca's chapters on pawn endings teach the student to create a passed pawn where they have a majority, to count the moves needed for rival passed pawns to queen, and to use a single pawn to hold back two enemy pawns.[^s2] Small structural details, such as which colour squares the pawns stand on relative to one's bishop, can decide the result.[^s2]

**Precise rather than approximate play.** Fewer pieces mean that many endings can be calculated exactly. Müller and Lamprecht built *Fundamental Chess Endings* on endgame tablebases and engines that consult them, which let them state definitive verdicts where earlier writers could only estimate.[^s3]

## How endgame knowledge is organised

Endgame theory is usually divided by material. Capablanca starts with the elementary mates (king and queen, king and rook, two bishops, bishop and knight against a lone king), moves on to king and pawn endings and the opposition, then to minor-piece endings, queen against rook and rook endings.[^s2] Modern textbooks follow the same logic but add a second axis, the player's strength: Jeremy Silman's *Complete Endgame Course* is arranged by rating level, from basic mates for beginners, through building blocks such as the opposition, the Lucena and Philidor positions, triangulation and the square of the pawn, to advanced concepts like the principle of two weaknesses.[^s4] Müller and Lamprecht's work, by contrast, is a one-volume encyclopaedia of all the major endgame types, combining analysis with practical rules of thumb.[^s3]

## Key principles

Capablanca's endgame advice, still standard, can be summed up in a few rules:[^s2]

- **Activate the king** and bring it towards the centre or the side where the play is.[^s2]
- **Put the king in front of the pawn.** When escorting a pawn, the king should stand in front of it with at least one square between them; if instead the defending king blocks the pawn's path, the ending is usually drawn.[^s2]
- **Use the opposition** in king and pawn endings, since it often decides who can advance.[^s2]
- **Keep the initiative.** Passive defence tends to lose; harass the opponent, force their pieces to defend pawns, and when one weakness has been fixed, create a second.[^s2]
- **Don't relax.** Capablanca shows a won ending thrown away in three moves to make the point that full concentration is needed until the end.[^s2]

## Example position

In the diagram position (White Ke4, Pe2; Black Ke7) White's king stands in front of its pawn with squares between them, the arrangement Capablanca recommends for escorting a pawn to promotion.[^s2] White's king leads the way and the pawn follows behind it, rather than the pawn being pushed alone; positions in which the kings face each other directly are instead decided by the opposition.[^s2]

## Why study it

Capablanca argued that endings are not optional for an ambitious player, noting that the leading champions before him had all been very strong in endgames, and urged students to learn rook-and-pawn endings in particular, since they are the most common.[^s2] Müller and Lamprecht add a practical reason: modern time controls finish games in one session, so players must handle endgames at the board without adjournment analysis.[^s3]

## Connections

The endgame is one phase of **chess** as a whole and the stage where long-term **chess strategy**, such as pawn structure decisions made in the middlegame, pays off.[^s2] Its main branches include **king and pawn endgames** and **rook endgames**, the latter being the most common type in practice.[^s2] Its most characteristic technique is the **opposition**, which Capablanca treats as governing pawn endings.[^s2] **José Raúl Capablanca** is closely associated with endgame mastery and with teaching the ending first.[^s2] Modern theory is increasingly checked against **endgame tablebases**.[^s3]

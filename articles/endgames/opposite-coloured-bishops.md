# Opposite-coloured bishops

When each side is left with a single bishop and one bishop travels on light squares while the other travels on dark squares, the bishops are said to be of opposite colours. Neither bishop can ever attack, block or exchange the other, so each player effectively controls one colour complex unopposed.[^s5] In a pure ending with only kings, bishops and pawns this makes the defence unusually resilient: an extra pawn, and sometimes two, is often not enough to win.[^s6] Endgame manuals nevertheless treat the material as a subject with its own rules rather than an automatic draw. Dvoretsky's manual gives it a full chapter, opening with "the most important rules",[^s2] and Bezgodov's 2024 monograph collects 174 practical examples precisely to show that many such endings can be won.[^s3]

## Why the defence is so strong

The defender's basic weapon is a blockade on the colour his opponent's bishop does not control. A king or bishop that stands in front of a passed pawn on a square of the defender's colour cannot be driven away by the enemy bishop, and the attacker's king alone usually cannot break it.[^s5] Holmer's summary of the classic advice distinguishes two set-ups: a king fortress, where the king itself blocks the passed pawn, and a bishop fortress, where the bishop does the blocking and the defender must watch for the attacking king approaching it; Alburt considered the king fortress the easier one to hold.[^s5] A related guideline concerns pawn placement: the defender wants his pawns on the colour of his own bishop, where they are protected, while the attacker wants his pawns on squares his bishop does not cover, so that the two pieces control different squares.[^s5]

## When the stronger side wins

The decisive factor is usually two passed pawns. With two connected passers the defender needs his king in front of them; pawns that reach the sixth rank normally win.[^s6] De la Villa notes that drawing chances are still higher than in other endings with two connected pawns, because the defender can either blockade them on his colour or give up the bishop for both.[^s1] With two separated passers, the further apart they are the better: the defending king and bishop each have to stop one pawn, and the attacking king breaks through to support one of them. Pawns only one file apart generally cannot win,[^s6] and a defender whose bishop can watch both pawns from a single diagonal is also safe.[^s1][^s5] Dvoretsky's chapter accordingly covers bishop and two connected pawns against bishop, separated passed pawns, the king blockading a passer and the bishop restraining it.[^s2]

Bezgodov stresses practical resources beyond these rules: activating a boxed-in king, creating a passed pawn on the wing the defending bishop cannot reach, the colour of the promotion corner, and sacrificing the bishop or a pawn to free connected passers.[^s3]

## Key example

De la Villa's first example has White Ke5, Bd3, pawns e6 and f6 against Black Ke8, Bb4. Pushing a pawn achieves nothing: e7 lets Black give the bishop for both pawns, and f7+ leaves the pawns fixed on light squares. White first checks to force the black king to choose a side, then walks the king round to the other side: 1.Bb5+ Kd8 2.Kf5 Bc5 3.Kg6 Bb4 4.Kf7 Bc5 5.e7+ wins. If instead 1...Kf8, the same plan works on the queenside with 2.Kd5 Ba3 3.Kc6 Bb4 4.Kd7 Bc5 5.e7+.[^s1]

## In the middlegame

With heavy pieces still on the board the logic reverses. The attacking side effectively plays with an extra piece on the colour its opponent's bishop cannot defend, so opposite-coloured bishops tend to favour whoever has the initiative, and an attack can matter more than material.[^s6] Bezgodov devotes chapters to such bishops alongside rooks, queens and knights and to direct attacking play,[^s3] and Sergey Kasparov's monograph on the bishop treats the middlegame case in a separate chapter from the endgame.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

A common practical error is to trade into an opposite-coloured bishop ending a pawn up expecting an easy win; one extra pawn without other advantages is usually a draw.[^s6] For the defender, the error is passivity on the wrong colour: pawns left on squares the attacker's bishop can hit, or a blockade set up on a square the enemy bishop controls, can be broken.[^s5]

## Connections

This ending belongs to the broader family of endgame types and contrasts with endings where the bishops share a colour, which Dvoretsky treats in the next chapter.[^s2] Its drawing method is a blockade, often a fortress, while the winning technique frequently relies on zugzwang, as when the attacking king outflanks the defender in de la Villa's example.[^s1] The topic also links to good and bad bishops and to passed pawns, whose number and separation decide most of these endings.[^s6]

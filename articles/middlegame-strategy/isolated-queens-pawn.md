# Isolated queen's pawn

An isolated pawn is one with no pawns of the same colour on the neighbouring files.[^s1] The isolated queen's pawn (IQP, or isolani) is the most important case: a white pawn on d4 or a black pawn on d5 with the c- and e-pawns of that side gone, so it can never be defended by another pawn.[^s3][^s5] Aron Nimzowitsch called it one of the cardinal problems of positional play, and whole monographs are devoted to it.[^s4][^s3]

## Two views of the same pawn

The structure has been argued over since the nineteenth century. Wilhelm Steinitz treated the isolated d-pawn mainly as a liability: an enemy knight can blockade it and, in time, it can be won. Siegbert Tarrasch replied that the same pawn supports advanced posts for its owner's knights and is therefore often an asset.[^s1] Tarrasch put the theory into practice with Black, and his defence (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 c5) accepts an isolani deliberately; Tigran Petrosian later used it against Boris Spassky in their 1969 world championship match.[^s3] Modern writers mostly frame the question as one of activity: Semkov argues that piece activity, not the static structure, usually decides these positions.[^s2]

## How IQP positions arise

Many mainstream openings produce the structure. Typical routes include the Queen's Gambit Accepted and the Orthodox and deferred-Tarrasch lines of the Queen's Gambit Declined, where White ends up with a pawn on d4 after exd4.[^s3] The Panov–Botvinnik Attack of the Caro-Kann (1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.c4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Nf3 Bb4 7.cxd5 Nxd5) and some lines of the Alapin Sicilian also give White the isolani, while the Tarrasch Defence and the Tarrasch Variation of the French give it to Black.[^s5]

## Example

A classic illustration is the opening of Zukertort–Steinitz from their 1886 match, a Queen's Gambit Accepted: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nf3 dxc4 5.e3 c5 6.Bxc4 cxd4 7.exd4 Be7 8.O-O O-O, reaching the diagram position (see `fen`).[^s2][^s3] White's d4-pawn stands alone, but White has free development, open c- and e-files and the e5-square for a knight; Black's future depends on whether the pawn can be fixed and attacked.

## Plans for the side with the isolani

- **Use the outposts.** The d4-pawn controls e5 and c5; a knight on e5 is the classic springboard for a kingside attack.[^s3]
- **Attack the king.** With extra space, the IQP side aims queen and bishop batteries at the king and lifts rooks along the third rank, as Zukertort did with a rook swinging via d3 to the kingside.[^s5][^s2]
- **Prepare the break.** The advance d4–d5 (or ...d5–d4 for Black) opens the position and is most effective when its owner leads in development.[^s3]
- **Avoid aimless simplification.** As pieces are exchanged the pawn becomes harder to defend, so the IQP side generally needs to act while the middlegame lasts.[^s5]

## Plans against the isolani

- **Blockade.** A piece, ideally a knight, on the square in front of the pawn (d5 against d4) stops it advancing and cannot be driven away by pawns.[^s3] Semkov warns that the blockade is not automatic: sometimes controlling e5 matters more than occupying d5.[^s2]
- **Exchange pieces.** Trading reduces the attacking potential of the IQP side; the exchange of dark-squared bishops is a recurring theme.[^s3]
- **Pile up on the pawn.** With fewer defenders left, the pawn can be attacked by rooks and queen and eventually won, as Anatoly Karpov did against Gata Kamsky in game four of their 1996 title match.[^s5]

## Pitfalls and the endgame

A common mistake for the defender is to fall behind in development while eyeing the pawn: that is exactly when the d4–d5 break works best for the IQP side.[^s3] For the IQP side, the opposite mistake is passivity. Endgames are less one-sided than the cliché suggests: Semkov finds them generally drawish, with pawn endings usually holding and rook endings decided by rook activity on the open files.[^s2]

## Connections

The IQP is a kind of **pawn structure** closely tied to the **outpost** and **blockade** concepts. It is the defining feature of the **Tarrasch Defence** and arises from the **Queen's Gambit Accepted** and the Panov–Botvinnik line of the **Caro-Kann Defence**.[^s3][^s5] It was championed by **Siegbert Tarrasch**.[^s1] It contrasts with **hanging pawns** and other central pawn pairs, which the monograph literature treats alongside it as a related structure.[^s3]

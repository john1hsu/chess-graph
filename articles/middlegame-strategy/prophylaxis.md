# Prophylaxis

Prophylaxis in chess means seeing what the opponent intends and stopping it before it happens. Serper, quoting the Chess.com glossary in that sense, goes as far as calling it the most important concept in the game.[^s5] Grooten reduces the habit to one question asked after every enemy move: what does my opponent want? Answering it guards against short tricks and also exposes the opponent's long-term plans.[^s6]

## Origins: Nimzowitsch's system

The word entered chess theory through Aron Nimzowitsch. In *My System* the first chapter of Part II is titled "Prophylaxis and the Centre", and Nimzowitsch presents prophylaxis there, together with the collective mobility of the pawn mass, as a main postulate of positional play.[^s1][^s2] His neighbouring chapters treat restraint and over-protection, and the companion volume *Chess Praxis* groups its illustrative games under the heading "Over-Protection and Other Forms of Prophylaxis", which shows that he regarded over-protecting a strong point as one kind of prophylactic measure.[^s2] In his foreword to the Quality Chess edition, Artur Yusupov lists prophylaxis with the blockade among the 1925 ideas that still shape master play.[^s1]

## Petrosian, Karpov and the modern view

Yusupov writes that Tigran Petrosian and Anatoly Karpov perfected a prophylactic style built on restricting the opponent's options, and that many of Garry Kasparov's decisions also rest on prophylactic grounds.[^s1] Britannica describes Petrosian's play as patient and designed to wear an opponent down gradually rather than to win with one blow.[^s4] Serper cites the remark that Petrosian stopped his opponents' ideas before they had even thought of them, and calls prophylaxis the cornerstone of Karpov's play.[^s5]

The Soviet trainer Mark Dvoretsky made "prophylactic thinking" a training subject in its own right, using Karpov's games to demonstrate it.[^s5] His book on preventive thinking contains a whole chapter of positional exercises of this kind; in one, from Botvinnik–Levenfish, Moscow 1937, the key move 41.Rd7 keeps the enemy king from reaching e7, and in another, from Gligoric–Gruenfeld, Skara 1980, 21.Bc1 is aimed at a knight manoeuvre Black was preparing.[^s3] Dvoretsky also shows that prophylaxis need not be passive: in a Dolmatov ending a pawn advance is labelled very specific, aggressive prophylaxis.[^s3]

## How it works in practice

Grooten separates two levels. The basic level is the routine check of the opponent's threats, which saves a player from cheap tactics; the advanced level, where Karpov and Petrosian excelled, requires knowledge and experience to sense the opponent's strategic plan and take the air out of it.[^s6] Serper notes that even at the lowest level, a beginner who asks what the opponent is going to do will not fall for Scholar's Mate.[^s5] Typical prophylactic means are taking a key square away from an enemy piece, stopping a freeing pawn break, preventing an exchange that would relieve the opponent, and moving the king away from future checks before starting active operations.[^s3][^s6][^s7]

The commonest mistake is to think only about one's own plan. Serper points out that strong grandmasters also forget to check the opponent's intentions; in an example from the 2026 TePe Sigeman tournament, the natural 58.Qf6 would have let Black draw, while the quiet 58.Kg1 kept the win.[^s5] The opposite error is passivity: a prophylactic move earns its place only if the opponent's idea was real, and it should be followed by play of one's own.

## Example: Karpov–Unzicker, Nice 1974

In this closed Ruy Lopez, after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 6.Re1 b5 7.Bb3 d6 8.c3 O-O 9.h3 Na5 10.Bc2 c5 11.d4 Qc7 12.Nbd2 Nc6 13.d5 Nd8 14.a4 Rb8 15.axb5 axb5 16.b4 Nb7 17.Nf1 Bd7 18.Be3 Ra8 19.Qd2 Rfc8 20.Bd3 g6 21.Ng3 Bf8 22.Ra2 c4 23.Bb1 Qd8, Black was preparing to contest the open a-file and trade rooks.[^s7] Karpov answered 24.Ba7 (the `fen` position): the bishop occupies the a-file itself, so the rook exchange Black wanted never comes. Leitão calls it perhaps Karpov's most famous move and notes a few moves later that Black has no plan left.[^s7]

## Connections

Prophylaxis is a branch of **chess strategy** and was **championed by** **Aron Nimzowitsch**, who linked it to **restraint**, the **blockade** and **over-protection**; it became part of the legacy of **hypermodernism**.[^s1][^s2] Its great practitioners were **Tigran Petrosian** and **Anatoly Karpov**, and the game Karpov–Unzicker, Nice 1974 **exemplifies** it.[^s1][^s7] As a thinking habit it complements the search for **candidate moves**: before choosing a move, a player first asks what the opponent is threatening or planning.[^s3][^s6]

# Carlsbad structure

The Carlsbad structure is a symmetrical-looking but unbalanced pawn formation. White has pawns on d4 and e3 plus a queenside pair on a2 and b2, while Black has d5 and c6 plus a7 and b7.[^s1][^s4] It comes from an exchange on d5 in which White's c-pawn has been traded for Black's e-pawn, so each side has two pawn islands: White gets a half-open c-file, Black a half-open e-file, and Black keeps three queenside pawns against White's two.[^s4] Grooten notes that the standard literature uses the name for the structure that follows cxd5 exd5, and that it holds more content than many players suspect.[^s5]

## Name and occurrence

The name comes from the Carlsbad (now Karlovy Vary) tournament of 1923, where the line was popular, although the formation had been seen in master play well before then.[^s1][^s4] Its classic source is the Exchange Variation of the Queen's Gambit Declined, 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.cxd5 exd5 5.Bg5 Be7 6.e3 c6, the position in the diagram.[^s4][^s2] The same pawns, with colours reversed (White c3 and d4 against Black d5 and e6), appear in the Caro-Kann Exchange Variation, for example after 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.Nf3 Nc6 5.Bd3 Bg4 6.c3 e6, and in the London System.[^s4][^s1] Bauer links renewed interest in the structure to the popularity of these three openings.[^s1] It can also come from the Nimzo-Indian and other queen's-pawn openings.[^s4]

## Plans for White

White has three families of plans, and much of the art lies in choosing between them according to where Black's king and pieces stand.[^s1][^s2]

- **The minority attack.** White advances b2–b4–b5 to exchange on c6 and leave Black with a weak, backward c-pawn on a half-open file.[^s1][^s5] Chess.com cites Capablanca–Golombek (1939) as a classic example.[^s4] Semkov, writing about modern practice, considers this flank advance with fewer pawns less effective than its reputation suggests.[^s2]
- **The central break.** White prepares e3–e4, often with Nge2 and f2–f3, to challenge d5 and gain central space.[^s1][^s4] Semkov credits Kasparov with championing this approach in the 1980s and 1990s, and Chess.com points to Kasparov–Short from the 1993 world championship match, in which White's central pawns overran Black.[^s2][^s4]
- **The kingside attack.** With a knight on e5 supported by f2–f4, sometimes combined with g2–g4 or long castling, White attacks Black's king; Chess.com names this the Pillsbury Attack after the American master.[^s4][^s2] Semkov regards the set-up with Qc2, Ne5 and f4 as White's most aggressive plan, and gives Carlsen–Mamedyarov, with long castling, among his model games.[^s2]

## Plans for Black

Black's counterplay is real, and Grooten summarises it as either preventing or delaying White's queenside play, or building a dangerous initiative on the kingside once White has castled short.[^s5]

- **Knight on d6 or e4.** Prusikin draws on what he calls Capablanca's insights: a knight on d6 controls b5 and slows the minority attack, can jump to c4 to block the c-file, and can join a kingside attack via e4.[^s3] Bauer also lists blockading knights on d6 or e4.[^s1]
- **Kingside pawn play.** Prusikin's model games, such as Portisch–Kasparov (Skellefteå 1989) and Toth–Kramnik (Rio de Janeiro 1991), show Black storming with ...h5, ...g5 and ...g4, often with ...f5, while keeping queens on to retain attacking chances.[^s3] Bauer gives ...f5–f4 as Black's typical kingside lever.[^s1]
- **Piece manoeuvres and breaks.** Typical regroupings include ...h6 to question the g5-bishop, exchanges with ...Ne4, the knight route ...Nbd7–f8–e6 or g6, and a central counter-strike with ...c5.[^s2] Black can also contest the c-file with a rook on c8.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

For White, the main error is to launch b4–b5 automatically: if Black's pieces are ready on the kingside, the minority attack can be too slow, and both Semkov and Prusikin's annotated games show it achieving less than its reputation in many positions.[^s2][^s3] For Black, a passive set-up lets White choose the plan at leisure; the knight route to d6 and a timely kingside initiative are the antidotes most often recommended.[^s3][^s5] Because the structure arises from several openings, a player who knows its plans can carry them over from one opening to another, which is why authors such as Bauer treat it as a subject in its own right.[^s1]

## Connections

The Carlsbad structure is one of the named formations within **pawn structure** and is the classic home of the **minority attack**, the plan it most often **leads to**.[^s4][^s5] It is **named after** the **Carlsbad 1923** tournament.[^s1] It arises from the **Queen's Gambit Declined** Exchange Variation and in reversed form from the **Caro-Kann Defence** Exchange and the **London System**.[^s1][^s4] **Garry Kasparov** championed White's central plan, and **José Raúl Capablanca** both played a classic minority attack and supplied the knight-on-d6 ideas used by Black.[^s2][^s3][^s4] It is a neighbour of **hanging pawns**, another Queen's Gambit structure built around the c- and d-files.[^s5]

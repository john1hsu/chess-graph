# Weak squares

A weak square is one that a player can no longer fight for: the opponent controls it, and no pawn or piece of the defending side can effectively contest that control.[^s4] Usually the cause is pawn play, because the pawns that once guarded the square have moved past it and pawns never move backwards.[^s4][^s6] Wilhelm Steinitz, the first world champion, coined the term "hole" for a square that has lost its pawn protection and can be favourably occupied by an enemy piece, and counted such squares, especially central ones or those near the king, among the structural weaknesses that positional play should target.[^s1]

## Why squares matter as much as pawns

Club players tend to count pawns and overlook squares. Wahls argues that weak squares deserve at least as much attention as weak pawns, since there are far more squares than pawns and the damage from a lost square builds up slowly rather than showing up in the material count.[^s6] Silman lists control of a hole or weak square as one of the basic imbalances a player should assess in any position.[^s3] Britannica notes that structural weaknesses tend to become more exploitable as pieces are exchanged, so a hole that seems harmless in a crowded middlegame can decide an endgame.[^s1]

## How weak squares arise

- **Pawn advances.** Every pawn move gives up control of the squares it used to cover. Silman's contents make the point bluntly: even an innocent-looking pawn move can weaken a square.[^s3]
- **Space gains.** Advancing pawns to gain space leaves squares behind them; Silman warns that gains in space equal potential weak squares, and Wahls notes that a space-gaining structure can leave weaknesses on its own third rank.[^s3][^s6]
- **Doubled and isolated pawns.** Doubled pawns create weak squares, above all the square in front of the leading pawn.[^s2] The square in front of an isolated or backward pawn is weak for the same reason: no neighbouring pawn can ever guard it.[^s1][^s6]
- **Missing bishops.** When a bishop is exchanged, the squares of its colour lose a defender. If the remaining pawns stand on the other colour, a whole group of squares becomes weak at once.[^s4][^s5]

## Weak colour complexes

The most dangerous form is a complex of same-coloured weak squares around the king. Till's standard case is a black king behind pawns on f7, g6 and h7: those pawns stand on light squares, so f6, g7 and h6 depend on the dark-squared bishop, and once that bishop is traded the squares are guarded, if at all, only by the king.[^s4][^s5] If the attacker keeps a queen and a bishop of that colour, the combination is especially strong.[^s5] Katz calls the dark squares around a king on g8 without a g7-bishop extremely weak and shows attacks built on landing the queen on g7 or h7 with a bishop controlling the long diagonal.[^s2]

In the illustrative position in the `fen`, Black has no dark-squared bishop and pawns on f7, g6 and h7. White's plan is the one Till and Wahls describe: bring the bishop to h6 and the queen toward f6 or g7, using pieces of the right colour to penetrate the holes that no black pawn can cover.[^s5][^s6]

## Exploiting and defending

For the attacker, a weak square is an address. Wahls recommends choosing the ideal square for a piece and planning the route to it backwards, trading off the opponent's remaining defender of the square, and preventing the hole from being closed by a timely pawn advance.[^s6] Sometimes a latent hole only matters after a pawn move forces the issue, so part of the plan is to make the weakness relevant.[^s6]

For the defender, the main rules are preventive: do not move pawns onto the colour of a bishop you have already traded, and do not trade the bishop that guards the colour your pawns have left.[^s4][^s5] Till's summary lesson is never to build a weak complex in front of your own king and then exchange its defenders.[^s5] Wahls adds that every weakness has a price, so a player who creates one should be sure the gain is worth it.[^s6]

## Connections

Weak squares are a direct consequence of **pawn structure** and a central theme of **chess strategy** since Steinitz.[^s1] An **outpost** is a weak square seen from the side that can occupy it with a supported piece.[^s4][^s6] Colour complexes tie the topic to **good and bad bishops** and to the value of the **bishop pair**, since losing one bishop exposes squares of its colour.[^s4][^s5] **Space advantage** often comes at the cost of weak squares behind the advanced pawns, and weak squares near the king are a leading cause of failures in **king safety**.[^s3][^s2]

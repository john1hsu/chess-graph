# Alekhine's gun

**Alekhine's gun** is the name given to a formation in which a player stacks all three heavy pieces on one file with the two rooks in front and the queen at the back.[^s1][^s5] It is a battery: each piece supports the one ahead of it, so the force bearing on the file, and on any target at its far end, is as great as it can be.[^s5] Silman calls it the strongest of all open-file set-ups, going one step beyond simply doubling the rooks.[^s3] The formation takes its name from Alexander Alekhine's game against Aron Nimzowitsch at San Remo 1930, where White completed the battery on the c-file with 26.Qc1.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin of the name

Edward Winter's research found the term in print no earlier than Jeremy Silman's *How to Reassess Your Chess* (Los Angeles, 1993), which applied it to the position after 26.Qc1; Silman later said he did not remember where the name came from.[^s1][^s2] Winter noted that the periodicals he checked from 1930 made no comment on 26.Qc1 itself.[^s1] Soviet-era instructional literature had a related label: a 1984 Moscow textbook, translated in a 1985 Spanish book edited by Yakov Estrin, said that this arrangement of the heavy pieces was often called "Alekhine's", probably because of the San Remo game.[^s1]

The formation itself is much older than the name. Winter cites Kennedy–Mayet, London 1851, as the earliest example he could quote with the rooks ahead of the queen, and Calvi–Kieseritzky, Paris 1842, as an earlier tripling with the queen placed between the rooks instead.[^s1] Bronznik and Terekhin, in *Techniques of Positional Play* (2013), discussed Blackburne–Rosenthal, Paris 1878, under the heading "Blackburne's battering ram" and argued that the rooks belong in front of the queen.[^s1]

## Why the queen goes behind

The order of the pieces is the defining feature: earlier triplings sometimes put the queen between the rooks, but the version named after Alekhine has both rooks leading, the arrangement Bronznik and Terekhin also recommend.[^s1] Stacking all three pieces lets the player both control the file and pile pressure on a target standing on it.[^s5] The battery is usually built on an open or half-open file, where heavy pieces are most effective, but it can also be set up on a closed file in preparation for a pawn break that will open it.[^s5] Chess.com's terms page gives Karpov–Spassky, 1979, as an example on a half-open d-file, where pressure on an isolated d5-pawn tied most of Black's pieces to its defence.[^s5]

## Typical plans and pitfalls

- **Load slowly, then fire.** Naroditsky stresses that the gun usually needs patience to assemble and is most dangerous against a defender who waits passively without counterplay.[^s4]
- **Look beyond the file.** In his examples the decisive blow sometimes fell on a neighbouring file, or came from a sudden change in the position, once the defender's pieces had been tied to guarding entry squares.[^s4]
- **Do not overrate it.** Silman warns that an open file can be a road to nowhere and that threats elsewhere may outweigh it; he also shows a case where a gun was undone because its owner grabbed a pawn he should not have taken.[^s3]

## Example: Alekhine–Nimzowitsch, San Remo 1930

In this French Defence, the c-file opened after 12.b4 cxb4 13.cxb4. Alekhine then played 20.Rfc1, 21.Rc2, 22.Rac1, 24.Rc3, 25.R1c2 and 26.Qc1, and Nimzowitsch answered 26...Rbc8, doubling on the same file.[^s6] Silman's verdict is that Black was already lost by then, since the pressure on c7 and c6 left his pieces without useful moves.[^s3] After 27.Ba4 b5 28.Bxb5 Ke8 29.Ba4 Kd8 30.h4 Qe8 31.b5, Black resigned (see `fen`); Silman describes the end as zugzwang.[^s6][^s3] He adds that Alekhine set up the gun again as Black against William Winter at Nottingham 1936, and won.[^s3]

## Connections

Alekhine's gun is the strongest way of occupying an **open file**, and so belongs to general **chess strategy** about heavy pieces and files.[^s3][^s5] It is a special case of a **battery**, the stacking of line pieces on one line.[^s5] Its usual goal, as with any file operation, is entry to the seventh or eighth rank, making it a route to a **rook on the seventh rank**.[^s3] The name honours **Alexander Alekhine** and his model game **Alekhine versus Nimzowitsch, San Remo 1930**, which ended in **zugzwang**.[^s1][^s3] **Anatoly Karpov** used it against an **isolated queen's pawn** in 1979.[^s5]

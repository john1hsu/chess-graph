# Pawn chain

A pawn chain is a group of pawns standing one behind another on the same diagonal, so that each pawn protects the one in front of it.[^s4] In practice the term is used mostly for chains that have run into an enemy chain and become fixed: Herman Grooten describes a chain as pawns that are locked against each other, with both sides then manoeuvring their pieces behind the barricade.[^s5] Because only the rearmost pawn has no pawn protecting it, that pawn, the **base**, is the weak point; the front pawn is usually called the head or tip.[^s4][^s5]

## Nimzowitsch's theory

Aron Nimzowitsch made the chain one of the pillars of *My System* (1925). In his preface he says he treats the pawn chain in the same systematic way as the passed pawn.[^s1][^s2] Chapter 9 is devoted to it, and its section headings give the theory in outline: the base of the chain and the idea of two separate battlefields; attacking the chain; attacking the base as a strategic necessity; carrying the rules of the blockade over from passed pawns to chains; the choice between a sudden assault and a slow siege of the unprotected base; and finally transferring the attack from one link to the next.[^s1][^s2] In the later *Chess Praxis* he returned to the problem with a group of games headed as his new treatment of the pawn chain.[^s2]

The central rule is that the attacker should strike at the base rather than the head: the base is the one pawn in the chain that no pawn defends, whereas the head is protected by the pawn behind it.[^s4] Nimzowitsch's headings treat the attack on the base as a strategic necessity and add a separate section on transferring the attack, the case where the fight has to move on from the first target.[^s1][^s2]

## Space and the direction of the chain

A chain divides the board. Each side has more room behind the tip of its own chain than behind its base, so the chain is said to point towards the wing where its owner has more space.[^s4] The practical guideline is to play on the side where the chain points, and to attack the opponent's chain at its base on the other wing.[^s4] Grooten notes that the typical chains of the French Defence and the King's Indian Defence are mirror images of each other across the centre.[^s5]

## The French Advance chain

After 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5 c5 4.c3 (the `fen` above), White's chain c3-d4-e5 points to the kingside and Black's e6-d5 chain points to the queenside.[^s4][^s7] Black's standard plan follows the theory exactly: ...c5 hits d4, the base of White's chain, and the counterstrike ...f6 attacks the head at e5 to break the chain up.[^s7] The same d4-e5 versus e6-d5 structure, with an open c-file, also appears in lines of the Caro-Kann, the Sicilian and the Slav.[^s7] For White, the difficulty is often how to break Black's chain at all; Grooten stresses deciding which pawn should advance and which should wait, and regrouping the pieces first, and offers Anand against Ramirez (2004) as a model game.[^s6]

## The King's Indian chain

In the King's Indian, when White answers ...e5 with d5, the chains point the other way. Sam Collins summarises the plans: White plays on the queenside, typically with the c4-c5 break, while Black seeks kingside play with ...f7-f5, and after f2-f3 continues ...f5-f4 and ...g6-g5-g4, a pure race.[^s3] So against Nakamura at the 2015 Sinquefield Cup shows the pattern: 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6 5.Nf3 O-O 6.Be2 e5 7.O-O Nc6 8.d5 Ne7 9.Ne1 Nd7 10.f3 f5 11.Be3 f4 12.Bf2 g5 13.Nd3, after which the c4-c5 break is the critical theme.[^s3]

## Pitfalls

Attacking the head of a chain is usually less effective than attacking its base.[^s4] A frequent club error is to play on the wing where the chain gives the opponent space instead of one's own; another is to rush a pawn break before the pieces are ready to support it, which Grooten's French examples warn against.[^s4][^s6] In race positions such as the King's Indian, both sides' plans run on opposite wings, so time spent elsewhere is usually wasted.[^s3]

## Connections

The pawn chain is a specific form of **pawn structure** and a basic topic of **chess strategy**, systematised and **championed** by **Aron Nimzowitsch**.[^s1][^s2] Its direction **leads to** a **space advantage** on one wing.[^s4] Nimzowitsch linked the fight against a chain to the **blockade**, and chains are the defining structures of the **French Defence** and the **King's Indian Defence**, where a pawn **breakthrough** against the base decides many games.[^s1][^s3][^s5]

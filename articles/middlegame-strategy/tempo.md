# Tempo

A **tempo** (plural **tempi**) is one move, or one turn to play, regarded as a unit of time. Edward Winter's survey of the term notes that the Oxford English Dictionary does not record the chess sense, and he points to B. H. Wood's *Easy Guide to Chess* (1942) for a definition along exactly these lines.[^s1] The word is Italian for "time", and a player is said to gain a tempo by making a useful move that also forces the opponent to answer it, so that the opponent's plan is postponed; whatever one side gains, the other loses.[^s6] The earliest chess use Winter has found is in *The New Chess Player* of 28 August 1852, where an annotator wrote that a side "gains a tempo" by giving up a pawn.[^s1]

## Time in the opening

Capablanca's *Chess Fundamentals* (1921) treats the opening as a race: the main thing is to develop the pieces quickly, and before development is complete no piece should be moved twice unless it wins material or gains freedom of action.[^s2] In his comments on the line 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 Nd7 4.Bc4 h6, he describes Black's slow pawn moves as lost time and the later ...h6 as a forced penalty for them, while White brings out pieces in preference to pawns.[^s2] The positive side of the same idea appears in his chapter on the force of a threatened attack: a threat makes the opponent spend a move defending, which is why he writes that it is always an advantage to threaten something.[^s2] Developing a piece with an attack on an exposed queen is the commonest way of gaining tempi in practice, and moving the same piece several times to escape such attacks is the classic way of losing them.[^s6]

## A concrete example

Capablanca illustrates the point with his game against Blanco at Havana in 1913: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Nd7 5.Nf3 Ngf6 6.Nxf6+ Nxf6 7.Ne5 Bd6 8.Qf3 c6 9.c3 O-O 10.Bg5 Be7 11.Bd3 Ne8 12.Qh3.[^s2] He admits that 7.Ne5 moves an already developed knight a second time, but every later White move comes with a threat, while Black's 8...c6 achieves little and the retreat 10...Be7 undoes the bishop's earlier move; by then, in his judgement, Black has lost too much time.[^s2] In the key position above, after 12.Qh3, the queen bears down on Black's kingside and Black answers with 12...f5, a further pawn move that loosens his position.[^s2]

## Tempi and material

Masters have long tried to price time in material. Siegbert Tarrasch wrote in *Das Schachspiel* (1931) that a pawn can be valued at three tempi, and Rudolf Spielmann's *The Art of Sacrifice in Chess* (1935) gives the same three-for-one rate for open positions; Winter's readers traced the idea back to an 1866 *Deutsche Schachzeitung* article by Eugen von Schmidt.[^s1] Tarrasch also called tempo "the soul of chess" in his own magazine in 1933.[^s1] The rule of thumb explains why gambits work: a pawn given up for three useful developing moves is roughly a fair trade, while a pawn given up for one is usually not. Capablanca adds the necessary caution, showing a game in which Black gives up the exchange to gain time for an attack and the correct reply is to take the material and survive.[^s2]

## Spare tempi in the endgame

In the endgame a tempo is often counted literally. In king and pawn endings the side that can make a harmless waiting move at the right moment forces the opponent to give way, so pawns that have not yet moved are a reserve of tempi; John Nunn's *Nunn's Chess Endings* (Gambit, 2010) has a section devoted to reserve tempi, and in one of its examples a pawn sacrifice wins precisely because it removes the opponent's last reserve tempo.[^s3][^s4] Balogh and Mikhalchishin show the same resource in opposition battles: with a pawn still on its second rank, White can choose between a one-square and a two-square advance and so lose a move to win the opposition, whereas advancing it too early throws the win away.[^s5] one of Nunn's examples is a draw saved "by one tempo", which is why counting moves precisely matters more here than in any other phase.[^s3]

## Typical mistakes

- Moving the same piece repeatedly in the opening, especially the queen, while the opponent develops with threats.[^s2][^s6]
- Making pawn moves that neither develop nor control the centre, then paying for them with further defensive moves.[^s2]
- Spending a reserve pawn move early in a pawn ending, leaving no waiting move for the decisive moment.[^s5]
- Sacrificing material for a single tempo, or for time that cannot be turned into a concrete attack.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Tempo is the unit in which **development** is measured, and a lead in tempi is what allows a player to seize the **initiative**; Capablanca links the two directly, saying the initiative should be kept by developing and by harassing the opponent's development.[^s2] Its exchange rate against material underlies the **gambit** and stands in contrast to plain **material advantage**, a link made explicit by Tarrasch, whose dictum about tempo is the best-known statement of the idea.[^s1] In **king and pawn endgames** tempo turns into the struggle for the opposition and into techniques such as **triangulation**, which exists to lose a move on purpose.[^s3][^s5] The Capablanca–Blanco game, discussed in *Chess Fundamentals*, is a clear model of gaining time through threats.[^s2]

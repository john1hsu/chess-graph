# Chess strategy

Chess strategy is the part of the game concerned with lasting features and long-range aims rather than with the move-by-move working out of variations. Encyclopaedia Britannica divides chess theory into opening knowledge, tactics, positional analysis (above all of the pawn structure), long-range planning and endgame technique; strategy covers the middle three of those concerns in everything but the forcing details.[^s1] Writers have defined it in several ways: Max Euwe saw it as setting an aim and devising schemes, C. J. S. Purdy called it simply the choosing of aims, and Fred Reinfeld described it as the art of building favourable situations that tactics then cash in.[^s2] Edward Lasker's *Chess Strategy* put it as the tasks the game sets the player, with tactics deciding how to accomplish them on the actual board.[^s3]

## How strategic thinking developed

François-André Philidor's *L'Analyze des échecs* (1749) was the first systematic account of how games are won. He urged players to form an overall plan once their pieces were out, to anticipate the opponent's intentions, and to treat pawns as the backbone of the position, warning against isolated, doubled and backward pawns and noting that a bishop's worth depends on how its own fixed pawns hem it in.[^s1]

Wilhelm Steinitz, the first world champion, turned these hints into a theory. In his view a position holds small advantages for both sides that tend to cancel out; an attack is justified only once the balance has tipped, and the stronger side should collect small, durable assets — the bishop pair, a knight anchored on a square no pawn can attack, extra space — and convert them later into material or a decisive attack.[^s1] He also named the **hole**, a square that can no longer be guarded by a pawn, and stressed that pawn weaknesses grow more serious as pieces are exchanged.[^s1]

Siegbert Tarrasch codified Steinitz's ideas into memorable rules while giving more weight to space and mobility, Emanuel Lasker added a practical and psychological dimension, and Capablanca refined the technique of turning tiny edges into wins.[^s1] After the First World War the hypermoderns, led by Aron Nimzowitsch and Richard Réti, argued that the centre could be controlled from a distance rather than occupied, and Nimzowitsch made **prophylaxis**, the restraint of the opponent's plans, a central idea.[^s1] His *My System* (first instalment 1925, English edition 1929) is organised around strategic elements rather than openings: open files, the seventh rank, passed pawns, outposts, pawn chains, and the sequence of restraint, blockade and destruction.[^s5] The Soviet school later prized the initiative and dynamic imbalances, accepting structures earlier masters avoided and giving up the exchange to wreck an opponent's pawns.[^s1]

## The main elements

Classical textbooks single out a fairly stable list of factors:

- **Pawn structure.** Lasker calls the pawns the most permanent element and treats the pawn skeleton formed in the opening as the starting point for the middlegame plan.[^s3]
- **Mobility and the centre.** Lasker argues that a piece's effectiveness depends on its mobility, so equal material does not mean an equal position; control of the central squares gives the pieces room.[^s3] Capablanca likewise regards central control as the usual route to the initiative.[^s4]
- **Initiative and time.** Capablanca advises keeping the initiative as long as possible and giving it up only for material or a lasting positional gain.[^s4]
- **Weaknesses and targets.** Holes, backward and doubled pawns become objects of attack, and the plan is to fix them and pile pressure on them.[^s1][^s3]
- **Attack and defence balance.** Lasker's rule of thumb is to attack only where more force can be brought to bear than the defence can gather.[^s3]

White's knight is anchored on d5, and Black's d6 pawn is backward on a half-open file where White's rook can attack it. Nothing is being won by force, yet White holds exactly the sort of durable advantages Steinitz described — a strong outpost and a fixed weakness — and can build on them slowly, for instance by doubling rooks on the d-file.[^s1] Material is level, which is Lasker's point that counting pieces does not settle who stands better.[^s3]

## Common mistakes

- **Planless moves.** Capablanca warns against moves in the opening that do nothing for development,[^s4] and Britannica notes his insistence that every opening move serve a clear plan.[^s1]
- **Premature attack.** Steinitz's principle is that an attack launched before the balance has shifted tends to rebound on the attacker.[^s1]
- **Ignoring structure when trading.** Because pawn weaknesses matter more as pieces come off, careless exchanges can turn a small structural flaw into a lost endgame.[^s1]
- **Rules as dogma.** The hypermoderns showed that classical rules about the centre were guidelines, not laws.[^s1]

## Connections

Chess strategy **contrasts with** chess tactics, its short-term, calculating counterpart; Purdy and Abrahams both stressed that the two are intertwined in real play.[^s2] It **has parts** such as pawn structure and the outpost, both prominent in Steinitz's and Nimzowitsch's teaching,[^s1][^s5] and is **related** to the blockade, the isolated queen's pawn (which Tarrasch championed for the space and knight posts it gives) and material advantage, the usual result of a strategic edge being converted.[^s1] As a body of ideas it was **developed by** Wilhelm Steinitz and later Aron Nimzowitsch.[^s1][^s5]

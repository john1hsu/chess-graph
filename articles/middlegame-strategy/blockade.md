# Blockade

A blockade is the stopping of an enemy pawn by a piece placed on the square directly in front of it. Nimzowitsch's own definition is the mechanical stopping of an enemy pawn by a piece, and his standard picture is a white knight on d4 facing a black pawn on d5.[^s2] The idea is old in practice, but Nimzowitsch was the first to argue it out as theory: he published *Die Blockade* in 1925, the same year as *My System*, and later collected his blockading games under the heading "From the blockade workshop" in *Chess Praxis*.[^s2][^s3]

## Why blockade instead of just guarding

Nimzowitsch's famous image was that a passed pawn is a criminal to be kept under lock and key. Serper draws the practical lesson: controlling the square in front of the pawn is not enough, a piece should actually stand on it.[^s5] A pawn that is merely attacked can still advance when the guards are distracted; a pawn with a piece in front of it cannot move at all.

*My System* devotes a section of its passed-pawn chapter to blockading, with three separate reasons for it, followed by sections on "the main and secondary functions of the blockading piece" and on the struggle against the blockader.[^s1] In practical terms a blockade does two jobs at once: it takes away the pawn's urge to advance, which Nimzowitsch saw as its main source of strength, and it fixes the pawn so that it can be attacked later.[^s2][^s5] Nimzowitsch also carried the rules over from passed pawns to whole pawn chains in a later chapter.[^s1]

## The best blockaders

The knight is the classic blockader. It is not harmed by standing in front of a pawn, since it jumps over obstacles, and its moves reach the neighbouring files. Kidambi stresses a further point against a protected passed pawn: a knight in front of the pawn also attacks the pawn that protects it, something a bishop or rook on that square cannot do.[^s4] In the illustrative position given in the `fen` (constructed for this article), White's knight on d4 sits in front of Black's isolated d5-pawn, supported by the e3-pawn, and no black pawn can ever drive it away.

Other pieces can blockade, but less comfortably. The king can blockade well in an endgame, where reaching the square in front of the pawn is a basic defensive goal; Nimzowitsch's game against Rubinstein at Breslau 1925 shows a king heading for exactly that frontal post.[^s1] Earlier in the game, however, the king is a poor blockader because it is so sensitive to checks and threats; *My System* shows a blockading king collapsing to a mating attack in a Nuremberg 1904 game.[^s1]

## Fighting a blockade

The side with the pawn tries to remove or overload the blockader. In Nimzowitsch–Alapin, St Petersburg 1913, the black piece on b6 had to be chased away before White's b-pawn could run, and White won soon afterwards.[^s1] Exchanging the blockader, attacking it with pieces, or opening a second front so that it must leave its post are the usual methods.

Against a mass of pawns, Markos lists three defensive approaches: blockade the pawns on the most suitable squares, damage the chain (for example by creating doubled pawns), or slip pieces behind the chain where pawns can no longer attack them.[^s3] He shows Aronian against Vachier-Lagrave in 2013 giving up a bishop rather than lose the key blockading square on f6, and still winning.[^s3]

## Mistakes and limits

Blockading for its own sake can backfire. Nimzowitsch himself gave a double exclamation mark to 6.Ne3 against Behting (Riga 1919), sending the knight to e3 to blockade an e4-pawn instead of developing normally; Kidambi calls the mark exaggerated and doubts the evaluation is wholly correct.[^s4] A blockade also does not win by itself. It must be followed by pressure on the fixed pawn or play elsewhere, which is why the slogan associated with Nimzowitsch runs restrain, blockade, destroy.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

The blockade is a core idea of **chess strategy** and a direct consequence of **pawn structure**: it **counters** the **passed pawn** and is the standard answer to an **isolated queen's pawn**.[^s2][^s5] The blockading square is usually an **outpost**, since no enemy pawn can attack it.[^s4] Nimzowitsch, who **championed** the concept, framed it within restraint and **prophylaxis**, and it became one of the signature ideas of **hypermodernism**.[^s1][^s6]

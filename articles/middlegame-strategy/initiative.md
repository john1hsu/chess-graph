# Initiative

In chess the initiative is the ability to force matters.[^s1] The player who holds it chooses the battleground: each move carries a threat or a demand, and the opponent has to answer it rather than carry out plans of their own. Jeremy Silman stresses that the initiative is not the same thing as an attack on the king. It means setting the agenda so that the other side must react, and he classes it as a *dynamic* plus, in contrast to *static* pluses such as extra material, a better pawn structure or more space.[^s5]

## Where it comes from

The classic source is a lead in development. Britannica notes that superior development in a position with few central pawns gave one player the initiative, and that Paul Morphy treated it as his first priority. Among weaker players the initiative changed hands with every error, but Morphy rarely let it go once he had it, often giving up pawns so that his queen, rooks and bishops could join the attack sooner.[^s2][^s1] Other sources of initiative include open lines towards a target, a pawn break the opponent cannot ignore, and pressure against a weak point that ties the defender's pieces down.[^s5][^s3]

## A temporary advantage

Because it rests on time rather than on permanent features, the initiative is usually transient. Silman warns that it fades once the opponent finishes development or consolidates, so the player who holds it must either strike quickly or convert it into something lasting: an extra pawn, more space, a weakness in the enemy camp or a superior minor piece.[^s5] This is the practical meaning of Wilhelm Steinitz's dictum that a player whose position justifies an attack must attack, or lose the advantage.[^s2]

## Plans and principles

- **Keep the momentum.** Ivan Sokolov's *Sacrifice and Initiative in Chess* devotes its first part to the initiative: when it is at stake, how to grab it, and how to keep it, including by ignoring the opponent's threats in favour of stronger counter-threats.[^s3]
- **Pay with material when necessary.** Sokolov argues that a sacrifice is the natural product of the initiative; giving up a pawn or a piece is often how lines are opened and tempi gained.[^s3]
- **Know when to cash in.** Silman cautions that trading the initiative for material can backfire if the defender neutralises the pressure while keeping the extra material, and that initiative and static advantages often pull in opposite directions; which matters more depends on the position.[^s5]

## Attitudes to the initiative

Schools of thought have differed over its value. Steinitz and Tarrasch were willing to defend solid but passive positions, and Emanuel Lasker showed that cramped positions could still yield strong counterattacking chances.[^s2] The Soviet school, by contrast, placed the initiative above most positional considerations; when defending, its players preferred to generate counterplay, and they sought unbalanced positions that forced both sides to find the best moves.[^s1][^s2]

## Example: Carlsen–Rapport, Wijk aan Zee 2019

In round eight of Tata Steel 2019, after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 e6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nc6 5.Nc3 Qc7 6.g3 a6 7.Bg2 Nf6 8.O-O d6 9.Nxc6 bxc6 10.Na4 Rb8 11.c4 c5 12.b3 Be7 13.Bb2 O-O 14.Qe1 Nd7 15.Rd1 Bb7 16.Qc3 Bf6 17.Qd2 Be7 18.Qc3 Bf6 19.Qd2 Be7 20.f4 e5 21.Bc3 Bc6 22.Ba5 Qb7 23.Nc3 exf4 24.gxf4 Rfe8, Magnus Carlsen played the central break 25.e5 (see `fen`).[^s4][^s6] Sokolov explains it as a pawn sacrifice whose point is to open files for White's rooks; Carlsen was not interested in regaining the pawn, and after 25...Bxg2 26.Qxg2 dxe5 27.Nd5 e4 28.Bc3 the bishop on c3 joined a kingside attack Black could not withstand.[^s4] ChessBase reported that the break clarified matters in White's favour and that Rapport resigned after 40.Rxh7+.[^s6]

## Connections

The initiative is a dynamic element of **chess strategy** that **contrasts with material advantage** and other static pluses.[^s5] It usually **depends on development** and **leads to sacrifices**, which Sokolov calls its natural product.[^s3][^s2] **Paul Morphy** made it his first priority, and the **Soviet school** raised it above most positional factors, while **Steinitz** and **Lasker** framed when an attack is justified and how a cramped defender can fight back.[^s1][^s2] It often turns into a **space advantage** or an attack on **king safety**, and **Carlsen–Rapport, Wijk aan Zee 2019** shows a pawn given up to open lines.[^s5][^s4]

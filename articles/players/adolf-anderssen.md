# Adolf Anderssen

Karl Ernst Adolf Anderssen was born in Breslau, Prussia (now Wrocław, Poland), on 6 July 1818 and died there on 13 March 1879.[^s1] He studied mathematics and philosophy and spent his working life teaching mathematics and German at the Friedrichs-Gymnasium in Breslau, so chess was never his profession.[^s1][^s3] He learned the game as a boy and was largely self-taught from the classic textbooks; from 1846 he also edited one of Germany's first chess magazines.[^s2][^s3] Before turning to tournament play he composed problems, publishing a collection in 1842 and a revised edition in 1852.[^s1][^s3]

## Tournament and match career

Anderssen won London 1851, the first modern international tournament, a knockout event in which he beat Howard Staunton in the semi-final and Marmaduke Wyvill in the final; the victory made him the generally recognised, if unofficial, world champion.[^s1][^s3] In 1858 he travelled to Paris to play Paul Morphy and lost by seven wins to two with two draws.[^s3][^s8] After Morphy withdrew from chess Anderssen was again regarded as the strongest player, a standing confirmed by first place at London 1862.[^s1][^s3][^s4]

That position ended in 1866, when Wilhelm Steinitz challenged him to a match in London, played for £100 a side between 18 July and 10 August with the first to eight wins taking the stakes. Anderssen won seven games, Steinitz eight, and there were no draws.[^s4] Anderssen remained a top competitor for another decade: he won Baden-Baden 1870 with 13/18, half a point ahead of Steinitz, and took first or shared first at events including Aachen 1868, Hamburg 1869, Barmen 1869, Krefeld 1871, Leipzig 1871 and Leipzig 1876.[^s3][^s7] André Schulz counts eleven tournament wins in sixteen starts between 1851 and 1878, his worst result being sixth at Paris 1878, and quotes Jeff Sonas's retrospective estimate of a peak rating of 2744 in 1870.[^s3]

## Style and legacy

Britannica calls Anderssen the greatest of the Romantics: his play aimed at combinations that forced an immediate decision.[^s1] The Columbia Encyclopedia describes him as the prime example of chess's Romantic style, with fierce attacks built on giving up material.[^s2] Asked why he had not sacrificed against Morphy, he reputedly said Morphy would not let him, a remark that captures how an opponent who developed quickly and kept the position under control could blunt his attacks.[^s1] His best-known pupils were Gustav Neumann and Johannes Zukertort, who later contested the 1886 title match with Steinitz.[^s3]

## The Immortal and Evergreen games

Two of his games became the emblems of the age. The Immortal Game, a casual game against Lionel Kieseritzky in London in 1851, ends with mate after White has given up a bishop, both rooks and the queen.[^s2][^s3] The Evergreen Game against Jean Dufresne followed in 1852.[^s3] Edward Winter notes that its earliest known printing is the Deutsche Schachzeitung of September 1852 and that the usual venue, Berlin, cannot be proved from early sources; the name goes back to Steinitz, who in 1879 annotated the game as an evergreen in Anderssen's laurel crown.[^s5] From an Evans Gambit, White sacrifices a knight on f6, then a rook on e7 and the queen on d7, and the two bishops finish the game with mate.[^s6]

The lesson for a club player is less the final fireworks than the build-up: the gambit buys a lead in development and open central files, and the decisive blow lands only once every white piece is aimed at the black king, which is still in the centre.[^s6]

## Connections

Anderssen sits under the **Romantic era**, whose leading figure he was, and is **exemplified by** the **Immortal Game** and the **Evergreen Game**.[^s1][^s5] He **played in** London 1851 and Baden-Baden 1870, the two tournament wins that bracket his peak.[^s3][^s7] He is related to **Paul Morphy**, who beat him in 1858, to **Wilhelm Steinitz**, who took his place at the top in 1866, and to his pupil **Johannes Zukertort**.[^s3][^s4][^s8] The **Evans Gambit**, **combination** and **sacrifice** nodes describe the opening and the methods at the heart of his best-known wins.[^s1][^s6]

# Mikhail Botvinnik

Mikhail Moiseyevich Botvinnik was born on 17 August 1911 (4 August Old Style) in Kuokkala (now Repino, Russia) and died in Moscow on 5 May 1995.[^s1] He is counted as the sixth world chess champion and held the title three times, in 1948–1957, 1958–1960 and 1961–1963.[^s1][^s3] He was inducted into the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2003.[^s2]

## Career

Botvinnik took up the game during the Soviet chess boom of the mid-1920s and became a master at sixteen.[^s3] At fourteen, less than two years after learning the moves, he beat the reigning world champion José Raúl Capablanca in a simultaneous exhibition.[^s1] He won the Soviet championship for the first time in 1931 and went on to take seven national titles in all, while also qualifying as an electrical engineer at the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute in 1932.[^s1] Strong results in international tournaments of the late 1930s put him among the world elite, but two planned title matches against Alexander Alekhine fell through, the first because of the Second World War and the second because Alekhine died in 1946.[^s3]

With the title vacant, a match-tournament between the world's leading players was held in 1948 to choose Alekhine's successor, and Botvinnik won it to become champion.[^s1][^s3] He lost the crown to Vasily Smyslov in 1957 and to Mikhail Tal in 1960, but won it back in a return match each time, in 1958 and 1961.[^s2][^s3] His final reign ended with a loss to Tigran Petrosian in 1963, after which he no longer competed for the title, although he kept playing major tournaments until retiring from competition in 1970.[^s1][^s2] He also played for the Soviet Olympiad team from 1954 to 1964, and the team took gold each time.[^s3]

## Style and method

Sources describe his play as methodical and rational rather than primarily intuitive.[^s1] The Hall of Fame stresses his insistence on logic, deep theoretical study and physical as well as mental conditioning, and an approach that treated whole systems of play, from opening through endgame, as one connected subject.[^s2] He passed this approach on as a teacher: he is known as the patriarch of the Soviet chess school, mentored many young Soviet players including Garry Kasparov, and influenced a generation that included Anatoly Karpov.[^s1][^s2][^s3] He wrote many books, among them a collection of his own best games published in 1951 and *Half a Century of Chess* (1984).[^s1][^s2]

## Example: AVRO 1938

His best-known win is against Capablanca at the AVRO tournament in the Netherlands in 1938, a Nimzo-Indian in which White accepted doubled c-pawns to build a kingside attack with e4–e5 and f4–f5.[^s2][^s4] In the key position (FEN above), White's passed pawn on e6 and active queen are worth more than material: Botvinnik played 30.Ba3!, offering the bishop simply to deflect Black's queen from guarding e7 and f6, and after 30...Qxa3 31.Nh5+ gxh5 32.Qg5+ the e-pawn ran through and Capablanca resigned on move 41.[^s4] The game is a model of how a pawn-structure concession in the opening can be repaid by central dynamism.

## Computer chess

After 1963 Botvinnik turned increasingly to computer chess, aiming at an "electronic grandmaster".[^s3] His research favoured highly selective search, choosing a few promising moves by general principles rather than searching every line, an idea suited to the weak Soviet hardware of the time but overtaken once machines could search far more broadly.[^s3][^s5] His team stood apart from the brute-force Soviet program Kaissa, and his planning ideas were also applied to scheduling power-station maintenance.[^s3][^s5]

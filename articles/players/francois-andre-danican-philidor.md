# François-André Danican Philidor

François-André Danican Philidor was born in Dreux, France, on 7 September 1726 and died in London on 31 August 1795.[^s1][^s5] Edward Winter notes that older works, including Murray's *History of Chess*, gave a wrong death date of 24 August, and that contemporary newspapers support 31 August.[^s5] He came from a prominent family of court musicians and led a double career: alongside chess he was one of the leading French opera composers of his day, best known for *Tom Jones* (1765).[^s1][^s2][^s4] This node covers his chess career only.

## Learning the game and early fame

Philidor learned chess from musicians of the royal chapel at Versailles, where he served as a boy, and in Paris became a regular at the Café de la Régence, whose leading player, François Antoine de Légal, became his teacher.[^s4] A failed concert tour in the mid-1740s left him stranded in the Netherlands, and he turned to playing chess for money.[^s1][^s4] The Gale *Encyclopedia of World Biography* reports that in 1744 a display against two opponents at once made him famous enough to be mentioned in Diderot's *Encyclopédie*.[^s2]

In 1747 he moved to London and beat Philipp Stamma, the strongest player active there, at Old Slaughter's Coffee House.[^s4] Gale gives the result as eight wins, one loss and one draw in a ten-game match played on special conditions concerning draws.[^s2] In 1755 he also beat his old teacher Légal in Paris.[^s2][^s4] From the Stamma match until his death he was regarded as the best player in the world, though no formal title existed.[^s2][^s3]

## L'Analyze des Echecs

Philidor wrote his treatise in the Netherlands and published it in London in 1749 as *L'Analyze des Echecs*; it was soon translated into English and German, and a revised second edition appeared in 1777, dedicated to the members of his London club.[^s2][^s6] The Columbia Encyclopedia describes it as the first manual to explain openings, and stresses its attention to the middlegame, to pawns and to overall strategy.[^s3] Its most famous line, on page xix of the 1749 edition, calls the pawns "l'ame des Echecs", the soul of chess, and Winter shows that later French editions of 1752 and 1754 repeat it.[^s6]

The idea behind the phrase is that pawns, moving forward together and supported by pieces, decide the shape of the game, rather than serving as material to be sacrificed for a quick attack.[^s2] Gale reads the book as a textbook of the pawn as the basis of a successful attack, and Schulz calls it a textbook of positional play, which is why later writers treat Philidor as a forerunner of positional chess.[^s2][^s4]

## Blindfold play and London career

Philidor was the outstanding blindfold player of the eighteenth century. In Berlin, around his 1750 visit to Frederick the Great's court, he gave displays on three boards at once that the local press reported.[^s4] Winter cites Philidor's own letter of March 1790 describing a London exhibition of three games, two played without sight of the board, both of which he won, and a further blindfold display at Parsloe's coffee house in February 1794.[^s5] Gale adds that later advertised displays reportedly went up to four opponents.[^s2]

From 1774 Philidor spent a season each year as resident master of the London Chess Club at Parsloe's in St James's Street, giving lessons; the club eventually paid him a pension.[^s1][^s2][^s4] One of his best-known games is a win over Count Hans Moritz von Brühl played at odds, with Philidor giving a knight against a pawn.[^s1][^s3] He also played and, according to Schulz, easily beat von Kempelen's chess-playing automaton, the Turk, in Paris.[^s4] Winter records several myths about him, including a claimed blindfold display dated 1816, two decades after his death.[^s5]

## Example: the Philidor Defence

The name survives in openings and endings. The Philidor Defence, ECO C41, begins:

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6[^s7]

Black protects e5 with a pawn rather than a piece, a solid but somewhat passive way of holding the centre. Winter notes, however, that the origin of the name is unknown, and that the often-repeated claim that Philidor never played it goes back to Chernev's books.[^s5]

## Connections

Philidor sits under the **history of chess** as the dominant player between the Italian masters and the nineteenth-century tournament era.[^s2][^s3] He was **influenced by** his teacher **François Antoine de Légal**.[^s4] He is related to **pawn structure**, whose importance his book first made central, and to **blindfold chess**, which he popularised in public exhibitions.[^s2][^s5][^s6] Three nodes carry his name: the **Philidor position**, a drawing method in rook endgames; the **smothered mate** pattern known as **Philidor's legacy**, a term Winter shows first appeared in print in 1804; and the **Philidor Defence**.[^s3][^s5][^s7]

# Anatoly Karpov

Anatoly Yevgenyevich Karpov was born on 23 May 1951 in Zlatoust in the Urals and grew up in Leningrad; he learned the game at four and held a first-category rating by nine.[^s1] He won the European Junior Championship in 1967 and the World Junior Championship in Stockholm in 1969, and in 1970, at nineteen, he became a grandmaster.[^s1][^s2] He is counted as the twelfth world champion and was inducted into the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2004.[^s2][^s3]

## Road to the title

After winning Moscow's 1971 Alekhine Memorial and sharing second in the 1973 USSR Championship, Karpov qualified through the 1973 Leningrad Interzonal for the 1974 Candidates matches.[^s2][^s3] There he beat Lev Polugaevsky (three wins, five draws), former champion Boris Spassky (four wins to one) and Viktor Korchnoi (three wins to two, with nineteen draws).[^s3] That made him Bobby Fischer's challenger, but Fischer refused to play under the conditions FIDE set, and in 1975 Karpov was named world champion without a match.[^s1][^s2]

## Champion, 1975–1985

During his decade as champion Karpov was, in the Hall of Fame's words, a constant and dominant presence on the international tournament circuit.[^s2] He defended the crown twice against Korchnoi, narrowly at Baguio in 1978 and comfortably at Merano in 1981.[^s1][^s3] In 1984 he met Garry Kasparov and took a commanding early lead, but Kasparov fought back and the match was halted after 48 games with both players exhausted.[^s1] Karpov lost the 1985 rematch over 24 games, and failed to regain it in three further matches against Kasparov in 1986, 1987 and 1990, each decided by a narrow margin; in all, the two played five matches and 167 games between 1984 and 1990.[^s2][^s3]

## FIDE champion again, 1993–1999

When Kasparov left FIDE in 1993, FIDE stripped him of its title and Karpov won it back by beating Jan Timman.[^s1] He defended the FIDE title in 1996 and against Viswanathan Anand in 1998.[^s1][^s2] In 1999 FIDE moved to a knockout championship in Las Vegas; Karpov declined to take part, Alexander Khalifman won it, and Karpov has since played mainly rapid and exhibition events.[^s1][^s2] He also won the first FIDE-recognised rapid championship, in Mexico in 1988.[^s3]

## Record and style

The Hall of Fame credits Karpov with more than 160 first places, perhaps the best tournament record in the game's history, a peak Elo rating of 2780 and 90 months as the world's top-rated player.[^s2] His chess was positional rather than tactical: he built small advantages through precise, undramatic manoeuvring and was known for stamina.[^s1] Commentators collected by FIDE describe the same thing from different angles: Tal compared him to a tank that cannot be turned from its goal, Kasparov praised his subtle positional sense, persistence and practicality, and Kramnik observed that Karpov's advantages often grew while he declined to attack, against Steinitz's rule that the side with the advantage must attack.[^s3] Karpov himself remarked that he liked 1.e4 but scored better with 1.d4.[^s3]

## Example: Karpov–Unzicker, Nice Olympiad 1974

On top board for the Soviet Union against West Germany in the 1974 Nice Olympiad final, Karpov had White against Wolfgang Unzicker in a closed Ruy Lopez (ECO C98).[^s4][^s5] Black's ...c4 fixed the queenside, and White answered with a slow build-up: the a-file was opened, a rook doubled on it, and in the `fen` position above (after 24.Ba7) the bishop sits on a7 to stop Black from contesting the file with ...Ra8.[^s4] GM Rafael Leitão calls 24.Ba7 perhaps Karpov's most famous move and stresses that Black has no clear plan afterwards.[^s4] Karpov then turned to the kingside with f4–f5, exchanged light-squared bishops on f7 and brought his queen and knights to g6 and h5; Black resigned after 44.Nh5.[^s5] Unzicker reportedly complained afterwards that his knight could not jump out of its cage.[^s5] The lesson for club players is prophylaxis: take away the opponent's only active plan first, then open a second front.

## Connections

Karpov sits under the **world chess championship** and **holds the titles** of world champion and **grandmaster**.[^s2] He is bound to **Bobby Fischer**, from whom he received the title, to **Viktor Korchnoi**, his opponent in three matches, and above all to **Garry Kasparov**, with whom he contested five title matches.[^s1][^s3] He is a product of the **Soviet chess school** and was the leading Soviet board at the **Chess Olympiad** in Nice.[^s5] His style is a model of **prophylaxis**, and the **Ruy Lopez** and **Caro-Kann Defence** articles both name him a leading exponent of those openings; the Nice game, a closed Ruy Lopez, **exemplifies** his approach.[^s4]

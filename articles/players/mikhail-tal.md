# Mikhail Tal

Mikhail Nekhemyevich Tal was born in Riga, Latvia, on 9 November 1936 and died in Moscow on 28 June 1992.[^s1] He is counted as the eighth world chess champion, holding the title from 1960 to 1961, and was inducted into the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2003.[^s2][^s3] The Hall of Fame calls him "the fiercest attacking player ever to hold the title".[^s2]

## Career

Tal learned chess at six, became Latvian champion and a national master in 1953 at sixteen, and earned the grandmaster title in 1957.[^s1] That year he also won the first of his six USSR championships, the others coming in 1958, 1967, 1972, 1974 and 1978.[^s1][^s2] His rise to the top was unusually fast: he won the 1958 Interzonal in Yugoslavia and then the 1959 Candidates Tournament there, earning a match with Mikhail Botvinnik.[^s1][^s2]

In the 1960 match in Moscow Tal won 12½–8½, with six wins, two losses and thirteen draws, and at 23 became the youngest world champion up to that time.[^s1][^s4] In the rematch a year later Tal, in poor health, lost 13–8, with five wins against Botvinnik's ten.[^s1][^s5] His one-year reign was long the shortest in the title's history.[^s3]

He never regained the crown but stayed among the world's best for decades. He played in six Candidates tournaments, losing the 1965 Candidates final to Boris Spassky, and represented the Soviet Union in eight Olympiads, winning team gold each time.[^s2][^s3] From October 1973 to October 1974 he went 95 games without a loss (46 wins and 49 draws), a streak that stood as the record for many years; the Hall of Fame credits him with the two longest unbeaten runs in competitive chess.[^s2][^s3] In 1988, at 51, he won the world blitz championship in Saint John, Canada, finishing ahead of Garry Kasparov.[^s1][^s3] Britannica also rates him among the best and wittiest writers of the world champions; his books include *Life and Games of Mikhail Tal* and a book on his 1960 match with Botvinnik.[^s1]

## Style

Tal's fame rests on combinational chess full of sacrifices, played to seize the initiative and pose problems that were hard to solve over the board, even when they could not be fully justified by calculation.[^s1][^s3] The FIDE Museum adds that his quiet moves could be as strong as his sacrifices, and the Hall of Fame notes that from the 1970s his play became more positional while keeping its imagination.[^s2][^s3] Spassky recalled that when he pointed out a Tal sacrifice did not work, Tal agreed but played it anyway.[^s3]

For a club player the lesson is about practical chances rather than soundness: a sacrifice that keeps pieces active and the position complicated can pay off against a defender short of time and certainty. The danger is copying the risk without the calculation and judgement that stood behind it; many of Tal's attacks were built on precise follow-up and good endgame technique once the smoke cleared.[^s3][^s7]

## Example: sixth game, 1960

In the sixth game of the 1960 match, Botvinnik played a fianchetto against Tal's King's Indian (ECO E69).[^s4] On move 21 Tal left a knight to be taken on f4, gaining open lines and an advanced passed pawn instead of material.[^s6][^s7] Copeland notes that Botvinnik missed a better defence and that his later queen trade left Tal a pawn up with the bishop pair, which Tal converted.[^s7]

## Connections

Tal sits under the **world chess championship** and **holds the title** of world champion, which he took from **Mikhail Botvinnik** in 1960 and returned to him in 1961.[^s1][^s5] He **played in** the **Candidates tournament**, winning in 1959, and in the **Chess Olympiad** as a member of eight gold-medal Soviet teams, part of the **Soviet chess school**.[^s2][^s3] His play is a byword for the **sacrifice** and the **combination** used to keep the **initiative**.[^s3] The sixth game of the 1960 match **exemplifies** that approach.[^s6][^s7]

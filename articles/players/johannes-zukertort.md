# Johannes Zukertort

Johannes Hermann Zukertort was born in Lublin, then in Russian Poland, on 7 September 1842 and died in London on 20 June 1888.[^s2][^s4] The World Chess Hall of Fame, which inducted him in 2016, ranks him as the second-best player in the world, after Wilhelm Steinitz, from about 1871 to 1886.[^s1][^s4] He learned the game at about nineteen in Breslau, studied Bilguer's handbook, and in 1862 beat Adolf Anderssen at knight odds; by the late 1860s he was among the strongest players in Germany.[^s2][^s3] In 1872 he settled in England, where he spent the rest of his life as a professional player and chess writer.[^s2][^s3]

## A life wrapped in legend

Zukertort's biography is unusually hard to pin down. The 1906 Jewish Encyclopedia states that he qualified in medicine at Breslau and served in the Prussian army, but the Encyclopaedia Judaica says no evidence supports these stories, nor claims of a noble mother or tiger hunting in India.[^s2][^s3] Edward Winter has shown how such boasts were repeated for decades on the strength of faulty newspaper citations, and that several remain unconfirmed.[^s6] The chess record, by contrast, is well documented.

## Matches and tournaments

Zukertort lost a first match to Anderssen in 1868 but won the return in 1871, five wins to two.[^s2][^s4] Shortly after arriving in London he played Steinitz in 1872 and was beaten heavily, with one win against seven losses.[^s4] He then built a strong match record: wins over Samuel Rosenthal in 1880 and over Joseph Henry Blackburne in 1881, seven wins to two.[^s2][^s4] In tournaments he took third at London 1872, first at the Paris Exhibition tournament of 1878 after a play-off with Szymon Winawer, and second at Berlin 1881 behind Blackburne.[^s2][^s4]

His greatest success was London 1883, a double round-robin against most of the world's elite. He won 22 games and finished three points ahead of Steinitz, with Blackburne third.[^s1][^s4] He had the event virtually decided with weeks to spare, then collapsed and lost his last three games; contemporary and later accounts disagree about the cause.[^s2][^s3][^s4] The result set up a match with Steinitz for the title of world champion, played in New York, Saint Louis and New Orleans in 1886. Zukertort won four of the first five games but was outplayed afterwards and lost 12½–7½, ten wins to five with five draws.[^s1][^s2][^s4] His later results were mediocre, and in 1888 he suffered a stroke while playing at Simpson's Divan in London and died the next day.[^s3][^s4]

## Writer, editor and blindfold player

Zukertort was a prolific chess journalist. In Germany he edited the Neue Berliner Schachzeitung, first with Anderssen and then alone, and co-edited the second edition of Dufresne's handbook.[^s2] In England he co-founded and co-edited the Chess Monthly with Leopold Hoffer from 1879 until his death.[^s1][^s4] Encyclopedias stress his remarkable memory, and the Jewish Encyclopedia regarded blindfold play as his greatest strength; he is credited with displays of up to fifteen simultaneous blindfold games.[^s2][^s3][^s4] Several opening lines carry his name, most prominently 1.Nf3, which he favoured as a move-order route to 1.d4 positions.[^s4]

## Zukertort versus Blackburne, London 1883

His best-known game, played on 5 May 1883, became one of the most celebrated attacking wins of the century.[^s3][^s5] From a quiet queen's-pawn structure, White sends his kingside pawns forward, opens the f-file and then sacrifices the queen with 28.Qb4, after which a rook check on f8 and a bishop sweep through e5 break Black's defence.[^s5] Winter notes that the winning 31.Bxe5+ was widely praised, but that a forced mate starting with 31.Rg8+ was published in 1957, a point many later books ignore.[^s5]

For a club player the instructive part is the preparation: while Black doubles rooks on the c-file, White lines up the long bishop on b2, the f-pawn lever and the rook lift to e3 against the king, so the queen sacrifice lands with every piece already in place.[^s5]

## Connections

Zukertort sits under **history of chess** and **played in** the **World Chess Championship 1886** and **London 1883**, the two events that define his place in the game.[^s1][^s4] He is **exemplified by** his win over Blackburne at London 1883.[^s5] He is related to **Wilhelm Steinitz**, his great rival; to his teacher **Adolf Anderssen**; to **Joseph Henry Blackburne**, his frequent opponent; to **blindfold chess**, his specialty; and to the **Zukertort Opening** (1.Nf3) that bears his name.[^s1][^s2][^s4]

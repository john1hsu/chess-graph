# Bobby Fischer

Robert James "Bobby" Fischer was born in Chicago on 9 March 1943 and died in Reykjavík on 17 January 2008.[^s1] He learned the game at six, left high school at sixteen to play full time, and became the eleventh world champion, holding the title from 1972 to 1975.[^s1][^s2] He was the first native-born American world champion, and his victory was the first by a player from outside the Soviet Union in 24 years.[^s1][^s2]

## Early career and US dominance

Fischer first drew wide attention in October 1956, at thirteen, with his win as Black over Donald Byrne in the Third Rosenwald Trophy tournament in New York.[^s6] He won the US Open in 1957 and then the 1957/58 US Championship, becoming its youngest winner, and in 1958 he became the youngest grandmaster in history up to that time.[^s1][^s2] He won all eight US Championships he entered; in 1963/64 he scored 11 wins from 11 games, the only perfect score in the event's history.[^s1][^s2]

## The 1970–72 run

Fischer qualified for the 1971 Candidates matches from the Palma de Mallorca Interzonal of 1970.[^s3] He then beat Mark Taimanov 6–0 in Moscow in May 1971 and Bent Larsen 6–0 in Denver in July, winning every game of both matches.[^s3][^s4] Across the 1970–71 qualifying events he won 20 consecutive games, an all-time record.[^s2] In the final, played in Buenos Aires from September to October 1971, he lost the second game to former champion Tigran Petrosian but then won four in a row to finish 6½–2½ after nine games.[^s5] Afterwards his rating stood 125 points above that of the champion Boris Spassky, the widest such gap on record.[^s2]

In 1972 he beat Spassky 12½–8½ in Reykjavík, a match often described as the most famous of modern times; his share of the $250,000 purse was $156,000.[^s1][^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame stresses the contrast between Fischer's largely self-funded preparation and the state-supported Soviet school.[^s2]

## Forfeit and later play

In 1975 Fischer refused to meet the challenger Anatoly Karpov, and FIDE declared Karpov champion by default.[^s1] He then withdrew from competitive chess for almost twenty years. In 1992 he played a privately organised rematch against Spassky in Sveti Stefan, Yugoslavia, and won; it was not an official championship match.[^s1][^s2]

## Style and contributions

Fischer was known for deep opening preparation, precise endgame technique and lasting contributions to opening theory.[^s2] As White he almost always opened 1.e4, and Britannica notes that his wins often came from sudden attacks or counter-attacks built on positionally sound play.[^s1] His book *My 60 Memorable Games* (1969) is regarded as one of the classics of chess literature.[^s2]

## Example: the "Game of the Century"

The Byrne game, played on 17 October 1956, began 1.Nf3 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.d4 O-O 5.Bf4 d5 (ECO D92).[^s2][^s6] After 16.Bc5 Rfe8+ 17.Kf1 Black left his queen attacked with 17...Be6 (the position in the `fen` above).[^s6] The point is that White's king is stuck in the centre with the h1 rook undeveloped: after 18.Bxb6 Bxc4+ Black's minor pieces delivered a long series of checks that won back rook, bishop and pawns, and the bishops and knight later drove the king into a mating net, ending with 41...Rc2 mate.[^s6] The lesson is that piece activity against an exposed king can outweigh a large material deficit. Hans Kmoch used the title "Game of the Century" for the game in *Chess Review* in December 1956.[^s6]

## Connections

Fischer sits under the **world chess championship**, **holds the titles** of world champion and **grandmaster**, and **played in** the **1972 Fischer–Spassky match** and the **US Championship**.[^s1][^s2] He is linked to **Tigran Petrosian** and **Boris Spassky**, whom he beat in 1971 and 1972, and to **Anatoly Karpov**, who received the title when he declined to defend it.[^s1][^s5] His win over Byrne **exemplifies** his early attacking style.[^s6] He is also linked to **US Chess**, the federation of the championships he dominated, and to **Chess960** (Fischer Random Chess), whose own article records him as its developer.

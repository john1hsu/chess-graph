# Vladimir Kramnik

Vladimir Kramnik is a Russian grandmaster, born on 25 June 1975 in Tuapse, Russia (then in the USSR), who became the fourteenth world chess champion.[^s1][^s3] He learned the game from his father at four, took lessons at a Pioneers club from five, and reached the Soviet "candidate master" level at eleven; FIDE's museum notes that he was trained in the Botvinnik chess school.[^s1][^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame, which inducted him in 2024, records that he was ranked in the world top ten from his late teens until he retired at 43.[^s3]

## Rise to the elite

Kramnik won the world under-18 championship in 1991, and in 1992, aged 17, he made his debut for Russia at the Manila Olympiad, earning a gold medal for his individual performance.[^s1][^s2] Through the 1990s he won a long string of international tournaments, including the 1998 event at Wijk aan Zee, and in January 1996 he reached number one on the FIDE rating list.[^s1][^s2][^s7]

## World champion (2000–2007)

In October and November 2000 Kramnik played Garry Kasparov for the classical title at the Riverside Studios in London, in a match scheduled for sixteen games.[^s4] He won 8½–6½ without losing a game: two wins (games 2 and 10, both with White) and thirteen draws.[^s3][^s4][^s9] His key weapon was the Berlin Defence to the Ruy Lopez, a rare line at the time; Kasparov met it in games 1, 3, 9 and 13 and could not break it, and ChessBase credits it with blunting both Kasparov's attacking style and his computer-assisted preparation.[^s4] The Hall of Fame singles out this rehabilitation of the Berlin as his signature theoretical contribution.[^s3] Because FIDE ran its own championship cycle at the time, the match was not recognised by FIDE.[^s2]

He defended the title in Brissago, Switzerland, in 2004 against Peter Leko. Leko led after game 8, but Kramnik won the fourteenth and final game to tie the match 7–7, which was enough for the champion to keep the title.[^s6] In 2006 he met FIDE champion Veselin Topalov in Elista to reunify the title. The twelve classical games ended 6–6, and on 13 October Kramnik won the rapid tiebreak by 2½–1½ to become undisputed champion.[^s5][^s2]

Kramnik then accepted a tournament format for the next championship. Viswanathan Anand won the 2007 World Championship tournament ahead of him, and Kramnik also lost his 2008 title match against Anand.[^s1][^s2]

## Later career and retirement

He remained in the elite after losing the title. In the 2013 Candidates Tournament in London he scored 8½ points to share first with Magnus Carlsen but finished second on tiebreak.[^s2] On 29 January 2019, during the Tata Steel tournament in the Netherlands, he announced the end of his professional career, saying he wanted to work on chess projects for children and education while still playing occasional rapid, blitz and promotional events.[^s7] On the February 2019 FIDE list he stood at 2753, down from 2777 before that event.[^s8] FIDE's museum page (accessed November 2026) describes him as retired from classical chess but still playing some rapid and blitz.[^s2]

## Style and legacy

Kramnik is usually described as a deep, solid positional player whose match strategy was built on preparation. After the London match Kasparov said Kramnik knew exactly where he could go wrong, and Anand has called him one of the most influential players of the era.[^s2] His revival of the Berlin endgame changed elite opening practice: what had been a sideline became a standard way for Black to defuse 1.e4.[^s3][^s4]

## Example: Kramnik–Kasparov, London 2000, game 2

Kramnik's first win of the match came against Kasparov's Grünfeld Defence (ECO D85). White's rook took the b7-pawn, the queens came off on move 26, and the resulting rook-and-bishop endgame with an extra queenside pawn proved decisive; Kasparov resigned after 40.Bd5, putting Kramnik 1½–½ ahead.[^s4][^s9][^s10]

## Connections

Kramnik sits under the **World Chess Championship** and **holds the titles** of world champion and **grandmaster**; his predecessor and successor as champion were **Garry Kasparov** and **Viswanathan Anand**.[^s2][^s3] He **played in** the **Chess Olympiad** (Manila 1992) and the **Candidates Tournament** (London 2013), and he belongs to the **Soviet chess school** tradition through his Botvinnik-school training.[^s1][^s2] His name is tied to the **Berlin Defence** of the **Ruy Lopez**, and he announced his retirement during the **Tata Steel Chess Tournament**.[^s3][^s7] The game above **exemplifies** his match play.[^s4][^s10]

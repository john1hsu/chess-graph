# Howard Staunton

Howard Staunton (1810–1874) was an English player, author and editor who dominated chess in the 1840s and did more than anyone of his generation to organise the game in Britain.[^s1][^s2] He was a late starter: he settled in London in 1836 and only began playing seriously at about 26, yet within some seven years he was the leading player in Europe.[^s1][^s5] The World Chess Hall of Fame inducted him in 2016.[^s1]

## Rise to the top

Staunton's early record was modest. He lost a match to Aaron Alexandre in 1838, beat H. W. Popert in 1840 and played a long series of games against John Cochrane in the early 1840s, coming out well ahead.[^s4][^s5] The Columbia Encyclopedia dates his recognition as unofficial world champion to the Cochrane series of 1843.[^s2] The decisive step came against the French champion Pierre Charles Fournier de Saint-Amant. Saint-Amant won a short first match in London in 1843, but in the return at Paris late that year Staunton won the race to eleven wins by eleven to six, with four draws, after leading 10–3 and then losing three games in a row.[^s4][^s6] The result ended the long French supremacy centred on the Café de la Régence.[^s5] A third match planned for 1844 was called off when Staunton fell seriously ill.[^s4]

In 1846 he beat Bernhard Horwitz by 14 wins to 7 with 3 draws, and he also defeated Daniel Harrwitz.[^s4][^s5] Sources differ on exact scores for some of his minor matches, so the major results are the safer guide.[^s4][^s5]

## London 1851 and after

Staunton was the main organiser of London 1851, held during the Great Exhibition from 26 May to 12 July and generally regarded as the first international tournament.[^s1][^s6] He raised a prize fund of about £500 from clubs at home and abroad.[^s6] As a player it went badly: Adolf Anderssen beat him 4–1 in the semi-final, and Elijah Williams then beat him for third place.[^s5][^s6] Anderssen's win ended Staunton's standing as the strongest player, and his playing strength seems to have declined afterwards.[^s2][^s4] He wrote the tournament book, published in 1852.[^s4]

In 1858 Paul Morphy toured Europe hoping for a match, but negotiations came to nothing. Staunton cited his work on an edition of Shakespeare, and whether he was also wary of losing may never be settled; the episode was controversial at the time and still colours his reputation.[^s2][^s4][^s6]

## Writer, editor and lawgiver

Staunton's lasting influence came through print. He owned and edited the *Chess Player's Chronicle* from 1840 until 1854, and from 1845 until his death wrote the chess column of the *Illustrated London News*.[^s1][^s2][^s4] *The Chess-Player's Handbook* (1847) became his best-known work and ran to 21 editions, the last in 1939; it was followed by *The Chess-Player's Companion* (1849) and *Chess Praxis* (1860).[^s1][^s4][^s5] He also worked to standardise the rules across countries, and after being asked to review the laws at an 1853 chess association meeting he produced a code that became the English standard.[^s1][^s4]

## Openings and style

Staunton is remembered as a theorist with a positional outlook unusual for the Romantic era.[^s2][^s5] He was willing to fianchetto bishops and to play flank openings, which later writers see as foreshadowing hypermodern ideas.[^s5] His use of 1.c4 against Saint-Amant is the origin of the name English Opening, and the gambit against the Dutch Defence, 1.d4 f5 2.e4, carries his name as the Staunton Gambit.[^s2][^s4] British Chess News still rates it a fully playable gambit against the Dutch.[^s5] The idea is easy to grasp: White offers the e-pawn to strike at f5 at once, aiming for quick development and play against a kingside that Black has already loosened.

## The Staunton chessmen

The standard tournament design bears his name, but he did not design it. Nathaniel Cooke registered the pattern on 1 March 1849, and John Jaques of London made the first sets.[^s3] Staunton promoted it in his column from September 1849, and each set came with his booklet, *The Chess Player's Text Book*.[^s3][^s6] By the late nineteenth century it was the prevailing design, and from the 1920s the only one used in major tournaments.[^s3]

## Connections

Staunton sits under **history of chess** and **played in** **London 1851**, the tournament he organised.[^s1][^s6] He is related to **Adolf Anderssen**, who beat him there, and to **Paul Morphy**, the match that never happened.[^s4][^s6] The **Staunton chess set** and the **English Opening** carry his name, and his positional, flank-opening ideas set him apart from the **Romantic era** and point ahead to **hypermodernism**.[^s2][^s3][^s5]

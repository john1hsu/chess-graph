# Wilhelm Steinitz

Wilhelm (later William) Steinitz was born in Prague on 14 May 1836 and died in New York on 12 August 1900.[^s1] He learned the game at about twelve, went to Vienna to study at the Polytechnic Institute in 1858, and soon gave up his studies to play chess full-time.[^s1] In Vienna he made his name in the city's chess society and became the leading player of the Austrian Empire, which he represented at the London international tournament of 1862; he then settled in England.[^s4] In 1883 he moved to the United States and made New York his base, where he did much to develop American chess.[^s1][^s2]

## Career and championship

Steinitz won every serious match he played between 1862 and 1892, including matches against Adolf Anderssen and Johannes Zukertort.[^s2] His 1866 victory over Anderssen, then regarded as the world's strongest player, gave him a claim to be the best in the world, though he did not make a formal claim to the title until 1886.[^s1][^s4] That year's match against Zukertort, played from 11 January to 29 March in New York, Saint Louis and New Orleans, was the first official World Championship. The winner was the first to ten wins, and Steinitz recovered from an early deficit to win ten games, lose five and draw five.[^s5][^s3]

He defended the title three times: against Mikhail Chigorin in Havana in 1889 (10½–6½), against Isidor Gunsberg in New York in 1890–91 (10½–8½) and against Chigorin again in Havana in 1892 (12½–10½).[^s3] In 1894 he lost the title to Emanuel Lasker in a match played in New York, Philadelphia and Montreal, and the 1896–97 rematch in Moscow went to Lasker by 12½–4½.[^s3][^s1] Lasker went on to study and extend Steinitz's ideas.[^s4]

## Ideas and influence

As a young player Steinitz was a sharp attacker who sacrificed freely, but he came to argue that sound development and planning, not attack at any price, were the basis of correct play.[^s4][^s2] In Andrew Soltis's account for Britannica, Steinitz held that the game starts in balance and tends towards a draw with correct play, so an attack is justified only once the opponent has upset that balance, and a player who fails to attack when it is justified will lose the advantage.[^s6] Instead of chasing a lead in development, he gathered small, lasting gains, such as outposts, the bishop pair, extra space and a better pawn structure, that could be carried into the endgame and converted there. He coined the term "hole" for a square that can no longer be protected by a pawn.[^s6]

His style was as unusual as his theory. He was considered the first master of defence, ready to accept cramped positions, let opponents overextend, and then punish a premature attack.[^s6][^s1] He set out his views in print, notably in *The Modern Chess Instructor* (1889), and is widely seen as the first player to treat chess as a science. With Paul Morphy he is often credited with laying the foundations of modern chess.[^s4][^s6][^s2]

## A concrete example

Steinitz could still attack when the balance tipped. Against Curt von Bardeleben in round 10 at Hastings on 17 August 1895, an Italian Game (ECO C54), White gave up the d-pawn with 17.d5 to open lines towards Black's uncastled king. The knight then hopped via d4 and e6 to g5, and the rook went to e7 and began a series of checks along the seventh rank.[^s7][^s2] The diagrammed position, after 25.Rxh7+, is from the tournament book's score, which records the game as a win for White.[^s7]

## Connections

Steinitz sits under the **World Chess Championship**, whose official line he began in 1886 against **Johannes Zukertort**, and he **holds the title** of world champion from 1886 to 1894.[^s5][^s3] His matches link him to **Adolf Anderssen**, the leading attacking player of the previous generation, to **Mikhail Chigorin**, whom he beat twice, and to **Emanuel Lasker**, who took the title from him and built on his teaching.[^s2][^s3][^s4] His ideas about holes and strong squares lead directly to the **outpost** concept, and his Hastings 1895 win over von Bardeleben is a classic **example** of attacking once the balance had been upset.[^s6][^s7]

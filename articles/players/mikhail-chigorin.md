# Mikhail Chigorin

Mikhail Ivanovich Chigorin was born in Gatchina, near St Petersburg, in 1850 and died in Lublin, then in the Russian Empire, on 25 January 1908.[^s2][^s4] He grew up in the Gatchina orphan boarding school, where he learned the game, and began playing in tournaments in 1873.[^s2][^s4] In English he signed himself "Tchigorin", and German sources wrote "Tschigorin"; all name the same player.[^s3] The World Chess Hall of Fame, which inducted him in 2013, calls him the founder of the Russian school of chess.[^s1]

## Rise in Russia

Chigorin worked in the police service from 1871 to 1883 and became the strongest player at the Café Dominique, the St Petersburg players' haunt.[^s7] He trained with and played a series of matches against Emanuel Schiffers from 1878, losing narrowly once but winning the later ones, and also beat Semyon Alapin in matches in 1880 and 1881.[^s4][^s7] In 1879 he shared first in an all-play-all of Russia's leading players and won the play-off against Alapin.[^s7] By the early 1880s he was clearly the best player in the country.[^s2] Late in his career he won the first three All-Russian Masters' Tournaments, in 1899, 1901 and 1903.[^s1]

## International career

He was the first Russian to compete regularly in international tournaments, sharing third and fourth at Berlin 1881 and finishing fourth at London 1883.[^s4] At the sixth American Chess Congress, New York 1889, a double round robin of twenty players, he and Max Weiss tied for first, and a four-game play-off was drawn four times.[^s6] At Hastings 1895 he took second place behind Harry Nelson Pillsbury, whom he beat in their individual game.[^s1][^s3] He won Budapest 1896 after a play-off and the King's Gambit thematic tournament at Vienna 1903.[^s4]

## Matches against Steinitz

Chigorin twice played Wilhelm Steinitz for the world championship in Havana, in 1889 and 1892, and lost both.[^s1] In the first match he led after seven games but lost 6½–10½.[^s7] In between, the two fought a two-game telegraph match from October 1890 to April 1891, with Steinitz in New York and Chigorin in St Petersburg, to settle a dispute over opening analysis, including a line of the Two Knights Defence; Chigorin won both games.[^s5][^s7] The 1892 rematch was played to ten wins with no drawn-match clause. Chigorin led 8–7 after nineteen games, but in the twenty-third game, a piece up in a queenless position, he moved the bishop guarding h2 and allowed mate in two, and Steinitz retained the title.[^s8][^s7] He also drew matches with Isidor Gunsberg (Havana 1890, nine wins each) and Siegbert Tarrasch (St Petersburg 1893).[^s9][^s4][^s1]

## Style and openings

Contemporaries saw him as a bold, combinative player; Pillsbury called his game "fine, dashing" and rated him very highly in match play, though Edward Winter notes he has been both over- and underrated.[^s3][^s7] He was an expert in the Evans Gambit and the King's Gambit.[^s1] Several lines carry his name: the Chigorin Variation of the Ruy Lopez, the Chigorin Variation of the French Defence (1.e4 e6 2.Qe2, introduced by W. H. K. Pollock but used extensively by Chigorin), and the Chigorin Defence to the Queen's Gambit, 1.d4 d5 2.c4 Nc6 (the `moves` above).[^s3][^s4] That defence shows his approach: Black blocks the c-pawn and develops a knight at once, accepting a doubtful structure in return for quick piece pressure on d4 — the opposite of Steinitz's preference for solid structures and accumulated small advantages.[^s7][^s4]

For a club player the lesson is that active, concrete piece play can unbalance a positional opponent, but Chigorin's matches also show its cost: the decisive 1892 loss came from a lapse in a technically won position, not from his opening choices.[^s8]

## Organiser and writer

Chigorin edited and published *Shakhmatny Listok* from 1876 to 1881 and later led *Shakhmatny Vestnik*, and he wrote chess columns for several periodicals.[^s2][^s1] In 1884 he founded the St Petersburg Chess Amateurs Society, and in autumn 1893 his match with Tarrasch became the first international chess contest held in Russia.[^s2] He lectured, toured and gave simultaneous and blindfold exhibitions across the empire.[^s7][^s3] International Chigorin memorial tournaments began in St Petersburg in 1909, and from 1947 Chigorin memorials were held in the USSR.[^s2]

## Connections

Chigorin sits under the **world chess championship** through his two Havana matches against **Wilhelm Steinitz**, whose systematic positional style **contrasts with** his own dynamic play.[^s1][^s7] He **played in** London 1883 and **Hastings 1895**, and drew a match with **Siegbert Tarrasch**.[^s4][^s1] His expertise in the **Evans Gambit** and **King's Gambit** ties him to the open-game repertoire, and the **Chigorin Defence** keeps his name in the Queen's Gambit.[^s1][^s4] As founder of the Russian school he is the forerunner of the **Soviet chess school**, whose players honoured him with memorial tournaments.[^s1][^s2]

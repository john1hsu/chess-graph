# David Bronstein

David Ionovich Bronstein (1924–2006) was a Soviet grandmaster who came within a single game of the world title and remained among the leading players for roughly three decades.[^s1] He was born on 19 February 1924 in Bila Tserkva, near Kyiv, became a Soviet master in 1939 after taking second place in the Ukrainian championship, and received the FIDE grandmaster title in 1949.[^s4][^s6] The World Chess Hall of Fame inducted him in 2016, describing him as a major thinker and innovator of the game.[^s1]

## Road to the title match

Bronstein's international breakthrough was his victory at the Saltsjöbaden Interzonal of 1948.[^s4][^s6] At home he shared first place in the USSR Championship in 1948 and 1949 and won or shared the Moscow Championship six times between 1946 and 1968.[^s4] At the Budapest Candidates Tournament of 1950 he tied for first with Isaac Boleslavsky and won the play-off, which made him Botvinnik's challenger.[^s4][^s6]

## The 1951 match with Botvinnik

The match was played in Moscow's Tchaikovsky Concert Hall over a maximum of 24 games; the challenger needed 12½ points, while a tied match left the champion with the title.[^s5] Each player won five games and fourteen were drawn.[^s5] Bronstein won games 21 and 22 to lead by a point with two to play, but Botvinnik won game 23 and the last game was drawn, so the match ended 12–12 and Botvinnik kept the crown.[^s5][^s6] Rumours that the challenger was told to lose have never died; Bronstein himself later wrote of strong psychological pressure while insisting that how he responded to it was his own responsibility.[^s6] The match also showed his opening range: he won game 17 with Black in the Nimzo-Indian line now known as the Bronstein (or Byrne) Variation, and he played the King's Indian Defence twice.[^s5]

## Later career

Bronstein never got so close again. He shared second place at the famous Zurich Candidates Tournament of 1953, two points behind Vasily Smyslov, finished behind Smyslov and Paul Keres at Amsterdam 1956, and won the Gothenburg Interzonal of 1955.[^s4] He took second in the USSR Championship in 1957 and 1964/65, won tournaments such as Hastings 1953/54 and 1975/76, Belgrade 1954 and Szombathely 1966, and earned board medals at four Olympiads from Helsinki 1952 to Munich 1958, including gold on fourth board at Moscow 1956 and Munich 1958.[^s4] In 1976 he was among the few Soviet grandmasters who refused to sign a letter condemning Viktor Korchnoi's defection; he lost his stipend and was kept out of most foreign events for years.[^s6][^s4] He died in Minsk in December 2006.[^s4][^s6]

## Style and ideas

Bronstein's play was intuitive and daring, but his biographer-annotator Nikolay Minev stresses that it was never whimsical: the games show an ingenious, deeply grounded creative mind, and Bronstein liked to describe a game as a battle of nerves rather than a test of knowledge.[^s3] He was famous for explaining the hidden ideas behind moves, including the ones that never reached the scoresheet.[^s6] In the openings he is credited with transforming the King's Indian Defence from a discredited system into a dynamic main line.[^s1][^s6] Off the board he was an early advocate of faster chess and in 1973 proposed adding time to the clock for each move made.[^s1]

His book on Zurich 1953, published in English by Dover, covers all 210 games of the tournament and is valued because it was written by one of the competitors, offering insight into how top players think.[^s2][^s1] He also wrote on the King's Indian and, with Tom Fürstenberg, the autobiographical *The Sorcerer's Apprentice*.[^s4][^s6]

## A short example

Against Wolfgang Uhlmann at Moscow 1971, Bronstein used the King's Indian Attack set-up against the French Defence and won in 15 moves after Black's 14...a4, which Minev calls the decisive error.[^s3] The game shows White's typical plan: e4–e5 to cramp Black's kingside, Nf1 and h4 to bring pieces to the king, then Ng5 and Qg4 to strike at e6 and g7.[^s3]

## Connections

Bronstein **played in** the **1951 World Chess Championship** match, the final step of the **World Chess Championship** cycle that began with his **Candidates Tournament** win, and his drawn match with **Mikhail Botvinnik** sets the intuitive challenger against the methodical champion.[^s5][^s4] He was a product and member of the **Soviet chess school** and a regular at the **Chess Olympiad** in the 1950s.[^s4] In openings he is linked to the King's Indian and, through his 1951 win with Black, to the **Nimzo-Indian Defence**, and the **Bronstein delay** clock mode carries his name, fitting his advocacy of new time controls.[^s1][^s5]

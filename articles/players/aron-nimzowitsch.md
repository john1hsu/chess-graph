# Aron Nimzowitsch

Aron Nimzowitsch was born on 7 November 1886 in Riga, then part of the Russian Empire, and died in Denmark on 16 March 1935.[^s1] His father, a merchant and a strong player, taught him the game at the age of eight, but he only began to concentrate on chess after going to Berlin to study in 1904.[^s1][^s4] He never won the world championship, yet he is remembered as one of the most original thinkers the game has produced and as the founder of the hypermodern movement.[^s1][^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame inducted him in 2018.[^s2]

## Career

His early tournament record was mixed. A review of the first full-scale biography lists sixth place at Coburg 1904, fourth at Karlsbad 1907, seventh at San Sebastián 1911 and sixth at Karlsbad 1911, before a breakthrough second place behind Akiba Rubinstein at San Sebastián 1912 and a tie with Alexander Alekhine in the All-Russian masters' event at St Petersburg in 1913.[^s4] After serving briefly in the Russian army in 1916–17 he wrote chess columns in occupied Riga, then toured Scandinavia; on a Danish tour in 1922 he lost only 26 of more than 580 exhibition games, and Denmark became his home for the rest of his life.[^s2][^s4] His biographers single out the years 1914 to 1924 as a previously obscure period of his life.[^s3]

His peak came in the second half of the 1920s. The World Chess Hall of Fame ranks him third in the world from 1925 to 1930, behind Alekhine and José Raúl Capablanca, and names Dresden 1926 and Carlsbad 1929 as his most notable tournament victories.[^s2]

## Ideas and writings

Nimzowitsch is regarded as the father of hypermodern chess, alongside Richard Réti, Savielly Tartakower and Gyula Breyer, a reaction against the classical doctrine of Siegbert Tarrasch, with whom he carried on a long personal feud.[^s3][^s4] The core hypermodern claim is that the centre can be controlled from a distance by pieces rather than occupied at once by pawns; the review summarises his theses as a flexible centre, the view that an advancing enemy pawn chain is not automatically dangerous, and the recognition that a complex of squares of one colour can be a lasting weakness.[^s4] Britannica credits him with a new vocabulary that made masters' strategy easier to understand.[^s1]

His books remain in print and in use: *Die Blockade* (1925), *Mein System* (*My System*, 1925) and *Die Praxis meines Systems* (*Chess Praxis*, 1929).[^s2] Britannica calls *My System* a classic of chess literature.[^s1] Two openings carry his name: the Nimzo-Indian Defence (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4) and the Nimzowitsch Defence (1.e4 Nc6).[^s2] The Réti and Catalan openings are also tied to hypermodern ideas.[^s4]

For club players the practical lesson is that a broad pawn centre is a target as well as an asset: it can be pressured by pieces and undermined by pawn breaks, and a fixed complex of weak squares is a long-term liability. The common misreading is to treat "control from a distance" as passivity; the hypermodern approach only works when the pieces really bear on the central squares.[^s4]

## Example: the "Immortal Zugzwang Game"

His best-known win is Friedrich Sämisch v Nimzowitsch, Copenhagen, played on 10 March 1923, a Queen's Indian Defence (ECO E18).[^s5][^s6] Black gave up the knight on h5 with 20...fxe4, took on f2 with the rook, doubled rooks on the f-file and, after 25...h6, White resigned.[^s5][^s6] In the final position (the `fen` above) White's pieces are tied up so that almost every move worsens the position; Nimzowitsch promoted it as the "Immortal Zugzwang Game", and Edward Winter records that Emanuel Lasker likened White's pieces to being stalemated, while Wolfgang Heidenfeld later argued it was not strict zugzwang because White would gain nothing even by passing.[^s5]

## Connections

Nimzowitsch is filed under **hypermodernism**, the school he led and **is a member of**, and his Carlsbad 1929 victory links him to that event.[^s2][^s4] He is related to **Siegbert Tarrasch**, the classical theorist he opposed, and to **José Raúl Capablanca**, one of the two players ranked above him in his best years.[^s2][^s3] His strategic vocabulary connects him to the **blockade**, the subject of his 1925 book, and to concepts such as the **outpost** and the **pin** that he analysed; the **Nimzo-Indian Defence** is named after him, and his game against Sämisch **exemplifies** his style.[^s2][^s5]

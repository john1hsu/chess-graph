# Tigran Petrosian

Tigran Vartanovich Petrosian (also spelled Petrosyan) was born on 17 June 1929 in Tbilisi, Georgia, and died in Moscow on 13 August 1984.[^s1] A Soviet Armenian master, he is counted as the ninth world chess champion, holding the title from 1963 to 1969.[^s1][^s2] He was inducted into the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2003.[^s2]

## Career

Petrosian learned the game at about eight and trained at the Tiflis Palace of Pioneers under Archil Ebralidze, developing a solid, scientific approach in a childhood marked by wartime hardship.[^s2][^s3] After a share of the 1946 Georgian championship he moved to Yerevan, won the Armenian championship in 1946 and 1947, and became a master in 1947.[^s1][^s2] A move to Moscow brought stronger opposition, and after a slow start to the 1950s he won the first of four USSR championships in 1959, adding further titles in 1961, 1969 and 1975.[^s2][^s3]

He played in three Candidates tournaments before finishing first at Curaçao in 1962, which gave him the right to challenge Botvinnik.[^s3] In 1963 he won the match by five wins to two with fifteen draws, ending Botvinnik's long era at the top.[^s2][^s3] In 1966 he beat Boris Spassky 12½–11½, the first time since 1934 that a reigning champion had won a title defence, but in 1969 Spassky beat him and took the crown.[^s2][^s3][^s4] He kept competing at the highest level afterwards, reaching eight Candidates cycles in all, and his long Olympiad record for the Soviet Union, ten teams between 1958 and 1978, shows one loss in 130 games (79 wins and 50 draws).[^s2][^s3] He also earned team golds in each of his eight European Team Championships.[^s2] Away from the board he studied at the Yerevan Teachers' College, later did postgraduate work in philosophy, wrote *Chess and Philosophy* (1968) and remained active in the presidium of the Soviet chess federation.[^s1]

## Style

Britannica describes his play as subtle and very patient, aimed at weakening the opponent's position step by step rather than winning with one blow; he also drew a great many games with players of his own class.[^s1] The Hall of Fame notes that he rarely attacked directly and preferred slow manoeuvring, the source of the nickname "Iron Tigran" and of his reputation as perhaps the hardest player ever to beat.[^s2] Contemporaries quoted by the FIDE Museum stress that the caution hid real tactical strength: Tal called him a defensive virtuoso, Spassky a strategist who was also a tactician, and Kasparov judged that he could combine as well as Tal but kept that gift in reserve.[^s3] A club-level study of his games singles out prophylaxis, stopping the opponent's ideas before they appear, along with a feel for when an imposing pawn front is really a weakness.[^s5]

For a club player, the practical lesson is that safety comes first: check what the opponent wants, remove it, and only then expand. The pitfall is passivity; Petrosian's best games show the defensive groundwork turning into a decisive attack once the opponent has over-extended.[^s5]

## Example: tenth game, 1966

The tenth game of the 1966 match, a King's Indian with a fianchetto (ECO E63), is the most famous illustration.[^s4] Spassky's ...f5 and ...e4 left loose pawns; Petrosian broke with f3, gave back the exchange with 21.Ne3 and 24.Rxf4 to reach the black king, and finished with a queen check on h8.[^s5]

## Connections

Petrosian sits under the **world chess championship** and **holds the title** of world champion; he took it from **Mikhail Botvinnik** and both kept and lost it against **Boris Spassky**.[^s1][^s3] He **played in** the **Candidates tournament**, winning at Curaçao 1962, and in the **Chess Olympiad** as a long-serving Soviet team member, part of the **Soviet chess school**.[^s2][^s3] His approach is a model of **prophylaxis**.[^s5] The 1966 tenth game **exemplifies** his play.[^s4][^s5]

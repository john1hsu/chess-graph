# José Raúl Capablanca

José Raúl Capablanca was born in Havana on 19 November 1888 and died in New York on 8 March 1942, a day after suffering a stroke while watching a game at the Manhattan Chess Club.[^s1] He learned the moves at four by watching his father play, and as a boy beat Cuba's leading player; the World Chess Hall of Fame identifies that opponent as the Cuban champion Juan Corzo and puts Capablanca's age at 13.[^s1][^s2] He became the third official world champion, holding the title from 1921 to 1927, and was a charter inductee of the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2001.[^s1][^s2]

## Rise to the top

Capablanca studied briefly at Columbia University in New York, joined the Manhattan Chess Club and soon became its strongest player, then left university to concentrate on chess.[^s1][^s2] In 1909 he toured 27 American cities giving simultaneous exhibitions, scoring 96.9 per cent across 607 games.[^s2] From 1913 he was attached to the Cuban diplomatic service, a post that let him travel to the European chess centres and give exhibitions in London, Paris, Berlin and St Petersburg.[^s1][^s2] He narrowly lost the St Petersburg 1914 tournament to Lasker, but his tournament record over the following decade was extraordinary: Britannica says he did not lose a single game in active tournament play from 1916 to 1924, and the Hall of Fame counts only one loss between 1914 and 1924.[^s1][^s2]

## World champion

His match against Lasker was played in Havana from 15 March to 28 April 1921, scheduled for 24 games or the first to eight wins.[^s3] Capablanca won four games and drew ten without a loss; Lasker resigned the match after 14 games with the score 9–5.[^s3] Capablanca held the title for six years. In Buenos Aires, from 16 September to 29 November 1927, he met Alexander Alekhine in a match to six wins; after 34 games, 25 of them drawn, Alekhine had won six and Capablanca three, so the title changed hands 18½–15½.[^s4] Capablanca kept playing in top tournaments in the hope of a rematch but, in the Hall of Fame's assessment, never quite recovered his earlier level; he retired briefly in 1931 before returning in the 1930s.[^s2]

## Style and teaching

Capablanca's play was valued for its clarity. Britannica notes the deceptive simplicity of his games and how effortless he could make the defeat of another master look; the Hall of Fame calls him a master of positional play who was also strong tactically, and records his nickname, the Human Chess Machine.[^s1][^s2] Modern writers have also drawn attention to the attacking side of his chess: John Watson, reviewing Cyrus Lakdawala's *Capablanca Move by Move* (Everyman Chess, 2012), says he had remembered Capablanca mainly for endings and positional games and was surprised by the number of king hunts.[^s6]

His instructional book *Chess Fundamentals* (copyright 1921) reflects the same priorities. Its first chapter begins with simple mates, starting from king and rook against king, before moving to middlegame and opening principles, and later chapters return to endgame play and endgame strategy.[^s5] In a preface to a later edition Capablanca argued that the hypermodern movement had brought new tactics rather than new strategic principles, so the fundamentals were unchanged.[^s5]

## Example: Lasker–Capablanca, Havana 1921, game 10

The tenth game of the 1921 match, a Queen's Gambit Declined (ECO D61) won by Capablanca as Black, is the game the Hall of Fame singles out on his page.[^s2] After 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e3 O-O 6.Nf3 Nbd7 7.Qc2 c5 8.Rd1 Qa5 9.Bd3 h6 10.Bh4 cxd4 11.exd4 dxc4, White had an isolated queen's pawn on d4.[^s6] Capablanca exchanged the pieces that support it, accepted an isolated c-pawn of his own on move 20, and after 24.Rxd5 cxd5 reached the position in the `fen` above: White's d-pawn is fixed and blockaded, Black's knight is heading for f5 to attack it, and the c-file belongs to Black.[^s6] He then gained space with ...h5–h4 and ...a5–a4, traded queens on move 33, broke into White's second rank with his rook, won the b-pawn, and finally broke open the centre with ...e5; Lasker resigned after 68...Kd5.[^s6] It is a model of slowly converting small static advantages against an isolani.

## Connections

Capablanca sits under the **world chess championship** and **holds the title** of world champion. He **played in** the 1921 match, where he dethroned **Emanuel Lasker**, and the 1927 match, where he lost to **Alexander Alekhine**.[^s3][^s4] He is related to **Aron Nimzowitsch**, a leading contemporary associated with the hypermodern movement whose claims Capablanca weighed in his preface to *Chess Fundamentals*.[^s5] The tenth game of the 1921 match **exemplifies** his technique and is a classic illustration of play against the **isolated queen's pawn**.[^s2][^s6]

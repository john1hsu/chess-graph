# Siegbert Tarrasch

Siegbert Tarrasch was born in Breslau, Prussia (now Wrocław, Poland), on 5 March 1862 and died in Munich on 17 February 1934.[^s1] He studied medicine in Berlin and Halle and practised as a physician, living mostly in Nuremberg and later Munich, so that for most of his career chess was a second profession.[^s2][^s3] The World Chess Hall of Fame, which inducted him in 2008, describes him as perhaps the best player in the world in the 1890s and one of the strongest never to become world champion.[^s2]

## Tournament career

Tarrasch's first big success was first prize at Nuremberg 1884.[^s3] He then won four major German and English events in succession — Breslau 1889, Manchester 1890, Dresden 1892 and Leipzig 1894 — and a contemporary reference work notes that he lost only one game in each of the last three.[^s2][^s3] At Leipzig 1894, the ninth German Chess Federation (DSB) congress, he scored 13½ out of 17, half a point ahead of Paul Lipke, with Richard Teichmann third.[^s6] Britannica counts five major tournament victories in a row between 1888 and 1894.[^s1]

He took fourth prize at Hastings 1895 and at Nuremberg 1896.[^s3] His best result is usually considered Vienna 1898, where he tied for first with Pillsbury and won the play-off match.[^s1][^s4] Further wins included Monte Carlo 1903, and he shared second and third places with Dawid Janowski at Ostend 1905.[^s3] In matches he drew with Mikhail Chigorin at St Petersburg in 1893 and beat Frank Marshall decisively at Nuremberg in 1905.[^s3] His last great tournament was St Petersburg 1914, where he finished fourth.[^s2]

## The world championship

In 1892 Tarrasch declined an invitation to play a title match because of the demands of his medical practice, and two years later Emanuel Lasker became champion.[^s2] Their rivalry ran through the 1890s and 1900s, but Tarrasch never beat Lasker.[^s2] When they finally met for the title, from 17 August to 30 September 1908, the first four games were played at the Kunstpalast in Düsseldorf and the rest in Munich's town hall; Lasker won eight games to three with five draws.[^s5][^s4]

## Teacher and theorist

Tarrasch was the most influential chess writer of his generation, nicknamed the "Teacher of Germany" (*Praeceptor Germaniae*).[^s2][^s4] He co-edited the *Deutsche Schachzeitung* until 1897, and his annotated collection *Dreihundert Schachpartien* (1894) and the later *Die moderne Schachpartie* spread his ideas far beyond Germany; Britannica singles out *The Game of Chess* (published in English in 1935) as his best-known work.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Britannica describes his writing as developing and popularising the positional ideas of Wilhelm Steinitz, while disagreeing with Steinitz over what counted as a small advantage.[^s1]

His teaching emphasised rapid development, space, king safety, pawn structure, piece activity, outposts and the two bishops.[^s4] Above all he valued space and disliked cramped positions, which he said carried the germs of defeat; the side with more space should win by constriction, while the cramped side must look for freeing pawn breaks.[^s4] His clear, rule-like presentation made positional chess teachable, but it also earned him a reputation for dogmatism, and the next generation of hypermodern players, led by Aron Nimzowitsch, attacked exactly these principles.[^s4]

For a club player the lesson is twofold. Tarrasch's rules — develop quickly, gain space, keep your pieces active — remain sound defaults. The pitfall is to apply them mechanically: a broad centre or a space advantage is only an asset while it can be supported, which is the point his hypermodern critics pressed.[^s4]

## Openings

Several opening systems carry his name, including the Tarrasch Variations of the French Defence and of the Queen's Gambit Declined, both still played at the highest level.[^s4] The Queen's Gambit Declined, Tarrasch (ECO D32) arises after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 c5 (the `moves` above): instead of supporting d5 passively, Black challenges White's centre with the c-pawn on the third move, in keeping with Tarrasch's insistence on free piece play and his dislike of cramped positions.[^s7][^s4] The ChessBase article notes that the line was tested thoroughly in the 1969 world championship match between Boris Spassky and Tigran Petrosian.[^s4]

## Connections

Tarrasch sits under the **world chess championship** through his 1908 match against **Emanuel Lasker**, the rival he never overcame.[^s2][^s5] He was **influenced by** **Wilhelm Steinitz**, whose principles he systematised, and his doctrine **contrasts with** **hypermodernism**, the school of his long-time antagonist **Aron Nimzowitsch**.[^s1][^s4] He **played in** Leipzig 1894 and Hastings 1895.[^s3][^s6] His Queen's Gambit Declined line is related to the **isolated queen's pawn** structure treated elsewhere in the graph, and his variation of the **French Defence** keeps his name in modern repertoires.[^s4][^s7]

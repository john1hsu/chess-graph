# Garry Kasparov

Garry Kasparov was born on 13 April 1963 in Baku, then part of the Soviet Union.[^s1] He began playing at six, was Soviet youth champion by thirteen and became an international grandmaster in 1980.[^s1] From 1973 to 1978 he studied in the school of former world champion Mikhail Botvinnik.[^s1] He became the thirteenth world champion in 1985 and was inducted into the World Chess Hall of Fame in 2005.[^s2]

## Rise and the Karpov matches

As a teenager Kasparov twice shared first place in the USSR Championship, and in 1982 he won the Moscow Interzonal.[^s2][^s3] In the Candidates matches that followed he beat Alexander Beliavsky, Viktor Korchnoi and Vasily Smyslov, the last without losing a game.[^s3] His first title match with Anatoly Karpov, begun in Moscow in 1984 and played to six wins, became the longest in history: Kasparov lost four of the first nine games and then fought back through a long series of draws, and FIDE stopped the match without a result after 48 games, a decision Kasparov protested.[^s1][^s3]

The 1985 rematch, limited to 24 games, ended 13–11 for Kasparov, making him at 22 the youngest official world champion.[^s1][^s3] He then defended the title against Karpov three times: 12½–11½ in London and Leningrad in 1986, 12–12 in 1987 (a drawn match left the champion in place), and 12½–11½ in New York and Lyon in 1990.[^s3] The five matches made Kasparov and Karpov the defining rivalry of the late Soviet era; Boris Spassky's remark, quoted by FIDE, was that after them there were "no longer kings, but only prime ministers".[^s3]

## The split, Deep Blue and the Kramnik match

In 1993 Kasparov and the challenger Nigel Short left FIDE and played their match under the new Professional Chess Association (PCA); FIDE stripped Kasparov of its title, and he beat Short 12½–7½.[^s1][^s3] He defended the PCA crown against Viswanathan Anand in New York in 1995, 10½–7½, and the PCA folded in 1996.[^s1][^s3] The resulting double championship lasted thirteen years.[^s2]

Kasparov beat IBM's Deep Blue in 1996 but lost the six-game rematch in 1997, the first match a reigning world champion had lost to a computer under standard tournament conditions.[^s1] In 2000 he lost a 16-game classical championship match to Vladimir Kramnik.[^s1][^s2]

## Dominance and retirement

Kasparov was the world's top-rated player for twenty years, the first to pass 2800, and reached a peak rating of 2851.[^s2] The Hall of Fame also credits him with a ten-year unbeaten streak and fifteen consecutive tournament wins between 1981 and 1990.[^s2] After losing the title he kept winning elite tournaments and was still number one on the FIDE list when he retired from competitive chess in 2005.[^s2][^s3] He later played exhibitions and coached, among others, Magnus Carlsen and Viswanathan Anand.[^s1][^s2]

## Style and contributions

Kasparov's chess combined deep, dynamic opening preparation with relentless initiative. Kramnik, quoted by FIDE, compared him to Botvinnik but judged him more flexible, quick to take in new ideas; Karpov credited him with being among the first to make serious use of computers in preparation.[^s3] Carlsen called him the greatest player ever.[^s3] As an author he wrote the multi-volume *My Great Predecessors* (2003–06), a history of the world champions from Steinitz to Karpov, and *Deep Thinking* (2017) on the Deep Blue match.[^s1]

## Example: Kasparov–Topalov, Wijk aan Zee 1999

Kasparov won the 1999 Hoogovens tournament with 10/13, half a point ahead of Anand.[^s4][^s5] His fourth-round win as White over Veselin Topalov on 20 January began with a Pirc Defence, both kings castled queenside, and the first 23 moves were fairly quiet.[^s6] In the `fen` position above (after 23...Qd6) Kasparov played 24.Rxd4!, and after 24...cxd4 25.Re7+ the black king was driven up the board toward White's back rank.[^s5][^s6] Topalov later said that accepting the rook was the mistake and that declining it was simpler.[^s6] The decisive point came at move 37, and Black resigned on move 44.[^s6] The lesson is Kasparov's trademark: open lines and piece activity around the enemy king can be worth more than a rook. ChessBase calls it his "immortal".[^s5]

## Connections

Kasparov sits under the **world chess championship**, **holds the titles** of world champion and **grandmaster**, and is tied above all to **Anatoly Karpov**, his opponent in five title matches.[^s3] He was **influenced by** his teacher **Mikhail Botvinnik** and belongs to the **Soviet chess school** that produced both.[^s1] He **played in** the **Deep Blue versus Kasparov** matches, a landmark of computer chess, and lost his classical title to **Vladimir Kramnik**.[^s1] His Wijk aan Zee 1999 win over Topalov **exemplifies** his attacking style.[^s5][^s6] He is also linked to the **Sicilian Defence**, whose own article names him the Najdorf Variation's greatest exponent.

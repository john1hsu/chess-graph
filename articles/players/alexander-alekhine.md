# Alexander Alekhine

Alexander Alekhine, born Aleksandr Aleksandrovich Alyokhin in Moscow on 31 October 1892, was world chess champion from 1927 to 1935 and again from 1937 until his death in Estoril, Portugal, on 24 March 1946.[^s1] After the Russian Revolution he settled in France and took French citizenship, and he studied law at the University of Paris.[^s1][^s2] The World Chess Hall of Fame inducted him in 2004.[^s2]

## Early career

Britannica records that he was a master at 16 and a grandmaster at 22; the Hall of Fame counts him among the first players officially recognised as grandmasters, in 1914.[^s1][^s2] That summer he was playing at Mannheim when the First World War broke out and the tournament was abandoned; he was interned in Germany, and after his release served with the Russian army's Red Cross division.[^s1][^s2] In the 1920s he rose to the very top: third at New York 1924 behind Capablanca and Lasker, whose tournament book he annotated, and first at Baden-Baden 1925 in a field of 21.[^s2]

## World champion

During an exhibition tour of Argentina in 1926 he secured backing for a title challenge.[^s2] The match with José Raúl Capablanca was played in Buenos Aires from 16 September to 29 November 1927, to six wins; after 34 games, 25 of them drawn, Alekhine had won six and lost three, taking the title 18½–15½.[^s3]

He followed it with the most dominant stretch of his career. At San Remo 1930 he scored 14 from 15 (13 wins, two draws), three and a half points ahead of Nimzowitsch, and across San Remo, Bled 1931 and the 1930 and 1931 Olympiads he lost only one game.[^s6] The Hall of Fame also lists first places at London 1932, Pasadena 1932 and Zurich 1934.[^s2] He defended the title twice against Efim Bogoljubow, winning 15½–9½ in 1929 (11 wins, 5 losses) and 15½–10½ in 1934 (8 wins, 3 losses).[^s2][^s4]

In 1935, in a 30-game match played across thirteen Dutch cities from 3 October to 16 December, he lost to Max Euwe 15½–14½.[^s4] The rematch, from 5 October to 4 December 1937, went the other way: Alekhine won ten games to Euwe's four and regained the title 15½–9½ after 25 games.[^s4][^s5] The FIDE Chess Museum notes that this was the last world championship in which the reigning champion controlled the title and set the conditions; after the Second World War FIDE took over.[^s5] He was still champion when he died in 1946.[^s1][^s2]

## Style and legacy

Britannica characterises his chess by the great variety of his attacks; the Hall of Fame stresses deeply conceived, imaginative attacking play and detailed annotation.[^s1][^s2] He broke the world blindfold record in 1924, 1925 and 1933.[^s1] His two collections, *My Best Games of Chess 1908–1923* (1927) and *My Best Games of Chess 1924–1937* (1939), are regarded as classics, and Alekhine's Defence (1.e4 Nf6) is among the openings that bear his name.[^s1][^s2] Seven of his fifteen San Remo games went into his best-games collection.[^s6]

## Example: Alekhine–Nimzowitsch, San Remo 1930

In round 3 at San Remo, Alekhine met Nimzowitsch's French Winawer (ECO C17) with an early queenside space grab and then concentrated everything on the open c-file.[^s6][^s7] With 24.Rc3, 25.R1c2 and 26.Qc1 he stacked both rooks and the queen on the file, the formation now called Alekhine's Gun.[^s7] Black could only mirror the doubling, and after 30.h4 (the `fen` above) he had no useful moves left: the knight on c6 cannot move without exposing the queen on d7 to the a4 bishop, the rook on c7 is tied to its defence, and b4–b5 is coming. Nimzowitsch resigned, which ChessBase describes as a resignation in zugzwang in the middlegame.[^s6][^s7]

## Connections

Alekhine sits under the **world chess championship** and **holds the title** of world champion. He **played in** the 1927 match, where he dethroned **José Raúl Capablanca**, and in the 1935 and 1937 matches against **Max Euwe**, losing and then regaining the crown.[^s3][^s4] He **played in** **San Remo 1930**, his greatest tournament, and the round-3 win over Nimzowitsch **exemplifies** both his style and the battery known as **Alekhine's Gun**.[^s6][^s7] **Alekhine's Defence** carries his name, and his record exhibitions tie him to the history of **blindfold chess**.[^s1][^s2]

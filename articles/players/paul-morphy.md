# Paul Morphy

Paul Charles Morphy was born in New Orleans on 22 June 1837 and died there on 10 July 1884.[^s1] His public chess career lasted less than two years, yet in that time he became the strongest player in the world, and many writers still rank him among the most brilliant players in the game's history.[^s1] Because there was no formal title match in his day, his status as the first world champion is unofficial.[^s2]

## Early years

Morphy came from a well-to-do Creole family and learned by watching relatives play at home; sources differ on whether he knew the moves at eight or ten.[^s1][^s2][^s3] By his early teens he was the leading player in New Orleans, and as a boy he beat the visiting Hungarian master Johann Löwenthal in a short series of games, which Cutler gives as two wins and a draw and ChessBase as a three-game sweep.[^s3][^s5] He trained in law and was admitted to the Louisiana bar at 19 on condition that he not practise until he came of age.[^s1]

## The 1857 Congress and the European tour

In autumn 1857 Morphy won the First American Chess Congress in New York, an event for sixteen invited players, and was thereafter counted as the second United States champion.[^s2][^s3] He beat Theodor Lichtenhein 3–0 with one draw on the way, according to the tournament book, and defeated Louis Paulsen in the final by five wins to one with two draws.[^s4][^s6] After the Congress he offered to give odds of pawn and move to all comers.[^s3]

In 1858 he sailed for Europe. In England he repeatedly sought a match with Howard Staunton, the leading English player, who never agreed to one.[^s1][^s3] ChessBase's tally of his matches that year includes Löwenthal 9–3, Henry Bird 10–1, Samuel Boden 6–1 and, in Paris, Daniel Harrwitz 5–2.[^s5] The climax was his match against Adolf Anderssen, the strongest player in Europe, which Morphy won by seven wins to two with two draws while still recovering from illness.[^s3] In Paris he also gave a blindfold display against eight strong opponents, winning six and drawing two.[^s1][^s3]

## Retirement

Morphy returned to New York on 11 May 1859 as a national hero.[^s2][^s3] He then challenged any player in the world to a match at odds of pawn and move; when no one accepted, he ended his public career.[^s1] His attempt to build a law practice failed, and he gave up even private games in later years.[^s1][^s3]

## Style

Britannica credits Morphy with being the first to apply the principle of development before attack, which is now standard opening doctrine.[^s1] The World Chess Hall of Fame similarly describes him as one of the first to prefer long-term development to early raids and traps, while still finishing many games with spectacular piece sacrifices.[^s2] Anderssen likened him to a machine that reliably reaches its conclusion.[^s3] The practical lesson for a club player is the order of operations: get every piece out and open lines first, and the combinations follow.

## Example: Paulsen v Morphy, New York 1857

His best-known Congress game came against Paulsen. After 17...Qxf3!, Black gives up the queen for a bishop to strip the white king, and the rook and bishops then hunt it down.[^s4][^s7] Edward Winter notes that annotators from Zukertort and Steinitz onwards showed that Morphy missed quicker wins, starting with 22...Rg2, and that the exact date of the game is still uncertain.[^s4]

## Connections

Morphy sits under the **Romantic era**, whose sacrificial style he shared while pointing beyond it through his emphasis on **development**.[^s1][^s2] He **played in** the **First American Chess Congress** and is **exemplified by** the **Opera Game**, his 1858 win over the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard in Paris.[^s2][^s4] He is related to **Adolf Anderssen**, his chief European opponent; to **Howard Staunton**, the match that never happened; to **Louis Paulsen**, his opponent in the 1857 final; to **blindfold chess**, through his Paris display; and to the **U.S. Chess Championship**, whose title lineage counts him as its second holder.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

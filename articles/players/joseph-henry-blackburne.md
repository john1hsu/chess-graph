# Joseph Henry Blackburne

Joseph Henry Blackburne (10 December 1841 – 1 September 1924) was born in Manchester and died in Lewisham, south London; his biographer Tim Harding calls him Britain's greatest chess master of the Victorian era.[^s3] At his peak he was one of the world's top three players, and he made his living as a chess professional for more than half a century.[^s1] Winter's notes confirm 1841, not the 1842 given in some older books, as his year of birth.[^s2]

## Early career and blindfold play

Blackburne was a strong draughts player as a boy and took up chess only at about eighteen, inspired by Paul Morphy's exploits.[^s5] After losing his first games to the Manchester player Edward Pindar in 1861, he beat him a few months later and became Manchester club champion in 1862, ahead of Pindar and Bernhard Horwitz.[^s4] Louis Paulsen's blindfold display in Manchester late in 1861 prompted him to try blindfold chess himself.[^s3] By his own account he gave his first blindfold exhibition in 1862, aged nineteen.[^s2] In June 1863 he played 12 blindfold games at once in Manchester, a record that stood until Johannes Zukertort played 16 in London in 1876.[^s3] He kept giving blindfold displays into his sixties, and for decades toured Britain giving simultaneous exhibitions, the main source of his income.[^s1][^s5]

## Tournament record

His first tournament was London 1862, where his overall result was moderate but he beat Wilhelm Steinitz.[^s3] He won the British Chess Association challenge cup in 1868–69, in effect becoming British champion.[^s3] Abroad, he was third at Baden-Baden 1870 behind Adolf Anderssen, second to Steinitz in the London 1872 Gold Cup, and tied for first with Steinitz at Vienna 1873 before losing the tie-break.[^s3] The Vienna tournament book called him the "black death" of chessplayers, a name that stuck.[^s5][^s4]

The peak of his career was Berlin 1881, which he won outright in a field of 18, three points ahead of Zukertort.[^s3] He also shared first at Wiesbaden 1880, was third at Paris 1878 and London 1883, second at Nuremberg 1883, Hamburg 1885 and Manchester 1890 (behind Siegbert Tarrasch), and fourth at New York 1889.[^s3] Golombek counted 53 international events from London 1862 to St Petersburg 1914, with 814 games and a score above 62%.[^s5] Late in life he still beat Emanuel Lasker, at Hastings 1895 and London 1899, and at St Petersburg 1914 he defeated Aron Nimzowitsch.[^s3][^s4] In 1914, aged 72, he tied for first in the British Championship with F. D. Yates; the title went to Yates when Blackburne was too unwell for a play-off.[^s3]

## Style and match play

Contemporaries saw him as a practical fighter with remarkable combinative powers and fierce kingside attacks, and one writer called him faithful to the open game to the end.[^s4][^s5][^s2] He left no body of opening theory, though Golombek saw hints of later ideas in some of his games.[^s4][^s2] He did much worse in matches than in tournaments.[^s3] He lost heavily to Steinitz, including a 7–0 defeat in 1876, and failed to win a game against Lasker in 1892.[^s4][^s5]

The trappy line 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nd4, known as the Blackburne Shilling Gambit, bears his name. Winter reports that no game of Blackburne's with it has been found, and the earliest known game dates from 1911.[^s2]

## Connections

Blackburne sits under **history of chess**, in the closing decades of the **Romantic era**. He is related to his great rivals **Wilhelm Steinitz** and **Johannes Zukertort**, and to **blindfold chess**, the speciality that made his name.[^s3] He **played in** Vienna 1873, Berlin 1881, **London 1883** and **Hastings 1895**, and he is **exemplified by** his loss to Zukertort at London 1883, a game Winter discusses.[^s2][^s3] He is related to **Emanuel Lasker**, whom he beat twice in tournament play, and to the **Blackburne Shilling Gambit** that carries his name.[^s3][^s2]

# French Defence

The French Defence is Black's reply 1...e6 to 1.e4, normally followed by ...d5 so that the main position arises after 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5; the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings files 1.e4 e6 under C00, with the main lines spread over the following C0x and C1x codes (the Winawer, for instance, includes C17 and C18).[^s6] Grandmaster Damian Lemos describes it as one of Black's most reliable ways to meet 1.e4, and the authors of a Quality Chess repertoire call it solid and strategically rich, noting that its blocked centres have held up well against heavy computer analysis and reward positional understanding over memorised lines.[^s2][^s3]

## Origins and name

Edward Winter's research traces the opening's early labels: John Cochrane's 1822 treatise called it the "King's pawn one game", and the name "French Defence" came into use only in the following decade.[^s1] By 1861 Saint-Amant wrote of the opening "called the French game", while English writers of the period were often scornful; Miron Hazeltine's 1866 book spoke of "the execrable French Defence", and George Walker's *Bell's Life* column used "sneak" as a derisive label for such closed replies.[^s1] Daniel Harrwitz, by contrast, praised 1...e6 in his 1862 textbook as a defence in which the second player faces no immediate attack.[^s1]

## Main ideas

The pawns on e6 and d5 give Black a firm hold on the centre, but they also invite White to advance e4–e5, closing the centre and gaining space, especially on the kingside.[^s4][^s2] Black's answer is to attack the resulting pawn chain: the main lever is ...c7–c5 against d4, and the second is ...f7–f6 against e5, which Lemos calls one of the most popular French ruptures; ...g5 and an early ...c4 to close the queenside are further options.[^s2][^s5] The recurring problem is the c8-bishop, often a bad bishop behind its own pawns; Black tries to free it by opening lines or by queenside expansion with ...a6 and ...b5.[^s2] White, for its part, aims to use the extra space and dark-square control, with breaks such as f4–f5 and sacrificial ideas against h7, g7 and f7 that a French player must learn to recognise.[^s2][^s5]

## Main variations

White's third move defines the play, and modern books organise the opening around it.[^s2]

- **3.Nc3** is the most testing. Black can choose the **Winawer**, 3...Bb4; the **Classical** (or Steinitz) 3...Nf6, where 4.e5 Nfd7 5.f4 c5 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Be3 is the most popular continuation and 4.Bg5 is the main alternative; or the **Rubinstein**, 3...dxe4, which gives up the centre for easy development.[^s2][^s4]
- **3.Nd2**, the **Tarrasch**, avoids the pin of ...Bb4; one main reply is 3...Nf6 4.e5 Nfd7, while the Quality Chess repertoire is built on 3...c5.[^s4][^s3]
- **3.e5**, the **Advance**, fixes the chain at once; Black hits it with 3...c5, and after 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Qb6 6.a3 the fight centres on d4.[^s4]
- **3.exd5 exd5**, the **Exchange**, gives a symmetrical structure, and quieter systems such as the King's Indian Attack with 2.d3 sidestep the main debates.[^s4][^s2]

## Example and pitfalls

A model of White's strategy is Kasparov–Bareev, Novgorod 1997: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.e5 Nfd7 5.f4 c5 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Be3 cxd4 8.Nxd4 Bc5 9.Qd2 Bxd4 10.Bxd4 Nxd4 11.Qxd4 Qb6 12.Qxb6 Nxb6. Even with queens off, Lemos explains that White's space and good dark-squared bishop gave a lasting endgame edge, and White won.[^s2] By contrast, in Karjakin–Carlsen, Wijk aan Zee 2010, Black chose 7...Be7 8.Qd2 0-0 9.Be2 a6 10.0-0 b5, then freed the position with ...cxd4 and ...f6 and won.[^s2] The lesson for club players is that trading off Black's active pieces while the c8-bishop stays passive favours White, and that Black must strike with ...c5 and ...f6 before White's kingside attack arrives.[^s2]

## Connections

The French is a **semi-open game** and a direct **alternative to the Caro-Kann** and the **Sicilian**: like the Caro-Kann it meets e4 with an early ...d5, but it does so at the cost of shutting in the queen's bishop.[^s2] It leads into named lines such as the **Winawer Variation**, and its Advance-type pawn chains make it a standard teaching ground for **pawn structure** and the theme of **good and bad bishops**, which specialist courses on the opening build around.[^s5][^s2]

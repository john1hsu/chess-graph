# Open games

**Open games** are the openings that start 1.e4 e5, with both sides advancing the king's pawn two squares.[^s1] The family is also called the double king's pawn openings, and in the *Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings* classification it fills Volume C, which it shares with the French Defence.[^s5] Like its sister labels, "open" is a historical name rather than a promise: ChessBase's opening primer points out that some open games, the main line of the Ruy Lopez above all, produce closed, manoeuvring positions.[^s1] Together with the semi-open games (1.e4 and any other reply) and the closed games, it forms the traditional three-way division of opening play.

## The main members

The best-known open games differ mostly in what White does on moves two and three:[^s1]

- **Italian Game** (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5): quick, natural development aimed at f7; a 19th-century favourite that has been revived at the top level and is a common first opening for beginners.[^s1]
- **Ruy Lopez** or Spanish (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5, main line 3...a6 4.Ba4 Nf6): solid but theory-heavy, with strategically demanding middlegames.[^s1] Britannica notes that it is named after the 16th-century Spanish priest Ruy López de Segura, who did not invent it, and that it remains one of the most popular openings.[^s3]
- **King's Gambit** (1.e4 e5 2.f4): White gives a pawn to open lines; a mainstay of the Romantic era, now judged double-edged and rare in elite events.[^s1]
- **Petroff Defence** (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nf6): Black counterattacks e4 instead of defending e5, reaching symmetrical positions that are safe but often drawish at the top.[^s1]

Repertoire books for Black also have to deal with the Scotch, the Four Knights and assorted gambits, which shows how wide the family is beyond these four.[^s4]

## History

In the Romantic period of the 19th century, 1.e4 was by far the most common first move and was usually answered by 1...e5, so the open games were the main battleground of the era.[^s1] Players of that time used gambits — pawn sacrifices in the opening — to speed up development and open lines against the enemy king.[^s2] Under Wilhelm Steinitz and the classical players who followed, positional play took over and gambits almost vanished from master chess.[^s1][^s2] After the First World War the Hypermoderns showed that Black need not answer 1.e4 with 1...e5 at all, and the Soviet school later turned the Sicilian into the main aggressive alternative, so the open games lost their near-monopoly.[^s2] They never went away: modern encyclopaedia updates still give the Spanish, Italian, Two Knights and Petroff extensive new material, and reviewers note a recent preference for slow positional build-ups, as in the 4.d3 lines where the Ruy Lopez and Giuoco Piano meet.[^s6]

## Typical ideas and pitfalls

Because the centre pawns face each other symmetrically, the early fight revolves around piece development, pressure on e5 and e4, and the weak f7 and f2 squares, defended at first only by the king.[^s7] Emms's introductory book, as reviewed by ChessCafe, teaches these themes through traps and their refutations; one example is that after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5, the hasty 4.Ng5? simply loses the knight to 4...Qxg5, which also eyes g2.[^s7] The same review shows castling as the most natural defence of f7 against an early knight jump, with White's speculative Nxf7 handing Black a lead in development.[^s7] For Black, the attraction of 1...e5 is a stake in the centre and good squares for the pieces; a modern classical repertoire against 1.e4 can be built on positional principles and limited memorisation, using sound systems such as the Breyer in the Ruy Lopez.[^s4] The practical pitfall is the other side of that: open games reward accurate development, so a single loose tempo or a premature attack on f7 is punished far more quickly than in closed structures.[^s7]

## Connections

Open games sit under the general heading of **chess openings**, beside the **semi-open games** and **closed games**, and **contrast with** the semi-open family, in which Black avoids the symmetrical reply.[^s1] The family **has as parts** the **Italian Game**, **Ruy Lopez**, **King's Gambit** and **Petroff Defence**, and the **Evans Gambit**, a branch of the Italian.[^s1][^s7] Its history is bound up with the **Romantic era**, when gambits in these openings dominated, and with **Wilhelm Steinitz**, whose positional approach ended that era.[^s1][^s2]

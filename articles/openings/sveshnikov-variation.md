# Sveshnikov Variation

The Sveshnikov Variation is the branch of the Open Sicilian reached after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e5.[^s2] It is filed under B33 in the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings, the code databases use for Open Sicilians with an early ...Nc6 and ...Nf6.[^s6] With 5...e5 Black evicts the d4-knight with tempo and claims central space, but voluntarily leaves the d5-square without pawn protection and the d6-pawn backward. For decades that trade was regarded as poor strategy; Timoshchenko's publisher notes that theoreticians once called the line "anti-positional", yet it is now played at every level.[^s2]

## Names and history

The variation has three names. Older monographs call it the Lasker-Pelikan Variation; in the West it carries the name of Evgeny Sveshnikov; and in Russia it is known as the Chelyabinsk Variation.[^s2] Peter Doggers's report on the 2018 world championship recounts that Sveshnikov popularised the line in the 1960s and 1970s, at a time when conceding the d5 hole looked unacceptable.[^s3] Grandmaster Gennadi Timoshchenko is described by his publisher as one of the line's creative founders, and his 2018 monograph on it carries a foreword by Garry Kasparov.[^s2] Boris Gelfand used it against Viswanathan Anand in their 2012 match, and Magnus Carlsen had played it 27 times before he relied on it in 2018.[^s3]

## Main ideas

After 6.Ndb5 d6 Black threatens nothing immediate, but White's knight on b5 eyes d6 and c7, so the main line continues 7.Bg5 a6 8.Na3 b5.[^s1] Black's queenside pawns gain space and keep the a3-knight out of play, and the fight now turns on d5. White's standard reply is 9.Bxf6 gxf6 10.Nd5, planting a knight on the hole; Black's pawns are shattered but in return Black keeps the bishop pair, an open g-file and the freeing break ...f5.[^s1] Pavlovic's main line runs 10...f5 11.Bd3 Be6 12.O-O Bxd5 13.exd5 Ne7, where Black exchanges the d5-knight at once and the structure changes: White gets a queenside pawn majority and a space-gaining pawn on d5, Black a kingside majority, and both sides typically keep a strong minor piece.[^s1][^s3] The main alternative is 9.Nd5 Be7, keeping the pawn structure intact, to which Pavlovic devotes two chapters.[^s1]

```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e5 6.Ndb5 d6 7.Bg5 a6 8.Na3 b5
9.Bxf6 gxf6 10.Nd5 f5 11.Bd3 Be6 12.O-O Bxd5 13.exd5 Ne7
```

## Typical plans and pitfalls

For White the d5-square is the strategic anchor: a knight there is an ideal **outpost**, and White wants to keep a minor piece on it while bringing the offside a3-knight back into play (Pavlovic even examines the retreat 9.Nab1).[^s1] Sharp sacrificial tries also exist; Pavlovic covers the piece sacrifices 11.Nxb5 and 11.Bxb5 after 10...f5, noting that the latter is less popular now because Black has found resources.[^s1] For Black the danger is passivity: if White settles a knight on d5 and Black never achieves ...f5 or the exchange ...Bxd5, the backward d6-pawn and the weak light squares become lasting targets.[^s1] Because the main line after 7.Bg5 is so deeply analysed, many White players now steer for sidelines. 7.Nd5 Nxd5 8.exd5 is the "trendy" option in Pavlovic's book, in which White fixes the d5-pawn immediately and plays for a queenside majority.[^s1] Commentators at the 2018 match stressed timing: in such positions Black's kingside play must arrive before White's b4–c5 advance breaks through.[^s4] Earlier deviations such as 6.Nf5, 6.Nde2 and 6.Nb3 are covered as sidelines.[^s1]

## In practice

The variation took centre stage in the 2018 world championship. In game 8 Fabiano Caruana switched to 3.d4, Carlsen answered with the Sveshnikov, and Caruana's prepared 7.Nd5 gave him a noticeable edge before the game was drawn.[^s4][^s3] In game 10 Caruana again entered the Sveshnikov and surprised Carlsen with 12.b4; the game, double-edged throughout, was also drawn.[^s5] The database statistics for B33 show a broadly balanced record across more than thirteen thousand games, as of the page's 2025 coverage.[^s6]

## Connections

The Sveshnikov is a variation of the **Sicilian Defence** and an **alternative to** the **Najdorf** and the **Dragon** as Black's main systems in the Open Sicilian.[^s6][^s2] It is **named after** **Evgeny Sveshnikov** and has been **championed by** **Magnus Carlsen**, who **played** it in the **2018 World Chess Championship**.[^s3][^s4] Strategically it is the textbook case of conceding an **outpost**: the d5 hole and the **backward** d6-pawn are **weak squares** Black accepts in return for activity, while the 9.Bxf6 line hands Black the **bishop pair**.[^s1][^s2]

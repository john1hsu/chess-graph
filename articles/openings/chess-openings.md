# Chess openings

The **opening** is the first of the three phases of a chess game, the stage in which piece development and control of the centre dominate play.[^s1] Britannica's entry on development puts the job of the phase in concrete terms: getting the pieces onto good squares within roughly the first 10 to 15 moves.[^s3] The word also names a body of knowledge. "Opening theory" is the accumulated analysis of named sequences — the Ruy Lopez, the Sicilian, the King's Indian — that players study so they reach the middlegame with a sound position, and Britannica treats opening analysis as a core part of chess theory as a whole.[^s1][^s2]

## Principles of opening play

Capablanca's *Chess Fundamentals* (1921) gives the classical rules of thumb in its section on the general strategy of the opening.[^s4] The aim is to bring the pieces into play as quickly as possible; he reckons that full development normally takes about eight moves.[^s4] He advises moving pieces rather than pawns, bringing out a knight (preferably the king's knight) before the bishops, and moving each piece only once before development is finished unless a second move wins material or is needed to free the piece.[^s4] A separate section stresses control of the centre, arguing that a violent attack has little chance without command of at least two central squares.[^s4] He also prizes developing moves that carry a threat, because they gain time by forcing a reply.[^s4] Siegbert Tarrasch condensed similar ideas into slogans such as developing knights before bishops and not bringing the queen out early, while Capablanca's own view was that every opening move should serve a definite plan.[^s2]

How much these rules matter depends on the pawns. Britannica notes that development counts for less in blocked, semi-closed structures and for much more once the centre or the kingside opens, when a lead in development becomes a decisive asset.[^s3]

## How openings are classified

Openings are named and grouped by their first moves: king's pawn openings (1.e4), queen's pawn openings (1.d4) and others such as 1.Nf3.[^s1] Teaching materials divide them further by Black's reply. The open games (1.e4 e5) and closed games (1.d4 d5) are each defined by a fixed pair of first moves.[^s7] The semi-closed games are those in which Black does not answer 1.d4 with 1...d5 — chiefly the Indian defences after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4, where Black stops 2.e4 with the knight and then attacks White's centre with pieces.[^s6] Flank openings such as the English, the Réti and the Bird are harder to pin down, since they are defined by development on the wings rather than by a fixed pair of first moves.[^s7]

The standard reference index is the ECO code system, created by Chess Informant for its *Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings*.[^s5] The encyclopaedia is organised in five volumes, A to E, each covering a range of codes; the B volume alone, covering B00 to B49 in its first part, now fills two books in its 2021 edition.[^s5]

## How opening ideas changed

Early printed books analysed only a few basic opening moves, and the 18th-century debate between Philidor's slow, pawn-based development and the Modena masters' fast mobilisation and gambits shaped later theory.[^s2] Paul Morphy showed that in open positions a lead in development seizes the initiative, often bought with a pawn sacrifice.[^s2] The **hypermoderns** then challenged the idea that the centre must be occupied early with pawns, preferring to control it from a distance with fianchettoed bishops and flexible replies like 1...Nf6.[^s1][^s2] The King's Indian Defence shows the result: after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6, Black allows White a broad pawn centre and plans to strike at it, or at White's flanks, later.[^s6] Soviet players built long-lived opening systems and prepared them against specific opponents, a habit Britannica credits especially to Botvinnik.[^s2]

## Studying openings, and common pitfalls

The usual advice for club players is to learn ideas before variations. Paul van der Sterren's *Fundamental Chess Openings* is praised in John Donaldson's review because it explains, mostly in prose, why each system is played, and seldom goes past move ten.[^s8] Typical mistakes follow from breaking the principles above: moving the same piece repeatedly, pushing edge pawns, grabbing material while behind in development, or recapturing with the wrong piece, all of which Capablanca illustrates with traps.[^s4]

## Connections

The opening is one phase of **chess**, followed by the middlegame and the **chess endgame**.[^s1] It has as parts the **open games**, **semi-open games**, **closed games**, **semi-closed games** and **flank openings**, the groups into which teaching courses sort the named lines.[^s6][^s7] Its principles were codified by **Siegbert Tarrasch** and **José Raúl Capablanca** and revised by **hypermodernism**.[^s2][^s4] Theory reaches players through reference works such as the *Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings* and idea-based guides; each player distils it into a personal **opening repertoire**, and engines draw on it through an **opening book**.[^s5][^s8]

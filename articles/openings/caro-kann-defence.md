# Caro-Kann Defence

The Caro-Kann Defence is the reply 1...c6 to 1.e4, preparing 2...d5 to contest the e4-pawn; the main position arises after 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5.[^s2] In the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings classification the starting move 1.e4 c6 is filed as B10, with later Caro-Kann lines receiving their own B1x codes (for example B15 for 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 e5).[^s5] It is regarded as one of Black's most reliable answers to 1.e4: a modern repertoire book by grandmaster Lars Schandorff calls it a top-tier defence and presents it as a counter-attacking weapon rather than a purely passive one.[^s3]

## Origins and name

Edward Winter's research shows that 1...c6 appeared sporadically long before it had a name, for example in a Liverpool game of 1847, and that Max Weiss played it four times at Nuremberg 1883.[^s1] Contemporaries, including Wilhelm Steinitz, credited the Viennese amateur Marcus Kann (died 1886) with introducing it into master practice, after which the Berlin player Horatio Caro analysed it; the earliest known Kann example is Csánk–Kann, Vienna 1884.[^s1] Caro wrote about "Caro's Eröffnung" in *Brüderschaft* in October 1886, and the joint name "Caro-Kann" first appeared in Curt von Bardeleben's article in the *Deutsche Schachzeitung* of July 1890.[^s1]

## Main ideas

Compared with the French Defence, Black challenges the centre with ...d5 without first shutting in the c8-bishop behind ...e6. The standard plan is to strike at e4, develop that bishop actively (typically to f5), bring the knights out via d7 and f6 or e7, and castle kingside.[^s2] The price is time: the early bishop sortie can be harried, and White often gets more pieces into play first, so Black's position is solid but must survive White's initiative before its counter-chances appear.[^s2]

## Main variations

- **Classical, 3.Nc3 (or 3.Nd2) dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5.** After 5.Ng3 Bg6 6.h4 h6 7.Nf3 Nd7 8.h5 Bh7 9.Bd3 Bxd3 10.Qxd3, White has gained kingside space with tempo and exchanged Black's good bishop.[^s2]
- **4...Nd7.** Black prepares ...Ngf6 so that a knight exchange on f6 can be met by a knight recapture; Anatoly Karpov used this system often, and Black must know the sharp 5.Ng5 lines.[^s2]
- **4...Nf6.** After 5.Nxf6, Black chooses between the dynamic ...gxf6 (open g-file, damaged pawns) and the calmer ...exf6.[^s2] Schandorff's repertoire adopts 5.Nxf6 exf6.[^s3]
- **Advance, 3.e5.** Black can develop with 3...Bf5 4.Nf3 e6 5.Be2, where White's lead in development counts for less because the centre is closed.[^s2] The other main approach is the immediate 3...c5; after 4.dxc5 e6 5.a3 Bxc5 Black regains the pawn with the bishop and plays against White's advanced e5-pawn.[^s4] A recent repertoire for White notes that top players increasingly choose the Advance because it leaves more scope to outplay the opponent in the middlegame.[^s4]
- **Exchange and Panov, 3.exd5 cxd5.** The quiet 4.Bd3 Nc6 5.c3 Nf6 makes it harder for Black to develop the c8-bishop, while the Panov Attack 4.c4 Nf6 5.Nc3 pressures d5 and aims for fast development.[^s2]

## Example and pitfalls

The defining position after 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 already shows the tension: White must decide whether to defend e4 (3.Nc3, 3.Nd2), advance past it (3.e5) or release it (3.exd5).[^s2] Typical errors include losing time with the f5-bishop when Ng3 or h4–h5 gain tempi on it, accepting a shattered structure with ...gxf6 without the activity to justify it, and capturing ...dxc4 in the Panov, which hands White rapid development of the f1-bishop.[^s2] In the Advance with 3...c5, declining to recapture on c5 at once carries some risk.[^s4]

## Connections

The Caro-Kann is a **semi-open game** and a natural **alternative to the Sicilian Defence** and the **French Defence**, sharing the French's ...d5 counter-thrust but freeing the queen's bishop first.[^s2] It is **named after Horatio Caro and Marcus Kann**.[^s1] The Exchange and **Panov–Botvinnik Attack** lines lead to **isolated queen's pawn** structures for White, and the Nd7 system is associated with **Anatoly Karpov**.[^s2] The graph also links it to **Deep Blue versus Kasparov**, whose article analyses a Caro-Kann game from that match.

# Queen's Gambit

The Queen's Gambit is the opening 1.d4 d5 2.c4: White attacks Black's central d5-pawn with a wing pawn, offering it in exchange for the chance to build a broad centre.[^s1] Chessgames.com files the position after 2.c4 under ECO code D06.[^s7] The name overstates the sacrifice. USCF master James Eade calls the word "gambit" a stretch, because Black cannot really keep the extra pawn and White normally expects to win it back; the same position can also arise from 1.c4 d5 2.d4.[^s2] The opening is generally associated with slow, strategic pressure rather than early tactical fireworks.[^s2][^s1]

## History

Chess.com's opening reference traces the earliest record of the Queen's Gambit to the Göttingen manuscript of about 1490, with analysis by Alessandro Salvio and Gioachino Greco in the early seventeenth century, but it notes that masters did not regularly open with 1.d4 until the 1890s.[^s1] As positional play gained ground the opening became standard at elite level in the early twentieth century, and Alekhine and Capablanca relied on it in most games of their 1927 World Championship match.[^s1] Grandmaster Milos Pavlovic observes that the Declined has been one of the most popular choices in World Championship matches and lists Capablanca, Lasker, Alekhine, Spassky, Karpov, Kramnik and Anand among the champions who used it regularly.[^s5] Britannica notes that Mikhail Botvinnik's analysis of the Queen's Gambit Declined in the late 1930s was still winning him games nearly a decade later, and that the sharp Botvinnik Variation sees Black give up a pawn and loosen the kingside for compensation.[^s6]

## Main ideas

White's 2.c4 tries to remove or undermine Black's d5-pawn; if d5 disappears, White can follow up with e2–e4 and a full pawn centre.[^s2] Black's choice on move two decides the character of the game.[^s1]

- **Slav Defence, 2...c6.** Grandmaster Paul van der Sterren explains that ...c6 holds d5 as firmly as ...e6 while leaving the c8–h3 diagonal open, so the queen's bishop can come out to f5 or g4; it also prepares ...dxc4 and ...b5 to hold a pawn on c4, which makes White's choices sharper.[^s3] Chess.com lists it as Black's most popular reply.[^s1]
- **Queen's Gambit Declined, 2...e6.** The most classical answer keeps the centre solid but locks in the c8-bishop, which is why Black often looks for ...c5 or ...dxc4 followed by ...c5 or ...e5.[^s1][^s5] Pavlovic stresses that the Declined is not sharp, yet a small inaccuracy can leave Black passive for a long time.[^s5]
- **Queen's Gambit Accepted, 2...dxc4.** Black takes the pawn not to keep it but to gain time and strike back in the centre with ...c5 or ...e5, often leaving White with an isolated d-pawn.[^s2][^s4] In his 2023 monograph, Max Dlugy shows that Black can meet the central 3.e4 with 3...e5 and 4.Nf3 Bb4+, and that many endgames are balanced, with Black's queenside majority offsetting White's central space.[^s4]
- **Albin Countergambit, 2...e5.** A risky counter-sacrifice that masters seldom choose, though it scores well at lower levels.[^s1]

## Typical lines and pitfalls

A characteristic Declined position arises after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 d5 4.Nc3 Be7 5.Bg5 h6 6.Bxf6 Bxf6: White has given up the bishop pair for time, and Black must decide when to release the tension with ...dxc4, which Pavlovic presents as the correct reaction in several of these lines.[^s5] In the Slav, after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 dxc4 5.a4 Bf5, van der Sterren describes 5.a4 as White's most common and best try, stopping ...b5 before regaining the pawn.[^s3] In the Accepted, the line 3.e4 e5 4.Nf3 Bb4+ 5.Bd2 Bxd2+ 6.Nbxd2 sees White effectively offer a pawn for development, and Dlugy warns that each side must play precisely.[^s4]

Common errors run in both directions. For Black, a passive set-up that leaves the c8-bishop shut in can leave White with a lasting edge, and in the Accepted Dlugy's lines stress quick central counterplay with ...e5 against d4 rather than clinging to the extra pawn.[^s4][^s5] For White, the isolated d-pawn that often results is double-edged: Eade points out that it is a weakness if blockaded, but it can also power an attack when it advances to d5.[^s2] Symmetrical lines such as the Slav Exchange (3.cxd5 cxd5) are famously drawish when handled passively, though van der Sterren argues that White has more ambitious ways to play them.[^s3]

## Connections

The Queen's Gambit is the central opening of the **closed games** (1.d4 d5). It **leads to** the **Queen's Gambit Declined**, the **Queen's Gambit Accepted**, the **Slav Defence** and the **Albin Countergambit** as its main named branches.[^s1][^s3] The Accepted is a main source of the **isolated queen's pawn** structure.[^s2][^s4] Pavlovic pairs the Declined with the **Nimzo-Indian Defence** as a complete repertoire against 1.d4, since both arise after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6.[^s5] Among the champions who relied on it, **José Raúl Capablanca** used it in his 1927 title match with Alekhine.[^s1]

# Slav Defence

The Slav Defence is Black's answer 2...c6 to the Queen's Gambit, reached after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6, and is filed under D10 in the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings.[^s6] Like the Queen's Gambit Declined it keeps a firm hold on d5, but it leaves the c8–h3 diagonal open, so Black's queen's bishop can still come out to f5 or g4; the c6-pawn also supports ...b5, so a pawn captured on c4 can be defended at once.[^s1] Repertoire authors rank it, alongside the Queen's Gambit Accepted and Declined, among the hardest defences for White to face.[^s3]

## History

Grandmaster Bryan Smith notes that 2...c6 was largely neglected early in the twentieth century in favour of 2...e6, even though it guards d5 without blocking the bishop.[^s5] Paul van der Sterren describes it as a classical opening that became popular through the 1935 and 1937 world championship matches between Alexander Alekhine and Max Euwe, in which both players adopted it in turn.[^s1] Smith credits Euwe, for whom it was the main defence to 1.d4, with much of the earliest theory of the open lines with ...dxc4; in 1935 the decisive games 20 and 21 were both Slavs, which Euwe won with Black and then with White from the same position.[^s5] Van der Sterren adds that Vasily Smyslov kept the Slav Accepted in use in the 1950s, and that Botvinnik, Portisch and Kasparov succeeded with the Exchange Variation.[^s1]

## Key ideas

Black's typical plan is to capture on c4 and hold the pawn with ...b5; if Black takes without being able to follow up with ...b5, White simply wins the pawn back.[^s4] Black gives up the centre for a while and aims to challenge it later with pieces or the ...e6–e5 lever.[^s4] Van der Sterren observes that 2...c6 forces White to choose between restrained development (Nf3, Nc3, Bf4 or Bg5) and sharper play that may involve giving up a pawn.[^s1]

## Main variations

- **Slav Accepted, 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 dxc4.** Van der Sterren calls 5.a4 the most common move and White's best chance for an edge: it stops ...b5 at the cost of time and the b4-square, and 5...Bf5 is by far Black's most popular reply.[^s1] Smith gives 6.Ne5 Nbd7 7.Nxc4 Qc7 as the best continuation from Euwe's era.[^s5] The gambit 5.e4 b5 6.e5 Nd5 7.a4 e6 leaves Black a pawn up in a sharp position, while 5.e3 b5 6.a4 b4 is a more cautious approach.[^s1]
- **Semi-Slav, 4...e6.** Black keeps the tension and reinforces d5, accepting a temporarily restricted c8-bishop; it leads to some of the most complicated lines of the Queen's Gambit, including the Meran and Botvinnik systems.[^s1][^s4]
- **Chebanenko, 4...a6.** A provocative move that has become popular since the late 1980s and is played by top players despite its odd appearance.[^s1][^s3]
- **Exchange, 3.cxd5 cxd5.** Dreev presents it as a positional way to avoid the main Slav lines, using White's extra tempo in a symmetrical position to fight for a small edge.[^s2] Van der Sterren regrets its reputation for prearranged draws and calls it a serious way of playing.[^s1]

## Example and pitfalls

The Slav Accepted after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 dxc4 5.a4 Bf5 shows the opening's bargain: Black has developed the problem bishop actively and keeps the c4-pawn for now, while White has prevented ...b5 and will regain the pawn with e3 and Bxc4 or with Ne5xc4.[^s1][^s4] Common errors include taking on c4 without the ...b5 follow-up, developing the bishop too early with 4...Bf5, which van der Sterren shows is met by 5.cxd5 cxd5 6.Qb3 Qb6 7.Nxd5, and playing the Exchange passively, as in the drawish sequence 4.Nc3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Nf6 6.Bf4 Bf5 7.e3 e6 8.Bd3 Bxd3 9.Qxd3 Bd6 10.Bxd6 Qxd6 11.O-O O-O.[^s1][^s4] Dreev notes that modern computer-aided preparation has made the Exchange less comfortable for Black than its old reputation suggested.[^s2]

## Connections

The Slav is a branch of the **Queen's Gambit** and an **alternative to the Queen's Gambit Declined** and the **Queen's Gambit Accepted**, differing from the Declined chiefly in keeping the light-squared bishop free, a point linked to **good and bad bishops**.[^s1] It **leads to** the **Semi-Slav Defence** after 4...e6.[^s1][^s4] It was **championed by Max Euwe and Alexander Alekhine** in their title matches, and the Exchange Variation was a weapon of **Garry Kasparov**.[^s1][^s5]

# Nimzo-Indian Defence

The Nimzo-Indian Defence is the position after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4, filed under E20 in the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings.[^s6][^s7] Instead of occupying the centre with ...d5 at once, Black pins the c3-knight, which is White's main support for the e4 advance, and so contests the centre with pieces from a distance; John Emms describes it as a sound, reliable defence built on this hypermodern idea of long-range central control.[^s4] Grandmaster Michael Roiz, who helped prepare the opening for the 2012 Anand–Gelfand world championship match, calls it one of the most complex openings strategically, with positions that are hard even for grandmasters.[^s3] It remains highly popular at all levels.[^s5]

## Origins and name

The move order has older forerunners: Edward Winter cites a game from Germany in 1861 with an early ...Bb4+ in a Queen's Gambit, and a 1917 game in Triberg, Rabinovich–Selesniev, that already reached 3.Nc3 Bb4.[^s1] Aron Nimzowitsch later wrote that he discovered 3.Nc3 Bb4 in 1913, the year he published his "new system", and he claimed the opening's origin in *My System*, mocking the label "Bogoljubow Opening".[^s1] Britannica credits him with developing the "Nimzowitsch-Indian" defence as part of the hypermodern theories set out in *My System* (1925).[^s2] The name settled slowly: Viennese magazines in 1925 simply wrote "Indisch", Tartakower in 1930 spoke of the "Variante de Nimzowitch", and in 1931 Hans Kmoch recommended "Nimzoindisch" in a footnote to an Alekhine consultation game.[^s1]

## Main ideas

The opening revolves around a few recurring imbalances. Emms names the central ones: Black's healthier pawn structure against White's bishop pair and centre, and Black's lead in development against White's bishop pair.[^s4] If Black plays ...Bxc3 and White recaptures bxc3, White gets doubled c-pawns, and much of Black's play aims at the c4-pawn and the weakened structure, with ...b6 and ...Ba6 or ...Bb7, central breaks such as ...c5 and ...d5, or ...e5 to open lines.[^s4] Where White avoids the doubled pawns, Black often uses a lead in development instead.[^s4] The e4-square is a second theme: in several lines a Black knight on e4 is a key resource, and in others the pin on c3 itself supports Black's counterplay.[^s3]

## Main variations

White's fourth move sets the character of the game, and both books cited here organise their material around it.[^s3][^s4]

- **4.e3**, the **Rubinstein**, is a solid developing system; a common main line runs 4...0-0 5.Bd3 d5 6.Nf3 c5 7.0-0 Nc6 8.a3 Bxc3 9.bxc3 Qc7.[^s6][^s4]
- **4.Qc2**, the **Classical** Variation, prepares to recapture on c3 with the queen to avoid doubled pawns.[^s4]
- **4.a3**, the **Sämisch**, forces the issue at once and accepts doubled pawns for the bishop pair and a broad centre.[^s4]
- **4.f3** prepares e4 directly (365Chess lists it as the Kmoch Variation), **4.Bg5** is the Leningrad, and **4.Nf3** can lead to the Kasparov Variation or the Romanishin System; 4.g3, 4.Qb3 and 4.Bd2 are further tries.[^s4][^s3][^s6]

## Example and pitfalls

The Rubinstein line above shows the typical bargain.[^s6] After 8...Bxc3 9.bxc3 Black has given up the dark-squared bishop, and White has doubled c-pawns but also the bishop pair and the prospect of a central push with e3–e4; Black's plans against such structures include pressure on c4 and the ...e5 break to open lines.[^s4] As a rule of thumb drawn from those imbalances, Black should not part with the bishop on c3 without a concrete plan against the pawns, since White's bishops gain strength as the position opens; White, in lines that avoid the doubled pawns, must catch up in development before Black's lead becomes decisive.[^s4]

## Connections

The Nimzo-Indian is one of the **Indian defences**, the family of 1.d4 Nf6 systems, and it was **developed by and named after Aron Nimzowitsch**, the leading figure of **hypermodernism**.[^s1][^s2] After 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Black chooses 3...Bb4 instead of 3...d5, so the Nimzo-Indian is an **alternative to the Queen's Gambit Declined** within the wider world of the **Queen's Gambit**.[^s7][^s6] Its central trade-off, structure against the **bishop pair**, makes it a standard teaching ground for **pawn structure**, and its fourth-move systems lead into named lines such as the **Rubinstein** and **Sämisch** variations.[^s4][^s3]

# Ruy Lopez

The Ruy Lopez, or Spanish Game, begins 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5. White develops a kingside piece, clears the way to castle at once, and puts indirect pressure on e5 by attacking the knight that guards it.[^s2] It is one of the open games and is now the most frequently chosen continuation after 1.e4 e5; play usually becomes strategically rich rather than tactically chaotic, and the main lines have appeared in many world championship matches.[^s2]

## Name and history

The opening is named after Ruy López de Segura, a Spanish priest from Zafra who is regarded as the first modern chess writer and analyst; his manual *Libro de la invención liberal y arte del juego del axedrez* appeared in 1561.[^s1] Britannica describes him as the opening's developer rather than its inventor.[^s1] A 2025 Spanish-language biography by Daniel and Francisco de Asís Gragera Ledesma, based on about five years of archival research, gives his birth as around 1530 and re-examines the traditional stories of his matches with Italian players.[^s7]

## Main ideas

The pressure from 3.Bb5 is indirect: the bishop targets the defender of e5 rather than the pawn itself, and it works as a long-term lever. White castles quickly and prepares d4 with c3, while Black must choose how to hold a foothold in the centre, whether by counterattack, piece activity or solid defence.[^s2] In the classical main line, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 6.Re1 b5 7.Bb3 d6 8.c3 O-O 9.h3, White spends nine moves preparing the central advance d4, and Black has several systems in reply.[^s2] Among them are the Breyer, with its knight retreat ...Nb8 credited to Gyula Breyer, and the Zaitsev, formulated by Igor Zaitsev and used by Anatoly Karpov as a mainstay in his title matches against Garry Kasparov; both remain in top-level use.[^s4] A modern White repertoire still recommends 9.h3 and treats the Zaitsev, Breyer and Chigorin as Black's chief replies.[^s5]

## Main variations

- **Berlin Defence, 3...Nf6.** Black heads for an early queen exchange and a slightly worse but very solid endgame, relying on the bishop pair; top players have found it hard to beat.[^s2] Its revival dates from the 2000 world title match in London, where Vladimir Kramnik used it to neutralise Kasparov's attacking preparation and won the match.[^s6]
- **Marshall Attack, 3...a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 6.Re1 b5 7.Bb3 O-O 8.c3 d5.** A true gambit: Black gives up the e5-pawn for lasting attacking chances, and the forced lines are so deeply analysed that weak preparation is quickly punished.[^s2] White often sidesteps it with anti-Marshall moves such as 8.a4.[^s5]
- **Open Variation, 5.O-O Nxe4.** Black takes the e4-pawn, which White soon wins back; Black gets active pieces, White a kingside majority.[^s2]
- **Arkhangelsk, 5.O-O b5 6.Bb3 Bb7.** Black plays for immediate counterplay, often adding ...Bc5 so both bishops aim at White's king; White answers with c3 and d4.[^s2] Fabiano Caruana's 2020 work reshaped the theory of this and the related Möller line.[^s5]
- **Exchange Variation, 4.Bxc6.** White gives up the bishop pair at once to saddle Black with doubled c-pawns, a weakness that tells in the endgame; Bobby Fischer used it in the 1972 world championship match.[^s2]

## Pitfalls

Each Black system carries its own risk: the Marshall demands exact knowledge of forced lines, the Open Variation's temporary pawn win opens the position and requires precise play, and the Exchange Variation leaves Black with doubled c-pawns that can become a lasting endgame weakness.[^s2] For White, the drawback is the reverse: against the Berlin even top players have struggled to show an advantage.[^s2] In the Marshall, history shows the danger of improvising: Winter's study of Capablanca–Marshall, New York, 23 October 1918, notes that Capablanca judged Black's attack powerful but doubtful, and he won by mating Marshall.[^s3] Winter also rejects the popular story that Marshall had saved the gambit for years specifically for Capablanca.[^s3]

## Connections

The Ruy Lopez sits under the **open games** and is the main rival to the **Italian Game** after 2...Nc6.[^s2] It is **named after Ruy López de Segura**.[^s1] It **leads to** the **Berlin Defence**, the **Marshall Attack** and the **Open Ruy Lopez**, and it has been **championed** by **Anatoly Karpov** (Zaitsev system) and **Vladimir Kramnik** (Berlin) in **world championship** play.[^s4][^s6] Like the **Caro-Kann Defence** on the semi-open side, it is a standard choice for players who prefer long strategic struggles.[^s2]

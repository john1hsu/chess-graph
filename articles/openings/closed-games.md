# Closed games

**Closed games** are the openings in which Black answers 1.d4 with 1...d5, so that both queen's pawns stand face to face in the centre.[^s1] In the *Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings* scheme they make up the core of Volume D, which covers all double queen's pawn openings (plus some Grünfeld-type lines reached via 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6).[^s4] The name, like "open" and "semi-open", is a traditional label: it reflects that the d5-pawn is supported and hard to exchange early, so the centre tends to stay blocked or semi-blocked rather than opening quickly, but a closed game can still become sharp.[^s1][^s2] Together with the open games (1.e4 e5) and the semi-open games (1.e4 with another reply), it is one of the three classical families of openings.

## The main members

ChessBase's opening primer groups the closed games around the Queen's Gambit, 2.c4, and a few systems without it:[^s1]

- **Queen's Gambit Declined** (2.c4 e6, main line 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nf3): Black holds d5 with the e-pawn; very solid, but the c8-bishop is shut in behind e6.[^s1]
- **Slav** (2.c4 c6): d5 is supported by the c-pawn, so the light-squared bishop stays free, at the cost of somewhat slower kingside development.[^s1]
- **Semi-Slav** (2.c4 c6 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 e6): Black combines ...c6 and ...e6 and threatens to take on c4 and hold the pawn; both 5.e3 and 5.Bg5 are deeply analysed elite main lines.[^s1]
- **Queen's Gambit Accepted** (2.c4 dxc4): Black gives up the centre for the c-pawn; trying to keep it too long hands White a strong initiative.[^s1]
- **Catalan** (2.c4 e6 3.g3): White fianchettoes the king's bishop to press along the long diagonal.[^s1]
- **Queen's Pawn systems** without c4, such as set-ups with Nf3, Bf4, e3 and c3: a safe, closed centre with simple plans and little theory, popular with amateurs.[^s1]

Because 2.c4 can usually be answered in several sound ways, a Black repertoire after 1...d5 must cover the Queen's Gambit plus the Catalan, the London System, the Torre Attack and other second moves.[^s5]

## History

Britannica's history of opening theory notes that before the Hypermodern revolution masters almost always met 1.d4 with 1...d5.[^s2] José Raúl Capablanca, who played both king's and queen's pawn openings, preferred 1.d4 because it kept the centre closed, and Steinitz's positional principles were developed especially for such blocked or semi-blocked centres, where slow manoeuvring is rewarded.[^s2] After the First World War Nimzowitsch and the Hypermoderns replied to 1.d4 with 1...Nf6, controlling e4 with pieces, and their Nimzo-Indian and Queen's Indian Defences took a large share of 1.d4 play away from 1...d5.[^s2] The classical reply never disappeared: Mikhail Botvinnik's late-1930s analysis of the Queen's Gambit Declined produced, in the Botvinnik Variation, a deeply unbalanced middlegame in which Black sacrifices a pawn and weakens the kingside for queenside chances, and it was still winning him games about a decade later.[^s2][^s3] A modern repertoire book for Black calls the Declined the most prestigious of openings and notes that most World Champions have played it with both colours for over a century.[^s5]

## Typical ideas and pitfalls

The defining tension is White's attack on d5 with c4, and Black's choice of how to support it, with ...e6, with ...c6, or by giving it up with ...dxc4.[^s1] Each choice has a cost. In the Declined the problem is the c8-bishop; in the Slav it is development speed; in the Accepted it is the centre, which White claims with e4.[^s1][^s6] Lars Schandorff's grandmaster guide for White follows a consistent plan: active development, central pawn space and, where possible, the e4 break, for example with f3 and e4 in exchange-type positions after cxd5 exd5 and Bg5, or e4 against the Accepted and the Semi-Slav.[^s6] Pawn structures matter more than memorised lines: each chapter of Ntirlis's repertoire begins with its typical plans and structures before the variations.[^s5] The practical pitfall for Black is passivity; a solid closed centre is not a licence to wait, and Black must find a freeing break such as ...c5 or ...e5, or a fight for e4, before White's space advantage becomes permanent.[^s1][^s6]

## Example

The Queen's Gambit Declined main line shows the family's character: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nf3, with both sides developing behind a fixed central pawn on d5 and White's c-pawn keeping up the pressure.[^s1]

## Connections

Closed games sit under the general heading of **chess openings**, beside the **open games** and **semi-open games**, and **contrast with** the open games, in which the centre usually opens early.[^s1] The family **has as parts** the **Queen's Gambit**, with its **Declined**, **Accepted** and **Slav** branches, and the **Catalan**.[^s1] Its history is tied to **hypermodernism**, whose 1...Nf6 systems challenged the dominance of 1...d5, and the exchange-type positions with cxd5 exd5 that White's repertoire books aim for connect it to pawn-structure topics such as the **Carlsbad structure**.[^s2][^s6]

# Dragon Variation

The Dragon Variation is the branch of the Open Sicilian reached after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 g6, classified as B70 in the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings; database pages file Yugoslav Attack games under later codes such as B75.[^s5] Black puts the king's bishop on g7, where it speeds development, eyes White's queenside and guards the king, and castles short.[^s1] The price is a fixed target: the pawn on g6 gives White a hook for an h-pawn advance, and the d5-square can become a weakness unless Black achieves the freeing ...d5.[^s1]

## Name and history

Where the name comes from is not settled. Edward Winter records that Fyodor Dus-Chotimirsky later claimed to have coined it after studying astronomy in 1901, seeing the constellation Draco in Black's pawns; Winter's earliest printed instance is a Dutch reference to the "drakevariant" in 1925, with German writers using "Drachenvariante" around 1924, one of them tying it to Louis Paulsen.[^s4] Anish Giri repeats the more common explanation that the d6–e7–f7–g6–h7 pawn chain resembles a dragon, while gently doubting it.[^s3] Giri also notes that the Dragon long carried a reputation as dubious at the top level yet stays popular among club players, and Gawain Jones traces a strong British tradition in it back to Harry Golombek and B. H. Wood in the 1940s.[^s3][^s1]

## Main ideas: the Yugoslav Attack

White's most testing reply is the Yugoslav Attack, 6.Be3 Bg7 7.f3 0-0 8.Qd2 Nc6, followed by long castling and a kingside pawn storm.[^s1][^s3] The plan is direct: push h4–h5 to open the h-file against g6, and play Bh6 to exchange Black's key defender on g7.[^s3] Black races on the other wing, using the half-open c-file, ...Qa5 and pawn advances such as ...b5–b4 to reach White's king first; Giri stresses that once the g7-bishop is traded, Black's king is far more exposed, so timing is everything.[^s3]

Two ninth moves define the modern debate:

- **9.Bc4**, stopping ...d5 and pointing at f7. Black's classical answer is 9...Bd7 10.0-0-0 Rc8 11.Bb3, after which 11...Nxd4 12.Bxd4 b5 is the Topalov Variation; 11...Ne5 with ...h5 against h4 gives the Soltis Variation; and 10...Rb8 is the newer "Chinese Dragon".[^s1] Giri instead recommends 9...Nxd4 10.Bxd4 Be6, challenging the bishop at once.[^s3]
- **9.0-0-0**, allowing 9...d5. Jones describes 10.exd5 Nxd5 11.Nxc6 bxc6 12.Bd4 as the main line of the whole Dragon, with Black's play coming down the half-open b-file and in the centre.[^s2]

```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 g6 6.Be3 Bg7 7.f3 O-O 8.Qd2 Nc6
9.Bc4 Bd7 10.O-O-O Rc8 11.Bb3 Nxd4 12.Bxd4 b5
```

In this Topalov position Black has offered the b-pawn to rip open lines towards c3 and the white king, while White wants to push the h-pawn as fast as possible.[^s1]

## Typical plans and pitfalls

The exchange sacrifice ...Rxc3 is the Dragon's signature weapon: Black gives a rook for the c3-knight to break White's queenside pawn cover and remove a defender of e4; Jones cites Ivanchuk–Carlsen, Bilbao 2008, as a model.[^s1] Defensive sacrifices occur too: Jones cites Radjabov–Carlsen, Baku 2008, where a sacrifice on g5 deflected White's f-pawn and handed Black the e5-square.[^s1] Common errors for Black are drifting into slow manoeuvres while White's h-pawn advances, or allowing Bh6 to trade the g7-bishop without obtaining counterplay; Giri recommends meeting the h-pawn with ...h6 or ...g5 in some lines to keep the h-file closed.[^s3] For White, Jones's assessment is that if White avoids the sharpest lines Black usually gets a comfortable game, so quieter systems such as 6.Be2 (the Classical) or 6.f4 (the Levenfish) rarely trouble a prepared Dragon player.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The Dragon is a variation of the **Sicilian Defence** and an **alternative** to the **Najdorf** (5...a6) and the **Sveshnikov** among Black's main fifth-move systems.[^s5][^s1] It **leads to** the **Yugoslav Attack**, its defining battleground, and its play revolves around **king safety** after opposite-side **castling** and the **exchange sacrifice** on c3.[^s1][^s3] Black can also reach the set-up by the move order 2...g6, which Jones discusses before settling on 2...d6.[^s1] Structurally, the d5-square is a potential **outpost** for White, and a recapture on d5 can leave Black with a **backward** e7-pawn, so the ...d5 break is a recurring strategic goal.[^s1][^s2]

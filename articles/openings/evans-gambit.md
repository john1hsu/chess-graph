# Evans Gambit

The Evans Gambit arises after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.b4. White offers the b-pawn so that, after 4...Bxb4, the move c2-c3 comes with gain of time against the bishop, followed by d2-d4 to seize the centre and open lines towards the black king.[^s1][^s3] It is a branch of the Giuoco Piano and is classified under C51 in the ECO system, the code Chessgames.com assigns to Kasparov's famous win with it.[^s7]

## Origins and the nineteenth century

The gambit is named after William Davies Evans (1790–1872), a Welsh sea captain.[^s1][^s3] According to GM Bryan Smith, Evans met Alexander McDonnell in London while on shore leave around 1826–27; in their first game b2-b4 came one move late, after 4.O-O d6, and Evans later played the proper gambit against McDonnell.[^s5] Andrew Martin's new monograph dates the first Evans–McDonnell game to London 1829, and C.J.S. Purdy wrote that the inventor won it.[^s2][^s1] Printed analysis followed in *Le Palamède* in 1838.[^s1]

The opening spread through practice: McDonnell and Labourdonnais both used it in their 1834 match, and Staunton, Anderssen and Morphy played it in the following decades.[^s5] Anderssen's 1852 win over Jean Dufresne, the "Evergreen Game", is the most celebrated example.[^s3] Tarrasch remarked that no opening had received more analysis or produced more beautiful games, yet by 1922 the German *Handbuch* judged it much less feared than when Tarrasch and Lasker wielded it.[^s1] Steinitz played it as a young attacker, then became its leading defender; his duels with Mikhail Chigorin were seen as a test of the gambit and of attacking versus defensive chess.[^s5]

## Main lines

- **Accepted, 4...Bxb4 5.c3**: the bishop must move again. The classical retreats are 5...Ba5 and 5...Bc5, for example 5...Ba5 6.O-O Nf6 7.d4 or 5...Bc5 6.O-O d6.[^s1] Martin's repertoire also treats 5...Bd6.[^s2]
- **5...Be7**: GM Smith calls this the most solid choice, often giving Black easy equality by returning material at the right moment.[^s6]
- **Lasker's ideas**: a *British Chess Magazine* note of 1895 covers 5...Ba5 6.O-O Bb6, a line Lasker advocated, and Smith links "Lasker's Defence" with ...d6 to Lasker's 1897 game against Chigorin; if White regains the pawn by exchanging queens, only Black can be better in the resulting ending.[^s1][^s6]
- **Declined, 4...Bb6**: Black refuses the pawn and keeps the bishop on its diagonal; this Evans Gambit Declined appears in analysis from 1861 onward.[^s1]

## Plans and pitfalls

White's compensation is time: rapid development, a pawn centre after c3 and d4, open lines and pressure against f7.[^s3][^s2] An early Qb3 to hit f7 is a typical way to make Black's development awkward.[^s6] Black usually does best by giving back the pawn to reach a sound position, or by capturing on d4 and preparing ...d5 with the knight on e7 so that e4-e5 cannot gain space with tempo.[^s6] The danger for Black is greed: holding the extra pawn while the king stays in the centre is how most miniatures are lost. Martin argues that the tactics flow naturally from the position rather than from memorised lines, and calls the gambit fully playable for club players even if it is mainly a surprise weapon at the top.[^s2]

## Kasparov's revival

At the Tal Memorial in Riga in April 1995, Garry Kasparov played 4.b4 against Viswanathan Anand. Raymond Keene called it a complete shock, since the gambit had hardly been seen at elite level for about a century.[^s4] The game went 4...Bxb4 5.c3 Be7 6.d4 Na5 7.Be2 exd4 8.Qxd4 Nf6 9.e5 Nc6 10.Qh4 Nd5 11.Qg3 g6, and Keene judged that Black should have castled; Kasparov later gave up another pawn with 15.Bh6 to keep the king in the centre, and Anand resigned after 25.Re1.[^s4][^s7] Kasparov also beat Jeroen Piket with it that year but never repeated it, and later champions have mostly used it in rapid, blitz and simultaneous games.[^s3]

## Connections

The Evans Gambit is a branch of the **Italian Game** and a classic **gambit**, **named after** Captain **William Davies Evans**.[^s1] It belongs to the attacking culture of the **Romantic era**, and like the **King's Gambit** it faded from grandmaster practice as defensive technique improved.[^s3][^s5] **Wilhelm Steinitz** and **Mikhail Chigorin** fought over it, **Adolf Anderssen** **championed** it in the **Evergreen Game**, and **Garry Kasparov** revived it, as **exemplified by** his 1995 win over Anand in Riga.[^s5][^s3][^s4]

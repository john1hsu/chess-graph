# Gambit

A gambit is an opening in which one side deliberately gives up material, almost always a pawn, to buy something less tangible: time to develop, a stronger centre, open lines or attacking chances.[^s4] Britannica describes the gambits of the nineteenth century as speculative opening sacrifices made to mobilise quickly and open lines for an attack.[^s1] The opponent is never forced to take: a gambit can be accepted or declined, and it is called sound when the compensation is adequate and unsound when it is not.[^s4]

## The word and its history

The term is old. Edward Winter's survey of the word traces it to Ruy López, who derived it from the Italian for "leg", with the sense of tripping or setting traps, and who treated gambit games as the trappiest openings of his day.[^s3] English usage goes back at least to a 1656 book that promised "almost an hundred Gambetts".[^s3] Gambits flourished in the Romantic era, when Paul Morphy regularly gave up pawns so that his queen, rooks and bishops could reach the attack early.[^s1] The tide turned with the positional school. Wilhelm Steinitz held that a single pawn was usually decisive between first-class masters, and by José Raúl Capablanca's time Britannica records that gambits had almost disappeared at master level.[^s1] Winter collects the famous dictum, attributed to Steinitz in several unsourced versions, that the way to refute a gambit is to accept it, and he notes Siegbert Tarrasch's view that taking a gambit pawn is dangerous in principle.[^s3]

## What a gambit buys

The currency of a gambit is mobility. Edward Lasker argues that the mobility of the pieces, not the bare count of material, decides a game, and that a sacrifice is justified only when it is paid back by an advantage in position.[^s2] Chess.com lists the usual forms of compensation: a lead in development, gained tempi, more active pieces, weaknesses in the opponent's structure, or extra space.[^s4] Because these assets are dynamic, the gambiteer usually has to use them before the opponent consolidates; if the attack fades, the extra pawn tends to tell in the endgame.

Lasker also draws a terminological line. In his usage an opening counts as a gambit only if the pawn offered is not simply recaptured, which is why he notes that the established name "Centre Gambit" is strictly inaccurate.[^s2] By the same logic the Queen's Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4) is often described as one of the soundest gambits precisely because White can usually win the pawn back, for example with Bxc4, while keeping a strong centre.[^s4] Lasker shows that Black's attempt to hold the c4-pawn with 2...dxc4 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.e3 b5 5.a4 does not work, and recommends waiting until White's king's bishop has moved before capturing, so that the bishop loses a tempo.[^s2]

## Accepting, declining, returning

Defending against a gambit is a matter of judging which assets the opponent is really after. Lasker's recurring advice in the open games is to strike back in the centre with ...d5, even at the cost of giving the pawn back, rather than clinging to material while undeveloped.[^s2] Against the King's Gambit (1.e4 e5 2.f4) he recommends that beginners decline with 2...Bc5, and he presents the Falkbeer Countergambit, 2...d5 3.exd5 e4 4.d3 exd3 5.Qxd3, as a line where White is a pawn up but the queen obstructs his own bishop and Black should regain the pawn with good development.[^s2] Gambits are not only a White weapon: in the Benko Gambit (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 c5 3.d5 b5) Black gives a pawn for open a- and b-files and long-term queenside pressure.[^s4]

## Example: the Danish Gambit

The Danish Gambit, 1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3 dxc3 4.Bc4 cxb2 5.Bxb2 (see `fen`), is the purest illustration: White gives up two pawns in exchange for rapid development, with both bishops already in play.[^s2] Lasker's antidote is not to grab the second pawn but to play 3...d5, freeing the queen's bishop; after 4.exd5 Qxd5 he judges that Black develops at least as easily as White, so the gambit holds no terrors for a player who answers simply.[^s2] The pattern generalises: the most reliable reply to many gambits is to complete development and return material at the right moment, rather than to keep it at any price.

## Connections

A gambit is a special case of the **sacrifice**, made in the **chess openings**, and its compensation is counted in **development** and **tempo**, which it trades for the **initiative**.[^s2][^s4] It is the opposite bargain to a **material advantage**, which Steinitz valued so highly.[^s1] Individual gambits are its parts: the **King's Gambit**, the **Evans Gambit** and the **Danish Gambit**; the **Queen's Gambit** contrasts with them because the pawn can usually be regained.[^s2][^s4] The style is **championed by Paul Morphy** and is the signature of the **Romantic era**.[^s1]

# King's Gambit

The King's Gambit begins 1.e4 e5 2.f4 and is classified under ECO C30.[^s5] White offers the f-pawn so that, if Black takes it, White can play d2-d4 to build a broad pawn centre and use the half-open f-file against the weak point f7.[^s1][^s6] The price is the safety of White's own king, whose protective f-pawn has gone, and the black pawn on f4 can become an awkward thorn in White's position.[^s6]

## History

The opening is very old. GM Bryan Smith traces its earliest recorded example to a game by Ruy López in Rome in 1560, and the Danish writer Andreas Nissen, quoted by Edward Winter, called it age-old and of Italian origin.[^s3][^s2] It was the signature weapon of the Romantic era: Greco, McDonnell, Kieseritzky and Anderssen all played it, and Anderssen's win over Kieseritzky, the "Immortal Game", began with it.[^s3][^s2] Nissen recorded that Morphy valued it highly, while Pillsbury estimated in 1901 that, between equal players, correct attacking play would win only about two games in ten.[^s2]

With Steinitz and the classical school associated with Tarrasch, the gambit fell out of favour around the turn of the twentieth century, and between about 1910 and 1940 few masters used it; Chigorin and Rubinstein were notable exceptions.[^s3] Rudolf Spielmann, called the great champion of the gambit, admitted that he kept getting drawn games even out of it.[^s2] After the Second World War David Bronstein and Boris Spassky brought it back to elite practice.[^s3] Spassky played it 29 times in classical games, scoring 15 wins and 14 draws without a loss, and his first game against Bobby Fischer, at Mar del Plata in 1960, went 1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3.[^s4] Fischer overpressed and lost, and in 1961 he answered with the article "A Bust to the King's Gambit", recommending 3...d6.[^s4][^s3] Fischer even wrote that the gambit loses by force, a verdict John Shaw disputes in his 2013 monograph.[^s1]

## Main lines

- **Accepted, 2...exf4 3.Nf3**: the main line. Black can hold the pawn with 3...g5, after which 4.h4 g4 5.Ne5 is the Kieseritzky Gambit and 4.Nc3 the Quaade Variation; other defences include Fischer's 3...d6, Becker's 3...h6 and the central break 3...d5.[^s1][^s6]
- **Bishop's Gambit, 3.Bc4**: Bronstein suggested that this, not 3.Nf3, was White's winning try, but Shaw concludes that it favours Black.[^s1]
- **Modern Defence**: Smith argues that Black can equalise by striking at the centre and giving back material, with the move order 2...d5 3.exd5 exf4 4.Nf3 Nf6.[^s3]
- **Declined**: the Classical 2...Bc5 develops the bishop onto the diagonal of White's weakened king and has many sub-variations, while the Falkbeer Countergambit 2...d5 strikes back in the centre.[^s1][^s5][^s6]

## Plans and pitfalls

White's plan is rapid development, central domination with d2-d4, and pressure on f7 along the f-file.[^s6][^s1] Shaw recommends treating the opening positionally rather than as a blind attack, and favours long castling or a g2-g3 plan to undermine the f4-pawn.[^s1] For White the recurring risk is the exposed king, so careless development invites a counterattack before the pawn is regained.[^s6] For Black, greed is the danger: hanging on to the pawn with ...g5 while lagging in development is how most miniatures are lost.[^s3][^s6] Spassky's record shows that even a slightly worse opening position can be outplayed by a player who understands the resulting middlegames.[^s4]

## Assessment today

Smith calls it borderline sound but risky, and ChessBase Learn notes it is rare in top tournaments because Black has several reliable defences.[^s3][^s6] Shaw found no line that forces an advantage for Black after 3.Nf3 and judged the gambit playable up to the very highest level as a practical weapon.[^s1] Short, Morozevich and Judit Polgar have used it as an occasional surprise.[^s3]

## Connections

The King's Gambit is one of the **open games** and the archetypal **gambit**; it belongs to the culture of the **Romantic era** and is **exemplified by** the **Immortal Game**.[^s3] It is **countered** most directly by the **Falkbeer Countergambit** and by Fischer's 3...d6, and was **championed** after 1945 by **David Bronstein** and **Boris Spassky**.[^s1][^s3][^s4] Like the **Evans Gambit**, it trades a wing pawn for time and the centre, and faded from elite play as defensive technique improved.[^s3]

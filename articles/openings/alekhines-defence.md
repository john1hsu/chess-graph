# Alekhine's Defence

Alekhine's Defence is the reply 1...Nf6 to 1.e4. Black develops a piece at once and deliberately lets White take the initiative, planning to attack White's central pawns once they have advanced.[^s2] In the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings it begins at B02 (1.e4 Nf6), with 2.e5 Nd5 3.d4 filed as B03.[^s6][^s7] It belongs to the semi-open games, and a modern repertoire book describes its strategy as bold and ambitious: allow a big centre, then break it up, which suits players who like sharp, unbalanced positions.[^s2][^s3]

## Origins and name

The move is far older than its name. Edward Winter traces 1...Nf6 lines in Allgaier's manual (the fourth edition of 1819 already gives 2.e5 Nd5 3.c4 Nb6 4.d4 d6 5.f4), in Petroff's 1824 treatise, and in games of the 1860s and 1890s; the Moscow composer Mikhail Klyatskin is also reported to have used it before Alekhine.[^s1] Alexander Alekhine's earliest known games with it date from 1921: a consultation game in Zurich on 27 August, then two games at Budapest that September, a draw with Sämisch and a win against E. Steiner.[^s1] The name followed quickly. Sir George Thomas reported it "tentatively" in the *British Chess Magazine* in May 1922, the same month Hans Fahrni published the first monograph, *Die Aljechin-Verteidigung*.[^s1] By Budapest 1926 it was fashionable enough to appear fifteen times in one tournament, although Alekhine himself later cooled on it.[^s1]

## Main ideas

After 2.e5 the knight should go to the centre with 2...Nd5, not back to g8.[^s2] White typically gains further time with c4 (driving the knight to b6) and d4, building a pawn wedge; Black answers with ...d6 and pieces aimed at e5 and d4, such as ...Nc6, ...Bg4 pinning the f3-knight, or a kingside fianchetto with ...g6 and ...Bg7.[^s2] The whole defence is a wager: if White's pawns can be held and supported, they cramp Black; if Black's counter-attack arrives in time, they become targets.[^s2][^s3] Database figures show the bet is reasonable in practice: on Chessgames.com, games after 1.e4 Nf6 are almost evenly split between White and Black wins.[^s6]

## Main variations

- **Modern, 2.e5 Nd5 3.d4 d6 4.Nf3.** White settles for a smaller centre, protects e5 with the knight and develops quickly; 4...Bg4 is a main reply.[^s2] Black can also capture on e5, and should then recapture with the knight; the Larsen line 4...dxe5 5.Nxe5 Nd7 allows the speculative 6.Nxf7, which gives White at least a perpetual check.[^s2] The Sveshnikovs rate 4.Nf3 as a small plus for White.[^s4]
- **Exchange, 3.d4 d6 4.c4 Nb6 5.exd6.** White trades the e-pawn and plays on the remaining space advantage; Black chooses between ...exd6 and ...cxd6.[^s2][^s4]
- **Four Pawns Attack, 4.c4 Nb6 5.f4.** White sets up pawns on c4, d4, e5 and f4.[^s2] The Sveshnikovs call it one of the most dangerous lines for Black and give 5...dxe5 6.fxe5 c5 7.d5 g6 as the main reply, striking at the centre with the c-pawn and the fianchettoed bishop.[^s4]
- **Chase, 3.c4 Nb6 4.c5,** and the **3.Nc3** system are sidelines in which White chases or blocks the knight; the Sveshnikovs judge the Chase to give White only practical chances.[^s4]

## Pitfalls and example

The key positions show the balance of risks. After 1.e4 Nf6 2.e5 Nd5 3.d4 d6 4.c4 Nb6 5.f4 dxe5 6.fxe5 c5 7.d5 g6, Black has surrendered space but has the c5-pawn and the coming ...Bg7 bearing on White's advanced centre.[^s4] Typical errors for Black are passivity, failing to challenge the centre before White completes development, and the lazy retreat 2...Ng8.[^s2] White's typical error is the opposite: advancing pawns without the piece support that keeps them from becoming weaknesses.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Alekhine's Defence is a **semi-open game** and an **alternative to the Sicilian Defence** as a fighting answer to 1.e4; like the Caro-Kann it avoids 1...e5, but it provokes rather than restrains White's centre.[^s2] It is **named after Alexander Alekhine**, whose 1921 games gave it currency.[^s1] Its plan of letting White occupy the centre and then attacking it ties it to **hypermodernism**, and its sharpest test **leads to** the **Four Pawns Attack**.[^s3][^s4] It is linked to **Bobby Fischer** and **Fischer–Spassky 1972**: Fischer surprised Spassky with it in game 13 at Reykjavik, his first use of it at the highest level, and won after Spassky erred on move 69.[^s5]

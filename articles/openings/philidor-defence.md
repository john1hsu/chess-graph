# Philidor Defence

The Philidor Defence is the open game 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6, filed under ECO code C41.[^s7] Instead of defending the e5-pawn with a knight, Black uses the d-pawn, keeping the f8-bishop's diagonal partly closed but building a firm pawn chain. It is named after the eighteenth-century master François-André Danican Philidor, whose view that pawns are the soul of chess gives the opening its character: Black plays around the pawn structure rather than for quick piece activity.[^s1] Publishers and reviewers describe it as a secondary but durable answer to 1.e4, solid yet capable of producing sharp, dynamic play.[^s1][^s3][^s6]

## History and move orders

Philidor himself recommended 2...d6 with the plan of ...f5, striking at White's centre; modern theory regards that plan as risky because it weakens Black's kingside and costs time.[^s5] Later generations found that 3.d4 is White's most testing reply, and Trigo Urquijo notes that the old tries 3...Nf6 and 3...Nd7 came to be judged insufficient for full equality.[^s1] The classic snag is that after 3.d4 Nf6 White can play 4.dxe5 Nxe4 5.Qd5, when the queen hits the knight and f7 and Silman's review rates the line as good for White.[^s4] Black can also surrender the centre with 3...exd4: the Antoshin set-up 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 Be7 and the Larsen fianchetto 4.Nxd4 g6 both give real counterplay, but reviewers call them somewhat shaky.[^s4][^s6]

The fix was a change of move order. Today the "modern" Philidor is usually reached by 1.e4 d6 2.d4 Nf6 3.Nc3 e5, a Pirc-style route that gives White no chance for the 5.Qd5 trick; Black then plays ...Nbd7, ...Be7 and ...0-0.[^s1][^s6] Silman traces his interest to seeing Russian grandmasters reach the Hanham this way, through a Pirc move order.[^s4] Christian Bauer's *The Philidor Files* (Everyman Chess) covered both starting moves and helped a revival in which Bacrot, Nisipeanu, Morozevich, Svidler and Aronian used it; Trigo Urquijo's book adds Carlsen, Vachier-Lagrave, Caruana, Ivanchuk and Grischuk among its users.[^s3][^s6][^s1]

## Main ideas

The central system is the **Hanham** set-up, with the e5-pawn held by ...d6 and ...Nbd7, often followed by ...c6.[^s5] Its main line after the modern move order runs 4.Nf3 Nbd7 5.Bc4 Be7 6.0-0 0-0, after which White's tries include 7.a4, 7.Qe2 and 7.Re1; Bauer's analysis, as Silman summarises it, finds Black holding in each.[^s4] Black's queenside plan is ...a6 and ...b6, putting a bishop on b7 against e4, or expansion with ...a6, ...b5 and ...c5; capturing on d4 at the right moment activates the d7-knight via c5.[^s2] White's sharpest anti-Hanham weapons are an early g4 thrust (associated with Shirov) and piece sacrifices on f7.[^s4][^s6]

A characteristic queenless structure arises after 4.dxe5 dxe5 5.Qxd8+ Kxd8 6.Bc4 Be6 7.Bxe6 fxe6. Black has doubled, isolated e-pawns, but they control central squares, and the open d-file gives Black's rooks activity; Trigo Urquijo calls this structure the most characteristic of the defence and considers it good for Black.[^s2][^s1] White's typical answer is to manoeuvre knights towards d3 and c4 to pressure e5.[^s2]

## Pitfalls

For Black, the key error is to trust the classic move order blindly and walk into 3.d4 Nf6 4.dxe5 Nxe4 5.Qd5.[^s4] Another is passivity: the defence is compact, so Black must still generate counterplay through ...c5, ...b5 or a timely ...exd4 rather than wait.[^s2] For White, the 4.dxe5 queen trade is no route to an easy edge; Black can deliberately aim for the queenless middlegame and try to outplay the opponent there.[^s6] Database results on 365Chess still favour White overall.[^s7]

## Connections

The Philidor is one of the **open games**, alongside other replies to 2.Nf3 after 1.e4 e5.[^s7] It is **named after Philidor**, whose pawn-centred philosophy the opening embodies, which links it to **pawn structure** as a theme.[^s1] It **leads to** the **Hanham Variation**, the ...Nbd7 set-up that dominates modern practice, and its usual modern move order **transposes** from a **Pirc Defence** start.[^s4][^s1] **Alexander Alekhine** championed the Hanham approach with ...Nd7, ...c6 and ...Qc7 and scored heavily with it, while **Aron Nimzowitsch** also played it often, with a balanced record according to Platz's database count.[^s5] The most famous Philidor game is Morphy's **Opera Game**, Paris 1858, which databases classify under C41 and which Morphy won.[^s8]

# Albin Countergambit

The Albin Countergambit is Black's most aggressive answer to the Queen's Gambit: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e5. Instead of defending d5, Black strikes at White's centre with the e-pawn.[^s7] After the usual 3.dxe5 d4, Black has given up a pawn but planted a pawn on d4 that cramps White's development, and hopes for quick piece play and attacking chances.[^s7] Chessgames.com files the opening under ECO D08, and the main line with 4.Nf3 Nc6 5.g3 under D09.[^s8][^s9]

## History

Hooper and Whyld, as quoted by Tomasz Lissowski, credit the Italian master Mattia Cavallotti with introducing the move against Carlo Salvioli at Milan 1881, and describe it as a provocative reply to the Queen's Gambit.[^s3] The opening takes its name from Adolf Albin (born 1848 in Bucharest), who used it against Emanuel Lasker at the New York impromptu tournament of 1893.[^s3][^s6] Albin lost that game, and Lasker won the event with a perfect 13/13, a year before becoming world champion; Albin finished second.[^s6] The gambit has stayed rare in grandmaster practice, but Alexander Morozevich has used it successfully at elite level, and the publisher of Luc Henris's 600-page monograph credits him with a remarkable renaissance of the line.[^s7][^s1]

## Main lines and ideas

The standard continuation is 3.dxe5 d4 4.Nf3 Nc6, after which Black plans to recover the e5-pawn or use the time to develop and attack.[^s7] Henris organises the theory around White's fifth move: 5.a3, the fianchetto 5.g3, and 5.Nbd2, with Black choosing between ...Nge7, ...Be6 and ...Bg4 set-ups in each.[^s2] In the g3 lines Black often develops with ...Be6 and ...Qd7, preparing long castling.[^s2] Henris's annotations repeatedly show Black aiming queen moves such as ...Qh3 or ...Qg5 at the white king and breaking with ...c5, and he warns Black not to fall behind in development.[^s2]

IM John Watson, reviewing Albin material in 2009, singled out 4.Nf3 Nc6 5.a3 as the critical line.[^s5] He suspected that White should keep a small edge somehow but admitted he had not found a convincing way to show it, even in top-level games, and called the gambit underrated.[^s5]

## The Lasker Trap and other pitfalls

The best-known trap punishes the natural 4.e3?: 4...Bb4+ 5.Bd2 dxe3! 6.Bxb4 exf2+ 7.Ke2 fxg1=N+!. Noam Elkies points out that Black wins by underpromoting to a knight with check, gaining a third knight by move seven; a queen on g1 would simply be taken by the rook.[^s4] The lesson for White is that the d4-pawn must be blocked or won by piece play, not attacked with e2-e3 while the king is still in the centre.

For Black, the danger is the opposite: White can often return the extra pawn at a well-chosen moment and keep a sound positional edge against Black's loosened centre.[^s7] Database results reflect this. Chessgames.com's D08 games score about 47 per cent wins for White to 33 per cent for Black, and its D09 games about 51 to 30 per cent.[^s8][^s9] Mione nevertheless recommends the gambit to developing players as a way to sharpen their tactical skills.[^s7]

## Connections

The Albin is a branch of the **Queen's Gambit** and a typical **gambit**, offering a pawn for time and space.[^s7] It is **named after** **Adolf Albin**, whose game against **Emanuel Lasker** in New York made it famous, and it was **championed** in modern elite chess by **Alexander Morozevich**.[^s6][^s1] As a reply to 2.c4 it is an **alternative to** the solid **Queen's Gambit Declined** and to the other sharp try, the **Chigorin Defence**, which IM Watson reviewed alongside it.[^s5] Its Lasker Trap is the classic example of an **underpromotion** in the opening.[^s4]

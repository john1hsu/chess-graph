# Italian Game

The Italian Game begins 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. It is one of the open games: White develops a kingside piece, prepares to castle, and points the bishop at f7, a square near Black's king that can quickly become a target for an attack.[^s1] The opening was a favourite of the nineteenth century, and the quiet 3...Bc5 lines are roughly four centuries old; it is classified as C50 in the ECO system.[^s1][^s6]

## Main branches

Black's two main replies give the opening its structure. After 3...Bc5 the game is a **Giuoco Piano** ("quiet game"), and after 3...Nf6 it is the **Two Knights Defence**.[^s1] Less common third moves such as the Hungarian Defence 3...Be7 and the Blackburne Shilling Gambit 3...Nd4 are also catalogued under C50.[^s6]

Within the Giuoco Piano, White chooses between two approaches. The classical one plays 4.c3 and then 5.d4 to build a broad pawn centre, as in 4...Nf6 5.d4 exd4 6.cxd4, a line classified as C54.[^s2][^s7] The other plays d2-d3, which seventeenth-century Italian masters called the **Giuoco Pianissimo**, "the quietest game".[^s2] Kalinin notes that the sharp Giuoco Piano lines, including the Greco Attack, were re-assessed several times before settling at roughly equal early in the twentieth century, while the Pianissimo has always been considered balanced.[^s2]

The **Evans Gambit**, 3...Bc5 4.b4, offers a wing pawn to gain time for c3 and d4; a typical continuation is 4...Bxb4 5.c3 Ba5.[^s1][^s3] Captain William Davies Evans, a Welsh packet-boat commander, devised it around 1824 and introduced it in London about two years later by beating Alexander McDonnell.[^s4] It attracted enormous analytical attention in the nineteenth century, and C.J.S. Purdy still regarded it as one of the few genuine gambits never refuted.[^s3]

Against the Two Knights, the most direct try is 4.Ng5, a fast attack on f7 that the Italian master Giulio Cesare Polerio was already analysing in the sixteenth century.[^s5]

## The modern Italian

For more than a century the Ruy Lopez was the most respected of the open games, but in recent years the Italian has overtaken it, and players from world championship level down to amateur events now usually put the bishop on c4.[^s2] ChessBase's opening guide names Magnus Carlsen, Fabiano Caruana and Wesley So among the elite players of the 4.c3 setup.[^s1] Kalinin's explanation is practical: computer preparation has made the sharpest lines hard to use at the top, so grandmasters moved to slower positions where both sides can still improvise.[^s2]

The modern treatment borrows heavily from the Ruy Lopez. White often plays c3 and d3 instead of an early d4, and reroutes the queen's knight on the "Spanish" path b1-d2-f1-g3; Kalinin traces this approach to Wilhelm Steinitz's strategic play with d2-d3.[^s2] A typical Pianissimo development scheme for both sides is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.d3 Nf6 5.Nc3 d6 6.Be3 Bb6 7.Qd2 Be6 8.Bb3 Qd7, in which the bishops face each other and both sides keep flexible castling options.[^s2]

## Plans and pitfalls

Typical plans in the slow Italian include a later d3-d4 to gain central space, a queenside initiative with a2-a4, kingside attacks after both sides castle short, and Black's freeing advance ...d6-d5.[^s2] Because so many move orders transpose, Kalinin organises the theory by key positions rather than by move sequences, and argues that a club player gains more from knowing the standard plans than from memorising variations.[^s2] The bishop on c4 is itself a target: "hunting the Italian bishop" is a recurring theme for Black, and White must judge when to retreat it or allow it to be exchanged.[^s2] White should not expect a forced advantage: even the forcing 4.c3 and 5.d4 lines came to be judged roughly equal, and in the Chessgames.com database the C54 line has scored almost evenly for both colours.[^s2][^s7]

## Connections

The Italian Game is one of the **open games** and the main **alternative to the Ruy Lopez** after 2...Nc6; the two openings have converged, since the modern Italian uses many Spanish manoeuvres.[^s2] It **leads to** the **Giuoco Pianissimo**, the **Evans Gambit** and the **Two Knights Defence**.[^s1][^s5] Its slow d3 treatment was **influenced by Wilhelm Steinitz**, whose positional ideas about restraint and **pawn structure** shaped how the line is played today.[^s2]

# Najdorf Variation

The Najdorf Variation is the position reached after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 a6, catalogued under ECO code B90.[^s6] It carries the name of the Polish-Argentine grandmaster Miguel Najdorf, and recent monographs describe it as one of the most popular openings at every level, prized as an uncompromising but theoretically sound weapon.[^s3] Everyman's 2018 repertoire book calls it highly aggressive yet objectively sound, and notes that it belongs to the repertoires of many of today's elite players.[^s4]

## Origins and exponents

André Schulz, writing for ChessBase on Najdorf's 110th birthday, says the idea of 5...a6 probably goes back to Karel Opočenský, but that Najdorf popularised it and scored well with it; according to the ChessBase Mega Database, Najdorf first played it at the 1939 Buenos Aires Olympiad against Christian Poulsen.[^s1] Milos Pavlovic's survey of the line lists a long roll of contributors on Black's side, including Kotov, Bronstein, Polugaevsky, Fischer, Tal, Gligorić, Portisch, Kasparov, Nunn, Ljubojević and Timman, and among the younger generation names Vachier-Lagrave and Nepomniachtchi as its leading standard-bearers.[^s2] Thinkers Publishing's 6.Bg5 monograph singles out Fischer, Kasparov and Anand as its champions.[^s3] The game database for B90 alone holds tens of thousands of games, with White scoring only slightly better than Black.[^s6]

## Why 5...a6?

At first sight 5...a6 is a slow pawn move in a sharp position. Its value is flexibility: it takes the b5-square away from White's knights and bishop, prepares ...b5 and ...Bb7, and postpones the decision about the centre. Pavlovic stresses that Black can still choose among ...e6 set-ups, ...e5 and ...g6, and he prefers ...e5 wherever it fits, observing that the Najdorf is one of the few Sicilians in which Black regularly puts a pawn on e5.[^s2] That ...e5 structure gains space and kicks the d4-knight, but it concedes a hole on d5, so much of Black's play revolves around controlling that square with pieces (...Be6, ...Nbd7, ...Rc8) and preventing a white knight from settling there as an outpost.[^s2]

## White's sixth moves

Because Black has committed so little, White has an unusually wide choice. The contents of one Najdorf repertoire book list 6.Bc4 (labelled the Fischer Attack), 6.Be3 with the English Attack, 6.f3, 6.g3, the classical 6.Be2, 6.f4 and the main line 6.Bg5.[^s5] Pavlovic adds 6.h3 and a whole chapter of sidelines such as 6.Rg1, 6.Bd3, 6.Qd3, 6.Qe2, 6.Qf3, 6.a3, 6.a4 and 6.h4.[^s2] Against 6.Be2 and 6.Be3 the reply ...e5 is common, while 6.Bg5 is usually met by 6...e6, after which 7.f4 leads to the sharpest positions in the whole opening.[^s5][^s3]

## Typical plans and pitfalls

After 6.Bg5 e6 7.f4, Black needs accurate move-order knowledge. Jarmula's monograph treats 7...Nc6? as refuted: 8.Nxc6 bxc6 9.e5 is the main continuation.[^s3] A sounder choice is 7...Qc7, which restrains e4-e5 and prepares ...b5 and ...Bb7; the danger is that White can answer with Bxf6, ruining Black's kingside pawns and making short castling awkward, in exchange for the bishop pair.[^s5] The best-known sharp branch is the Poisoned Pawn; Pavlovic recalls that Fischer lost a game in it to Spassky in their 1972 Reykjavik match, yet the Najdorf kept its popularity.[^s2]

Sidelines also carry traps for both sides. Pavlovic judges 6.Qf3, met by 6...Qb6, to block White's f-pawn and stop ...e5 at the cost of leaving f2 behind, and he regards 6.Rg1 e5 7.Nb3 Be6 8.g4 d5 as fully adequate for Black.[^s2] The general lesson for club players is that the Najdorf rewards understanding of typical structures more than memorised moves: Black must know which pawn set-up suits each white system.[^s4][^s2]

A short example of White's aggressive queen sortie, from Pavlovic's chapter on sidelines:

```pgn
[Event "Sideline example (Pavlovic)"]
[Result "*"]

1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6 6. Qf3 Qb6 7. Nb3 e6 8. g4 Nc6 9. g5 Nd7 10. Be3 Qc7 11. O-O-O b5 *
```

Here White has castled long and pushed the g-pawn, while Black has retreated the knight to d7 and started the queenside counter-attack with ...b5, a typical opposite-wings race.[^s2]

## Connections

The Najdorf is a variation of the **Sicilian Defence**, related to other Open Sicilian systems such as the **Dragon Variation**; Pavlovic notes that Black can even combine ...a6 with a ...g6 fianchetto, the so-called Dragadorf.[^s6][^s2] It is **named after** **Miguel Najdorf** and was **championed by** **Bobby Fischer** and **Garry Kasparov**.[^s1][^s4] It **leads to** the **Poisoned Pawn Variation**, whose best-known appearance was in **Fischer–Spassky 1972**, and to the **English Attack** with 6.Be3.[^s2][^s5] The weak d5-square in its ...e5 lines is a classic **outpost** for White's pieces.[^s2]

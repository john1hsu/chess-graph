# Berlin Defence

The Berlin Defence is Black's answer 3...Nf6 to the Ruy Lopez, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 Nf6. Instead of putting the question to the bishop with 3...a6, Black develops the king's knight and immediately attacks the e4-pawn.[^s2] Chessgames.com files the position under ECO C65, with games going back to 1840, while the main line with 4.O-O Nxe4 is classified as C67.[^s7][^s6]

## History

The defence is old: Edward Winter's notes reproduce analysis of it from Bilguer's *Handbuch des Schachspiels* (Berlin, 1843) and from Jaenisch's work of the same period.[^s4] By 1874 W. N. Potter could describe it as obsolete and rarely met in practice.[^s4] The nickname "Berlin Wall" appeared in the *British Chess Magazine* in 1979, apparently a pun on the line's solidity, and later came to mean one particular queenless position (see below).[^s4] For most of the twentieth century the Berlin was seen as a second-tier reply to the Ruy Lopez.[^s2] That changed in the 2000 world championship match, where Vladimir Kramnik used it to neutralise Garry Kasparov's preparation on his way to winning the title; since then it has become Black's most popular answer to 3.Bb5 at the highest level, and nearly all elite players carry it in their repertoires.[^s1][^s2]

## The Berlin endgame

The critical main line runs 4.O-O Nxe4 5.d4 Nd6 6.Bxc6 dxc6 7.dxe5 Nf5 8.Qxd8+ Kxd8.[^s2][^s3] Black has given up castling and has doubled c-pawns, but in return owns the bishop pair and a sound, compact position. Roiz argues that the result is better described as a queenless middlegame than an endgame, because so many pieces remain and Black's king is slightly exposed in the centre.[^s2] White's main asset is the kingside pawn majority supported by the pawn on e5; Black's queenside majority is crippled by the doubled pawns and cannot easily produce a passed pawn.[^s3] Most top-level games continue with 9.Nc3, though 9.Rd1+ (sending the king to e8) and 9.h3 are also major tries.[^s2]

## Typical plans

For White the classic plan is to advance the kingside pawns with f4–f5, gaining space and trying to create a passed pawn, while keeping the e5-pawn secure.[^s3] Cox notes that Black's knight often reaches g6 to attack e5, which makes it awkward for White to move the f3-knight and prepare f4.[^s3] Black's model set-up is ...Ne7–g6, ...h6, ...c5 and ...Be6, then using the bishops and gradually activating the king and rooks, which the king on d8 or e8 initially blocks.[^s3] A sample line from Shirov–Almasi, Tilburg 1996, is 9.Nc3 Ne7 10.Nd4 Ng6 11.f4, with White preparing f4–f5 to drive the knight away.[^s3]

## Pitfalls and reputation

Roiz warns that in practice Black's doubled queenside pawns are often a decisive factor, while White players tend to overestimate their kingside majority; neither side's long-term trumps are automatic.[^s2] Cox also stresses that a centralised king does not by itself save Black: some pure pawn endings arising from the structure are lost.[^s3] The line's main drawback for an ambitious Black player is its drawing reputation. Donaldson's review notes that Black can comfortably steer some lines towards a draw, and the database figures for the open variation show draws as by far the most common result.[^s5][^s6] White players who want to avoid the endgame commonly choose 5.Re1 or 5.Qe2 after 4...Nxe4, or the quieter 4.d3, which Roiz meets with 4...Bc5.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

The Berlin Defence sits under the **Ruy Lopez** as one of its main Black systems, an **alternative** to the 3...a6 lines that lead to the **Marshall Attack** and the **Open Ruy Lopez**.[^s2] It was **championed** by **Vladimir Kramnik** and made famous when it was **played in** the **2000 World Championship** match against Garry Kasparov.[^s1] After 4.Nc3 it **transposes** to the **Four Knights Game**.[^s2] Its main line is a study in **pawn structure** and the **bishop pair** in a queenless **endgame**, the themes to which Cox devotes over a third of his monograph.[^s3][^s5]

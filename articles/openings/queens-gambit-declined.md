# Queen's Gambit Declined

The Queen's Gambit Declined (QGD) is the reply 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6, in which Black answers White's attack on d5 by backing the pawn with the e-pawn rather than taking on c4 or supporting it with ...c6.[^s2] It has a reputation as one of the soundest defences to 1.d4: grandmaster Michael Prusikin builds a complete club-player repertoire around 2...e6, and grandmaster Milos Pavlovic notes that it has long been among the most-used openings in World Championship matches, played regularly by champions from Lasker and Capablanca to Karpov, Kramnik and Anand.[^s2][^s3] Prusikin notes that Capablanca and Alekhine debated the Queen's Gambit 31 times in their 34-game 1927 title match.[^s2]

## Main ideas

The price of 2...e6 is the queen's bishop on c8, which is hemmed in behind its own pawns; much of Black's strategy is about either freeing it or making its passivity irrelevant.[^s2] Black's standard ways to loosen the position are the break ...c5, often prepared by ...dxc4, and queenside expansion with ...b6 or ...b5.[^s3] Pavlovic shows ...dxc4 as Black's reaction to several of White's seventh-move tries in the 5.Bg5 h6 6.Bxf6 lines, typically followed by ...c5 or ...b6 and ...Bb7.[^s3] Prusikin stresses that, in many lines, getting the light-squared bishop out early (often to f5) is a recurring priority for Black.[^s2]

White usually develops with Nc3, Bg5 or Bf4, e3 and Nf3, and Schandorff's advice for White is to strive for the initiative and show that the extra tempo matters.[^s1][^s3] Positions tend to be decided by pawn structure rather than early tactics, and some main lines are notoriously solid: Chessgames.com's page on the Tartakower main line describes it as drawish, and its game sample shows a clear majority of draws.[^s6][^s2]

## Main branches

- **Exchange Variation, 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.cxd5 exd5.** Lars Schandorff recommends it for White as the most principled try; after 5.Bg5 c6 the pawns form the Carlsbad structure.[^s1] White can attack on the kingside or run the b2–b4–b5 **minority attack** on the queenside, while Black's typical regrouping is ...Be7, ...0-0, ...Nbd7, ...Re8 and ...Nf8.[^s1] Schandorff singles out Botvinnik's plan of Nge2, castling and f3 followed by e3–e4, used against Keres in Moscow 1952 and later by Kasparov.[^s1] Prusikin argues that Black has real kingside counterplay in this structure, including pawn storms with ...g5–g4.[^s2]
- **Orthodox Defence, 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e3 0-0 6.Nf3 Nbd7.** The classical set-up, filed under ECO D60.[^s5]
- **Lasker and Tartakower systems.** After 4.Nf3 Be7 5.Bg5 0-0 6.e3 h6 7.Bh4, Black can play 7...Ne4 (Lasker), offering exchanges, or 7...b6 (Tartakower), preparing to fianchetto the problem bishop.[^s1] Schandorff adds that taking on f6 first with 7.Bxf6 gains White little.[^s1] Pavlovic credits Makagonov with enriching the Tartakower line.[^s3] Prusikin lists the isolated queen's pawn, **hanging pawns** and a Black queenside majority among the structures the Tartakower produces.[^s2]
- **5.Bf4 lines.** Schandorff calls 5.Bf4 a popular modern choice and treats 6...c5 and 6...Nbd7 as viable replies after 5...0-0 6.e3.[^s1] Prusikin also covers the early 4.Bf4 c5 (the Harrwitz Attack).[^s2]
- **Tarrasch Defence, 3.Nc3 c5.** Black strikes at the centre at once, accepting an isolated d-pawn or hanging pawns for piece activity; IM Cyrus Lakdawala describes it as trading structural soundness for initiative.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

For Black the classic error is neglecting the c8-bishop: Prusikin makes its early activation a recurring priority, and without ...c5 or ...dxc4 to open lines Black can be left cramped.[^s2][^s3] For White, premature central advances are risky: Pavlovic calls 7.e4 in the 5.Bg5 h6 6.Bxf6 Bxf6 line a long-known source of problems, since after 7...dxe4 8.Nxe4 Nc6! White has no time to castle queenside and the d4-pawn comes under pressure.[^s3] In the Exchange Variation, Schandorff notes that Botvinnik's e3–e4 break must be prepared, not rushed.[^s1]

## Example

A typical Tartakower position arises after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e3 0-0 6.Nf3 h6 7.Bh4 b6 8.cxd5 Nxd5: the central tension is released, Black's queen's bishop is ready for b7, and the hanging-pawn and isolated-pawn structures Prusikin associates with the Tartakower are close at hand.[^s6][^s2]

## Connections

The QGD is the 2...e6 branch of the **Queen's Gambit** and an **alternative to** the **Slav Defence** (2...c6) and the **Queen's Gambit Accepted** (2...dxc4).[^s2] Its Exchange Variation **leads to** the **Carlsbad structure** and the **minority attack**, with **Mikhail Botvinnik** the model player for White's central plan.[^s1] The Tartakower and Tarrasch lines often produce **hanging pawns** and an **isolated queen's pawn**.[^s2][^s4] Pavlovic pairs the QGD with the **Nimzo-Indian Defence** as a repertoire, since both can arise after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6.[^s3]

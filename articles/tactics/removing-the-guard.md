# Removing the guard

Removing the guard (also called removing or removal of the defender) is the umbrella name for tactics that win something defended by first getting rid of what defends it. The target may be a piece, a pawn or a square on which mate or promotion would happen. Once its protector is gone, the target is loose and can be taken or used.[^s1][^s2] The English Chess Federation's Chess Skills leaflet presents it as a basic way of winning material and names three methods: capture the guard, deflect it with a check or threat, or overload it.[^s1]

## The four methods

Ward Farnsworth's tactics course *Predator at the Chessboard* gives the theme a whole chapter and sorts it into four techniques, all of which turn a protected target into an unprotected one:[^s2]

1. **Capture the guard.** Exchange or sacrifice to eliminate the defender outright.[^s2]
2. **Attack the guard** so that it has to move away, often with a pawn.[^s2][^s4]
3. **Distract the guard** by taking something else it protects, so that it is overloaded.[^s2]
4. **Block the guard** by putting a piece between it and the target (interference).[^s2]

Other teachers group these differently. ChessKid's lesson counts three ways (capturing, decoying and deflecting) and stresses looking at checks, captures and threats first.[^s5] Whatever the labels, the question is always the same: which enemy piece is doing the defending, and can it be removed before the target is collected?[^s4]

## Capturing the guard

The simplest version is a capture whose recapture leaves the real target hanging. Farnsworth's opening example has a rook capture a bishop that is the only guard of a knight; after the rook is recaptured, the queen takes the undefended knight, so two minor pieces are won for a rook.[^s2] The ECF leaflet shows the same idea aimed at a queen: a rook capture with check removes the knight that was guarding it, and when Black recaptures the queen falls.[^s1] The trick is most powerful when the first capture comes with check, because the opponent has no time to rescue the second target.[^s1][^s3]

A target defended twice is not automatically safe. Farnsworth advises tracing every chain of protection: removing one guard by force, preferably with check, can leave the target short of defenders, and the liquidation itself often opens up a fork, pin or discovered attack.[^s3]

## Example

The diagram position is a constructed illustration of Farnsworth's first pattern, not a game.[^s2] White to move. Black's bishop on e6 is protected only by the rook on e8, and it is the only piece guarding the knight on g4, which White's queen attacks. 1.Rxe6 removes the guard. If 1...Rxe6, then 2.Qxg4, and White has bishop and knight for a rook. Grabbing a pawn with 1...Nxf2 does not help, because 2.Rxe8+ comes with check and the knight is still lost afterwards. Starting with 1.Qxg4? Bxg4 merely gives up the queen for a knight, which is why the guard has to go first.

## Mating squares and promotion

The guard need not protect material. In one ECF exercise Black sacrifices a rook on f1 to remove the bishop that covered the decisive square, then mates with ...Qxg2+ and ...Nd3.[^s1] In another, a rook check on g2 forces the black rook to capture, and with that guard gone the h-pawn promotes.[^s1] Endgame kings are frequent victims as well: a king that must both stop a passed pawn and protect a piece cannot do both.[^s1]

## Pitfalls and practical advice

- **Count the recaptures and the move order.** One ECF exercise works only if the rook exchange comes before the bishop capture; reversing the order lets the defender escape with a check.[^s1]
- **Watch for counter-threats.** Farnsworth notes that the side under attack should look for a stronger reply elsewhere before recapturing automatically.[^s2]
- **Prefer pawn guards.** A piece protected by a pawn is hard to undermine, while a piece protected only by another piece can be exposed by an exchange, an overload, an interposition or a pawn attack on the guard. Advanced knights in particular are safer with pawn support.[^s2][^s4]
- **Sacrifice only for a reason.** The capture or lure should lead to concrete gain, not just a trade.[^s5]

## Connections

Removing the guard sits under **chess tactics** and has **overloading**, **deflection** and **interference** as parts: overloading and distraction are Farnsworth's third method, and blocking the guard is his fourth.[^s2][^s4] ChessKid treats the **decoy** as another way of luring the defender away.[^s5] The tactic often appears inside a **combination**, is aimed at the back rank in many **back-rank mate** patterns, and leaves positions ripe for a **fork**.[^s1][^s3] Its usual result is a **material advantage**.[^s1][^s2]

# Greek gift sacrifice

The Greek gift is the sacrifice of a bishop on h7 with check against a king that has castled short, the start of a mating pattern that normally needs three attackers: the bishop, a knight and the queen.[^s1] In its ideal form the knight checks from g5 after 1.Bxh7+ Kxh7 2.Ng5+, the king retreats to g8, and 3.Qh5 threatens mate on h7, a mate the queen gives with the knight's support.[^s1] The same idea with colours reversed is ...Bxh2+. It is also known as the classic (or classical) bishop sacrifice, the name under which Vukovic's *The Art of Attack in Chess* lists it among its topics.[^s4][^s5]

## The name and early history

The usual explanation is a double reference: Gioachino Greco, the leading player of the early seventeenth century, analysed the sacrifice, and the "gift" recalls the Trojan horse and Virgil's warning about Greeks bearing gifts.[^s7] Edward Winter's notes on Greco point out that Greco was already using the Bxh7+ and Ng5+ combination in the early 1600s, long before the nineteenth-century games often credited with it.[^s3] Winter also quotes Irving Chernev's view that Fritz's win against Mason at Nuremberg 1883 is usually given as the first tournament use of the bishop check on h7 in the French Defence, while Chernev himself pointed to an earlier Paulsen v Schwarz match game of 1879.[^s3] Chernev praised Morphy (against Guibert, Paris 1858) for avoiding castling in a position where the trap 8.Bxh7+ Kxh7 9.Ng5+ was waiting.[^s3]

## When it works

Silman lists the minimum attacking group as a bishop on the b1–h7 diagonal, a knight that can reach g5 and a queen that can check on h5 (or sometimes along the b1–h7 diagonal).[^s5] Helpers make the attack much stronger: a dark-squared bishop on the c1–h6 diagonal, which supports Ng5 and makes ...Kh6 suicidal; a rook that can swing to the kingside; a pawn on h4 with a rook behind it; and above all a pawn on e5, which takes f6 away from a defending knight and closes the king's escape squares.[^s5] The Exeter club's recipe puts the same points as checks a player can run quickly: no black knight on f6, a route for the queen to h5 or d3, a supporting piece such as Ng5 or a rook lift to h3, and no way for Black to cover h7 with ...Bf5 or ...Nf6.[^s7]

## Defences and pitfalls

Black has several resources. A queen on d8 or a bishop on e7 may simply capture the knight on g5; the king may decline the bishop with ...Kh8, leaving White only a pawn up; it may run along the back rank after Qh5; or it may step forward to g6 or h6.[^s1][^s5] Capturing on g5 is not always a cure: Farnsworth shows positions where ...Qxg5 is forced to stop mate, so White ends up with queen and pawn for bishop and knight.[^s1] When the king goes to g6, the attacker usually needs a queen check such as Qd3+ or Qg4 and pawn moves like g4 or h5 to build a net, and Farnsworth stresses that one inaccurate move lets the king escape.[^s2] Silman's survey of master games concludes that the sacrifice is most dangerous with both the e5 pawn and the c1 bishop in place, often unsound with neither, and that without an h4 pawn or the bishop on c1, ...Kh6 can hold, as Spassky showed against Teschner at Riga 1959.[^s6] The practical rule is to calculate the king walks to g6 and h6, not only the retreat to g8, before giving the bishop.[^s2][^s6]

## Example

The diagram position is from one of Greco's training games as republished by the Exeter club: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 Nf6 3.Bd3 Nc6 4.Nf3 Be7 5.h4 O-O 6.e5 Nd5.[^s7] Every helper is present: the e5 pawn has driven the knight from f6, the h4 pawn stands ready to open the h-file, and the d3 bishop is aimed at h7. After **7.Bxh7+ Kxh7 8.Ng5+ Kg8 9.Qh5**, taking the knight with 9...Bxg5 opens the h-file with 10.hxg5, and after 10...f5 11.g6 Black resigned, facing the queen and rook lined up on the open h-file.[^s7] In another version, 8...Bxg5 at once is met by 9.hxg5+ and a queen hunt that drives the king to e4, where 13.Qd3 is mate.[^s6]

A complete game shows the h4 version with the king sent forward. Silman gives this French Defence from Boston 1880:[^s6]

```pgn

1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Be7 5. Bxf6 Bxf6 6. Nf3 O-O 7. Bd3 c5
8. e5 Be7 9. h4 c4 10. Bxh7+ Kxh7 11. Ng5+ Kh6 12. Qd2 Bxg5 13. hxg5+ Kg6
14. Ne2 Qxg5 15. Nf4+ Kf5 16. Rh5 Qxh5 17. Nxh5 g5 18. c3 Kg6 19. Qc2+ Kxh5
20. Qh7+ Kg4 21. Qh3+ Kf4 22. Qf3# 1-0
```

The best-known modern example is Colle v O'Hanlon, Nice 1930, where Colle sacrificed on h7 after quiet d-pawn development, the king went to g6, and masters argued for decades over whether the attack was sound.[^s7]

## Connections

The Greek gift is a specific kind of **sacrifice** and one of the standard patterns of **chess tactics**; carried through to the end it is a forced **combination** leading to **checkmate**.[^s1][^s5] It is a direct test of **king safety** after short castling, and many classical examples arise from the **French Defence**, where the e5 pawn evicts Black's knight from f6.[^s3][^s6] The bishop works as a **decoy**, pulling the king onto h7 where the knight can check it.[^s1] The idea was **developed by** **Gioachino Greco** in the early seventeenth century, and Colle v O'Hanlon, Nice 1930 is a frequently cited **example**.[^s3][^s7]

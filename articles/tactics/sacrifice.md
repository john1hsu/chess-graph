# Sacrifice

A sacrifice is a move that deliberately gives up material, anything from a pawn to the queen, because the player expects to receive something worth more in return. The payment may be concrete (a forced mate, or regaining more material a few moves later) or lasting but harder to measure: faster development, open lines against the king, an outpost, or control of key squares.[^s1][^s4] Capablanca's view, recorded by Edward Winter, was that a strong player treats a sacrifice purely as a means to an end.[^s2] In the opening, sacrifices of a pawn or sometimes a minor piece for rapid mobilisation and attacking chances are called gambits.[^s3]

## Sham and real sacrifices

The classic framework is Rudolf Spielmann's *The Art of Sacrifice in Chess* (first published in the 1930s; Dover edition 2012). Spielmann split sacrifices into two families.[^s1] In a **sham** sacrifice the loss is only apparent, because a forced line wins the material back with interest or mates; he subdivided these into positional, gain and mating sacrifices.[^s1] Winter notes that Spielmann's contrast can also be rendered as "real" versus "temporary".[^s2] A **real** sacrifice has no forced pay-back: the material is gone and the player relies on judgement that the resulting position is worth it.[^s1] Spielmann's types of real sacrifice are those for development, obstructive, preventive (stopping the enemy king from castling), line-clearing, vacating, deflecting or decoy, sacrifices in the castled king's field, and the king-hunt; the second part of his book treats the sacrificial value of the exchange and the queen.[^s1]

The distinction matters in practice. A sham sacrifice can be checked by calculation to the end; a real one cannot, so it rests on evaluation and on the defender's practical difficulties. Winter quotes Heidenfeld's argument that an objectively unsound sacrifice can still be a reasonable practical decision if it misleads the opponent most of the time.[^s2]

## Sacrifice and combination

Many forcing combinations contain a sacrifice, but writers have long disagreed whether one is required. Winter's survey collects authors such as Pachman and Purdy who say combinations need not involve one, alongside others who see the sacrifice as the heart of a combination.[^s6] It is therefore more precise to treat the sacrifice as a technique a combination may use than as a synonym for it.[^s6]

## The positional exchange sacrifice

The best-known real sacrifice is giving up a rook for a minor piece, the exchange sacrifice. Vassilios Kotronias presents Tigran Petrosian as a leading, perhaps the foremost, exponent of its positional form, arguing that Petrosian refused to treat piece values as fixed numbers and saw that a rook can be worth less than a minor piece on a strong outpost or a firm grip on squares of one colour.[^s4] His book on the subject annotates 36 Petrosian games from 1947 to 1980, including losses where he judges the sacrifice unsound.[^s5]

**Example.** In Reshevsky–Petrosian, Zürich Candidates 1953 (a Nimzo-Indian), after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4 4.e3 O-O 5.Bd3 d5 6.Nf3 c5 7.O-O Nc6 8.a3 Bxc3 9.bxc3 b6 10.cxd5 exd5 11.Bb2 c4 12.Bc2 Bg4 13.Qe1 Ne4 14.Nd2 Nxd2 15.Qxd2 Bh5 16.f3 Bg6 17.e4 Qd7 18.Rae1 dxe4 19.fxe4 Rfe8 20.Qf4 b5 21.Bd1 Re7 22.Bg4 Qe8 23.e5 a5 24.Re3 Rd8 25.Rfe1, Petrosian played **25...Re6!!**, offering the rook to the bishop on g4 (diagram).[^s4] After 26.a4 Ne7 27.Bxe6 fxe6 his knight reached d5 and his light-squared bishop had no opposite number; Kotronias shows that Black was never in danger, and the game was drawn on move 41.[^s4]

## Pitfalls

- **Counting only material.** A sacrifice is judged by what replaces the material; in Kotronias's examples the compensation is something specific, such as an outpost, a blockade or control of one colour complex.[^s4]
- **Confusing hope with calculation.** Sham sacrifices should be calculated to the point where the material returns; real ones need an honest assessment of the long-term position.[^s1][^s2]
- **Trusting folklore.** Winter could find no reliable primary source for Steinitz's dictum that a gambit is best refuted by accepting it, and he treats a famous Tal quip about unsound sacrifices as unverified; neither is a substitute for analysing the position.[^s2]

## Connections

The sacrifice is part of **chess tactics** and the main lever of many a **combination**, though not a necessary one.[^s6] It is the deliberate inversion of a **material advantage**, and it **depends on** **calculation** for sham sacrifices.[^s1] **Gambits** are its opening form, prominent in the attacking style of the **Romantic era**.[^s3] The **exchange sacrifice** is a distinct sub-type, **championed by** **Tigran Petrosian** and **exemplified by** Reshevsky–Petrosian, Zürich 1953.[^s4][^s5] **Rudolf Spielmann** supplied the standard classification.[^s1]

# Domination

**Domination** is the tactical theme in which a piece is caught not because it is boxed in by its own pawns or the edge of the board, but because every square it could flee to is covered by the enemy.[^s2] Noam Elkies describes it as the entrapment of a piece that looks invulnerable, and notes that it demands economy: the attacker must control all of the victim's available squares with the force at hand.[^s2] According to Genrikh Kasparyan, the term was introduced by the French composer Henri Rinck.[^s1]

## Narrow and broad senses

In the narrow, classical sense, domination means that one side controls a set of squares so precisely that an enemy piece can be attacked and captured, winning material.[^s1] Kasparyan used the word more broadly, also counting pieces trapped by geometric patterns, pins or zugzwang, and Siegfried Hornecker points out that some of the studies in Kasparyan's collection are far from a classical domination.[^s1] Elkies gives a study by Kasparyan in which the queen is lost through zugzwang rather than by direct attack, an example of the wider sense.[^s2]

The theme is close to the **trapped piece**, but the emphasis differs: a trapped piece is usually hemmed in by its own position, whereas a dominated piece often stands in the open and is defeated purely by the coordination of the opponent's pieces.[^s2][^s3] The most striking cases are those in which weaker pieces capture a stronger one, such as minor pieces rounding up a rook or a queen.[^s3]

## In endgame studies

Domination is one of the great subjects of the **endgame study**. Kasparyan, whom his publisher calls the greatest study composer and analyst, devoted a whole book to studies in which White wins by trapping or dominating the opponent's pieces rather than by promotion or mate.[^s3] It first appeared in Russian in 1974, and an English translation was published in Moscow in 1980.[^s1] The synopsis credits Kling, Horwitz, Berger, Troitzky, Rinck, the Platov brothers and Kubbel among the composers who developed the theme.[^s3] Elkies shows a 1958–59 prize-winner by Dmitri Petrov in which a king and two bishops trap a rook on an open board, and a 1927 Troitzky study in which a knight hunts down a queen.[^s2]

## In practical play

Over the board, domination shows up most often in minor-piece endings. Karsten Müller calls dominating the enemy and zugzwang the attacker's sharpest endgame weapons, and observes that in bishop-versus-knight endings the stronger side is often the one better able to dominate the opposing minor piece.[^s5] Boroljub Zlatanovic's annotations describe the same idea as paralysing the knight: the bishop takes away its squares, often with quiet waiting moves, until the knight can no longer take part.[^s4] Queens can be dominated in the middlegame too: Hornecker cites the eighth game of the 2012 World Championship, in which Viswanathan Anand trapped Boris Gelfand's queen on h1 and won in 17 moves.[^s1]

The defender's lesson is to watch a piece's escape squares, not only the attacks on it. Elkies gives a game of Bobby Fischer's, against Schweber, in which a single careless king move allowed Fischer's queen to be trapped, and Zlatanovic's examples show how a knight short of squares can be immobilised long before it is attacked.[^s2][^s4]

## Example

In Petrov's study from *Leninska Smena* 1966 (White to move and win; the key position above), White plays 1.e7 Kxe7 2.Nf5+ Kf6 3.Nxh6 Kg7 4.Ng4 Rd3+ 5.Kc4 Rg3 6.Rg8+ Kxg8 7.Nh6+ Kh8 8.Bc5 Rg7 9.Bd6 Rg6 10.Be5+ Rg7 11.Nf5.[^s1] In the final position Black's rook on g7 is pinned by the bishop and attacked by the knight, the king cannot defend it from h8, and the rook falls: bishop and knight have dominated a rook in the corner.[^s1]

## Connections

Domination is a branch of **chess tactics** whose usual pay-off is a **material advantage**; it shades into the **trapped piece** on one side and, in Kasparyan's broad sense, into **zugzwang** and the **pin** on the other.[^s1][^s2] It is the knight's and bishop's chief weapon in **knight versus bishop** endings, and its home in composition is the **endgame study**.[^s3][^s5] The Anand–Gelfand game of 2012 is a modern practical example.[^s1]

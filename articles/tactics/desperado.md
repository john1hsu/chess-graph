# Desperado

A **desperado** is a piece that is doomed anyway and so uses its last move to do as much damage as it can: it captures something, gives check or creates a threat before it is taken.[^s5][^s6] The point is arithmetic rather than heroism. If a piece will be lost whatever you do, the only question left is what you get for it, and the desperado makes sure the answer is not "nothing".[^s6]

## Origin of the term

The word is credited to Emanuel Lasker. Mark Dvoretsky made that attribution in his foreword to the 2008 algebraic edition of *Lasker's Manual of Chess*, and the chess historian Edward Winter found no use of the term earlier than Lasker's *Lehrbuch des Schachspiels* (Berlin, 1926), pages 106–107.[^s1] Winter's list of the earliest known occurrences of chess terms gives the same 1926 Lasker reference for "desperado".[^s2] The Russell Enterprises edition of the *Manual* that carries Dvoretsky's foreword rewrote Lasker's text in figurine algebraic notation and added computer-checked corrections.[^s3]

## How it works

The motif appears whenever a piece cannot be saved: it is attacked and has no safe square, it is trapped, or both sides have pieces hanging at the same time.[^s6] In each case an ordinary retreat or defence just loses the piece. The desperado instead looks for the most useful thing the piece can still do on its way out, ideally a capture (which banks material) or a check (which keeps the initiative and denies the opponent a free move).[^s5][^s6]

Desperados often come in pairs or chains. When both players have pieces en prise, each side may capture with its threatened piece before recapturing, and the sequence of mutual desperados decides who ends up ahead.[^s6] Training material treats the desperado as a distinct theme alongside deflection, discovered attack and the zwischenzug; Cyrus Lakdawala's *Tactical Training*, for instance, gives it its own chapter.[^s4]

## Example

In the diagram position (White to move, FEN `r5k1/B1p2ppp/1p6/8/8/8/5PPP/3R2K1 w - - 0 1`), the white bishop on a7 is attacked by the rook on a8. Its only two squares are b8, which the rook also covers, and b6, which holds a black pawn. If White does nothing about it, ...Rxa7 wins a clean piece. The desperado is 1.Bxb6: the bishop is still lost after 1...cxb6, but it has taken a pawn with it, so White ends with rook and three pawns against rook and four instead of against rook and five. This is a constructed teaching position, not taken from a game; the point is only to show the "something rather than nothing" logic the sources describe.[^s5][^s6]

## Pitfalls and practical advice

The key question is whether the piece really is lost; a player who throws away a piece that had a quiet escape square has just blundered.[^s6] It is equally important to compare the options. A desperado capture that also gives check or attacks another target is often worth more than the first capture you see, so list every capture and check the doomed piece has before choosing.[^s6]

The defender has to watch for it too. When you attack a piece that cannot escape, check what it can take before it goes; a hasty move that leaves a second piece loose gives the desperado a target. Chess.com's teaching page stresses the same thing from the attacker's side: a doomed piece should make one last advantageous move.[^s5]

## Connections

The desperado is a branch of **chess tactics** and a close relative of the **zwischenzug**: both insert a move the opponent did not expect before the obvious exchange is completed, and teaching collections place them side by side.[^s4][^s6] It is a special kind of **sacrifice** in which the material was going to be lost anyway, and it often appears inside larger **combinations** where pieces are left hanging on both sides.[^s6] It is closely tied to the idea of a trapped piece, the most common reason a piece becomes doomed, and it rewards careful **calculation** of the final material count.[^s6] Its name comes from **Emanuel Lasker**.[^s1][^s2]

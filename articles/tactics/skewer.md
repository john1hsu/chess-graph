# Skewer

A skewer is an attack on two enemy pieces standing on the same line, in which the piece nearer the attacker is the more valuable one. It has to get out of the way, and the piece behind it is then captured.[^s1][^s6] In practice the skewering piece is a queen, rook or bishop, since the attack has to run along a rank, file or diagonal through the front piece to the one behind.[^s1][^s5]

## How it works

The skewer depends on the order of the targets. If the more valuable piece stands in front, the defender cannot simply leave it attacked, so it moves and exposes the piece behind.[^s6] Farnsworth makes the point with a king and knight on one diagonal: with the king in front, a check forces the king off the line and the loose knight behind it falls.[^s3] The strongest form is therefore a skewer *through the king*, because a check leaves the defender no choice about moving the front piece. The ECF's skewer exercises mostly begin with a check for exactly this reason.[^s1]

A skewer also works against a queen or a rook in front, as long as the piece behind is worth taking. ChessBase describes the classic case of a bishop hitting a queen with a rook behind it, and warns that a queen skewer along a diagonal wins only if the piece behind is unprotected.[^s5] Two rooks on the same diagonal are a natural target for a bishop.[^s4]

## Common patterns

- **Back-rank skewer.** A rook or queen checks along the back rank and wins a rook standing behind the king. ChessBase notes that rooks left in the corners are often unprotected, which makes this pattern frequent.[^s5]
- **Bishop against queen and rook.** A queen and a rook sharing a diagonal of the colour of the opponent's bishop are a standing invitation to a skewer.[^s5]
- **Endgame skewers.** With few pieces left, kings and rooks often stand on open lines, and a rook check that wins the enemy rook behind its king can decide the game; Chess.com gives a rook ending of this kind as an example.[^s6]
- **Prepared skewers.** Several ECF exercises need a forcing move first, such as a check that drives the king onto the line, before the skewer itself is possible.[^s1] Farnsworth likewise advises checking every forcing move, since a check can create a skewer that was not there before.[^s4]

## Example

In the diagram position (White to move), the black king on c7 and rook on h7 stand on the seventh rank. **1.Ra7+** checks the king, which must leave the rank, and **2.Rxh7** wins the rook. This is the same mechanism as the first ECF exercise, in which a rook check along the first rank forces the king to move and the rook behind it is captured.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Defended rear piece.** If the piece behind is protected and worth less than the attacker, the skewer may win nothing; the queen skewer in particular needs an unprotected target.[^s5]
- **Counter-threats.** The ECF exercises warn against tempting lines that fail to a better reply, for example a move that allows a mate in return.[^s1]
- **Lining up your own pieces.** The defensive lesson is to avoid placing king and rook, or queen and rook, on an open line or a diagonal the opponent's bishop controls.[^s5]

## Name

The term is British. Edward Winter traced it to Edgar Pennell, a Liverpool schoolteacher whose pupils were using it at the 1937 British Chess Federation Congress, as the magazine *CHESS* reported in 1939.[^s2] Other names exist: Kenneth Harkness proposed "X-ray attack" in *Chess Review* in 1947, a name that took hold especially in the United States; Milton Hanauer used "hurdle" in 1957; and French and Italian dictionaries use *enfilade* and *infilata*.[^s2]

## Connections

The skewer is a member of the family of **chess tactics**.[^s5] It **contrasts with** the **pin**, its mirror image: in a pin the less valuable piece is in front and cannot move without exposing the more valuable one behind, while in a skewer the valuable piece is in front and must move.[^s6][^s2] It also **contrasts with** the **fork**, which attacks two targets from one point rather than along one line. Like the **discovered attack**, it is often set up by a forcing check within a **combination**, it is a frequent motif in **rook endgames**, and a successful skewer **enables** a **material advantage**.[^s1][^s6]

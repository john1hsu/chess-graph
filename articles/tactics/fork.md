# Fork

A fork is a move with which one piece attacks two or more of the opponent's pieces at once. The English Chess Federation's training leaflets treat "double attack" and "fork" as names for the same idea: one piece hitting two targets in a single move.[^s2] Because the defender usually has time to answer only one threat, a fork normally wins material, and it is among the first tactics a developing player learns to look for.[^s4][^s5]

## How it works

The fork succeeds when the defender cannot meet both threats with one move. That happens when the targets are far apart, when neither can be protected while the other moves away, or when one of the threats is a check that must be answered first.[^s4] A fork that includes check is the most forcing version: the king has to move or the check has to be blocked, and the second target falls on the next move.[^s2][^s4]

Material arithmetic explains why even a "cheap" piece forking two valuable ones is so effective. On the conventional scale a knight or bishop is worth about three pawns, a rook five and a queen nine, so a knight that wins a queen or a rook, even at the cost of its own life, comes out well ahead.[^s6][^s3]

## Which pieces fork

- **Knight.** The classic forking piece. A knight attacks squares from which the enemy pieces it threatens cannot strike back, and its jumps are easy to overlook, so the knight fork is the usual starting point for studying tactics.[^s3][^s5] The ECF leaflet singles out forks of king and queen, and the "family fork" of king, queen and rook together.[^s1][^s4]
- **Pawn.** A pawn advancing between two pieces attacks both diagonally; since the pawn is the least valuable unit, either capture is a gain. The ECF examples include a pawn push that hits a knight and bishop, and a pawn check that forks the king and a rook.[^s2]
- **Queen, rook and bishop.** Long-range pieces can fork targets on opposite sides of the board. Queen forks are often combined with a mate threat, so the defender must parry the mate and lose the other piece; bishop and rook forks are common against loose pieces and in endings.[^s4][^s2]
- **King.** Even the king can fork two unprotected pieces next to it, as in an ECF exercise where the king attacks a rook and a bishop.[^s2]

## Setting up a fork

Forks rarely appear by accident; they are usually prepared by forcing play. Typical preparatory ideas in the ECF exercises are a check or a capture that drives a piece onto the fork square, an exchange or sacrifice on the square where the fork will land, and a pin that stops the defender from capturing the forking piece.[^s1][^s2] Farnsworth adds a positional point: knights posted on central and advanced squares are the ones that find forks, because from the fourth rank a knight can reach the opponent's back rank, and often the king, in one move.[^s3]

## Example

In the diagram position (White to move), White plays **1.Ne7+**. The knight checks the king on g8 and at the same time attacks the queen on c8; after the king moves, 2.Nxc8 wins the queen for nothing. This is the pattern of the first ECF knight-fork exercise, in which a knight check forks king and queen.[^s1]

## Pitfalls

- **Unprotected pieces and squares.** Loose pieces are the commonest fork targets, and a knight landing on an unguarded square near the enemy king can create a fork; checking which pieces are unprotected is a quick way to spot both your chances and your opponent's.[^s4]
- **Forks one move away.** A threatened fork is harder to see than an immediate one; opponents often walk into it because the forking piece is not yet attacking anything.[^s4]
- **Checking the follow-up.** A fork wins nothing if the defender has a counter-check, an in-between move or a capture of the forking piece; the ECF exercises repeatedly include lines where a tempting alternative fails to a better reply.[^s2]

## Connections

The fork belongs to the family of **chess tactics** and is a frequent building block of a **combination**, since sacrifices and checks are often played just to set it up.[^s1][^s2] It is closely **related** to the **pin**, which can prevent a forking piece from being captured,[^s1] and it **contrasts with** the **skewer**, where the two targets stand on one line instead of being attacked from a single point. Other double-threat motifs, the **discovered attack** and the **double check**, create two threats with two pieces instead of one, and an in-between move (**zwischenzug**) is a typical way to set up or refute a fork. A successful fork **enables** a **material advantage**, which is its usual purpose.[^s4][^s6]

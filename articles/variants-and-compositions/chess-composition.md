# Chess composition

Chess composition is the art of building artificial positions, usually with White to move, that set the solver a task. Britannica divides it into three broad families: endgame studies, orthodox problems and heterodox problems, with retrograde analysis as a further branch; good compositions are judged on originality, difficulty, beauty and economy.[^s1] The WFCC's Codex describes compositional chess as an independent form of chess activity that uses material found in, or derived from, the game to create artistic effects or constructional feats.[^s2]

## What a composition consists of

Under the Codex a composition normally has three parts: a position on the board, a stipulation in words (such as "White to play and win" or "mate in 3"), and a solution, and it is the creative work of one or more human authors.[^s2] The solution the author intends is the *author's solution*; beyond it, the content of a work may also include virtual play (tries), other play, and formal aspects.[^s2] The Codex groups compositions by stipulation into the historical families of studies, direct mates, selfmates and helpmates, plus newer groups, and separately recognises special types such as retroanalytical, mathematical and constructional works.[^s2] It also distinguishes works that follow the FIDE rules of play from those using modified rules, the fairy or heterodox genres with new pieces, conditions or boards.[^s2][^s1]

The families pose very different challenges. A study has no move limit and usually few pieces, so the difficulty lies in making scant material win or draw; a problem demands mate in a fixed, small number of moves and rarely looks like a game position.[^s1] In a selfmate White forces an unwilling Black to give mate, while in a helpmate the two sides cooperate.[^s1]

## Soundness and conventions

The central demand is uniqueness: a composition must be solvable only by the author's solution.[^s2] A *cook* is a solution that differs from the author's at the first move; a *dual* is an alternative way to fulfil the stipulation after the first move; a work is unsound if cooked, if it has a shorter solution, or if it has none.[^s2] Help-play works are also unsound if dualised, while studies tolerate many minor duals in the main line.[^s2]

Because a composed position has no game history, the Codex supplies conventions. A position is legal if it can be reached from the initial array, and in orthodox works an illegal position is not acceptable for composing tournaments unless their conditions allow it.[^s2] Castling is presumed permitted unless it can be proved impossible; an en passant capture on the first move is permitted only if it can be proved that the last move was the pawn's double step.[^s2] Unless stated otherwise, the fifty-move rule and the dead-position rule do not apply to solutions, except in retro problems.[^s2] Priority rests on first publication, and a work identical to an earlier one is anticipated and cannot receive an award.[^s2]

## A concrete case

The castling and en passant conventions show why composition is not simply "puzzles from games". In an over-the-board position the arbiter knows whether a pawn has just made a double step; in a diagram nobody does. So when a solver considers an en passant key, they must first prove from the position itself, by retrograde reasoning, that the double step was Black's last move; if it cannot be proved, the capture is unavailable, whereas castling is assumed to be available until disproved.[^s2]

## History and organisation

The earliest compositions are the *mansubat* of Arabic and Persian manuscripts, practical endgame positions; the modern problem style, in which a unifying theme replaced long, aimless mates, emerged in the mid-19th century, followed by English, German and Bohemian composing schools and, in the 20th century, extensive work on heterodox genres.[^s1] Composing tournaments have been held since the mid-19th century.[^s1]

International organisation began when FIDE's 1954 congress created a permanent subcommission for problems and studies; its first assembly met in Budapest in May 1956, named more than 70 international judges, announced the first individual world championship in composition and took on the task of drafting a code of rules, the later Codex.[^s4] In 1957 it agreed to publish a FIDE Album every three years, and the first album (1956–58) held 603 diagrams.[^s4] At its 53rd meeting, in Crete in October 2010, the Permanent Commission for Chess Composition (PCCC) renamed itself the World Federation for Chess Composition and adopted Swiss-registered statutes; the WFCC is the PCCC's successor and calls itself FIDE's natural partner for composition.[^s5][^s3]

As of November 2026, the statutes have the WFCC award, in conjunction with FIDE, composing titles (Grandmaster, International Master and FIDE Master for Chess Compositions), the International Judge title and solving titles. The composing titles depend on Album selections: a problemist needs at least 70 problems for the grandmaster title, a study composer at least 42 studies.[^s3] The WFCC's team event, the World Chess Composition Tournament (WCCT), is open to FIDE member federations, with eight sections from twomovers to retros.[^s6]

## Connections

Chess composition is the parent field of **chess problems** and the **endgame study**, and of genres such as the **helpmate** and **retrograde analysis**.[^s1][^s2] Its practice is described by the WFCC's **Codex of Chess Composition**, and the **World Federation for Chess Composition**, successor to FIDE's commission and still linked to **FIDE** through joint titles, organises it.[^s2][^s3][^s4] Korolkov's 1958 study **Excelsior**, in which White wins through six consecutive pawn captures, is a celebrated example.[^s1]

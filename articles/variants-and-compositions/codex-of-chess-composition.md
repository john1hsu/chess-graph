# Codex of Chess Composition

The Codex is the reference text that problemists, study composers, tourney directors and judges consult when they need to know whether a composed position is sound, legal or original. Its current version, published by the World Federation for Chess Composition (WFCC), is labelled "version Vilnius 2019".[^s1] Unlike the FIDE Laws of Chess for over-the-board play, it presents itself as largely descriptive: the introduction says Part One records existing practice, while the chapters on publication, priority and tournaments and the tournament annex offer guidance that no central body had previously provided.[^s1] A ChessBase column by the composer Siegfried Hornecker likewise stresses that the Codex is guidance rather than binding law, from which composers may deviate.[^s5]

## Structure

The Codex has two parts and two annexes.[^s1]

- **Part One, the chess composition.** General principles (a composition is a position, a stipulation and a solution created by one or more authors), the types of composition, soundness, and miscellaneous conventions.[^s1]
- **Part Two, the composition and the public.** Publication, priority, and composing and solving tournaments.[^s1]
- **Annex I** lists which lectures and solving events count as public presentation; **Annex II** gives model procedures for composing tourneys and sample rules taken from the World Chess Solving Championship.[^s1]

For orthodox works the Codex ties itself to the playing rules: a footnote names the edition of the FIDE Laws that applies.[^s2][^s1]

## Soundness: cooks, duals and short solutions

The heart of Part One is a vocabulary of defects. A *cook* is a solution that differs from the author's at the very first move; a *dual* is a second way of fulfilling the stipulation after the first move; a *short solution* reaches the goal in fewer moves than stated; and a work may also have no solution at all.[^s1] Any of the cook, the short solution or the absence of a solution makes a work unsound.[^s1] The treatment then splits by genre. In help-play, duals are also fatal, with a tolerated exception for promoting to pieces of partly similar power on the final move; in studies, an alternative way of meeting the stipulation is fatal but many minor main-line duals are accepted; in direct-mate and selfmate problems, alternative Black moves are not normally counted as duals, though judges may treat them as artistic flaws.[^s1] The Codex leaves the weight of each defect to the judge.[^s1]

## Conventions for diagrams

A diagram has no move history, so Chapter IV supplies defaults. A position is legal if it can be reached from the initial array by legal moves, and illegal positions are not accepted in orthodox tourneys unless the conditions allow them.[^s1] Castling is presumed legal unless it can be proved impossible, whereas an en passant capture on the first move is allowed only if it can be proved that the last move was the pawn's double step.[^s1] When castling and en passant rights depend on one another, the solution splits into mutually exclusive parts (partial retrograde analysis); if that fails for castling, the retro-strategy convention applies, under which whichever castling is executed first is deemed legal.[^s1] The fifty-move rule and the dead-position rule do not apply unless stated, except in retro problems, and a repetition draw counts positions across the proof game and the solution together.[^s1]

## Publication, priority and tournaments

First publication gives the author a priority date. Publication includes permanent print or electronic presentation, transient presentation in a generally accessible medium, and showing the work at a lecture or solving event listed in Annex I, after which it stays eligible for composing tourneys for two years.[^s1] For a work first published in a formal tourney award, the priority date is the tourney's closing date.[^s1] An unsound work loses its priority date unless a correction appears within three years, and a work identical to one with an earlier priority date is *anticipated* and cannot be honoured, while partial anticipation is left to the judge.[^s1] Chapter VII distinguishes informal tourneys, where entries are published before judging, from formal ones, where they are not, and sets out the duties of director and judge and a normal three-month window for claims after an award.[^s1]

## History and maintenance

FIDE's composition commission accepted the task of drawing up a code of rules for composition at its first meeting, in Budapest in 1956, and that code later became known as the Codex.[^s4] Hornecker credits the Croatian problemist Nenad Petrović, later the commission's president, with laying it down.[^s5] A 2001 copy of the Permanent Commission's text already shows the two-part structure and describes the tournament chapter as new ground.[^s6] The WFCC keeps a Codex Committee: at the 2019 Vilnius congress it carried changes that replaced a reference to the obsolete 1996 FIDE Laws with the 1 January 2018 edition and extended the list of borrowed articles of the Laws from 1–5 to 1–5, 9.2 and 9.3.[^s2] At the 2022 Fujairah congress the committee had nothing to report, and as of November 2026 the WFCC still publishes the Vilnius 2019 version.[^s3][^s1]

## Connections

The Codex is the rulebook of **chess composition** as a whole and of its branches, the **chess problem**, the **endgame study** and the **helpmate**, each of which it treats separately when judging soundness.[^s1] Its legality, castling and en passant conventions are what make **retrograde analysis** a solving method, and they adapt the over-the-board rules for **castling** and **en passant** to positions without a history.[^s1] It is developed by the **World Federation for Chess Composition** and depends on the **FIDE Laws of Chess** for the rules of play.[^s2] Its publication chapters define **priority and anticipation**, the basis on which tourney awards are made.[^s1]

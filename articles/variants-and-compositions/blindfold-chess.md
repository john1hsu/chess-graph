# Blindfold chess

Blindfold chess is ordinary chess in which one or both players cannot see the board. Moves are called out in notation, an assistant or the opponent plays them on a real board out of the blindfolded player's view, and the player keeps the whole position in the mind. The rules of the game do not change; only the player's access to the position does. Its showpiece is the blindfold simultaneous display, in which a single master plays many opponents at once without sight of any of the boards.[^s1][^s2]

## History and records

Blindfold play is older than modern chess: the standard history by Eliot Hearst and John Knott opens with a chapter on play "even before Philidor".[^s2] In Europe the turning point was François-André Philidor, whose London displays against two boards in 1782 and three in 1783 stand at the head of the record list.[^s1] The 19th century brought a steady climb: Kieseritzky played four in Paris in 1851, Louis Paulsen raised the mark in a series of American displays from five in 1857 to fifteen at Dubuque in 1859, and Johannes Zukertort reached sixteen in London on 16 December 1876.[^s1]

Harry Nelson Pillsbury pushed the record above twenty, playing 22 games in Moscow on 14 December 1902 with a score of 17 wins, 4 draws and 1 loss.[^s1] Vladimir Ostrogsky reportedly played 23 in Moscow in 1904, and the record then passed through Réti, Breyer, Alekhine and Koltanowski, who reached 34 boards in Edinburgh in 1937.[^s1][^s6] Miguel Najdorf played 40 in Rosario in 1943 and 45 in São Paulo on 24–25 January 1947, scoring 39 wins, 4 draws and 2 losses.[^s1] Hearst and Knott, writing in 2009, regarded Najdorf's 45 as the rightful record and rejected larger claims, including János Flesch's reported 52 boards in 1960 and Dimitrije Bjelica's 56 in 1997.[^s1][^s6] They also named Alexander Alekhine the greatest blindfold player of all time.[^s1]

The modern record belongs to Timur Gareyev. On 3–4 December 2016 at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, he played 48 opponents blindfold over 19 hours and 9 minutes, winning 35, drawing 7 and losing 6, while riding an exercise bicycle; Guinness World Records recognised it as the largest blindfold simultaneous, beating the 46 games set by Marc Lang in Germany on 27 November 2011.[^s4][^s5] As of November 2026 Guinness still lists Gareyev's 48 games as the record.[^s5]

## How players do it

Blindfold champions do not hold a photograph of the board. In Alfred Binet's 1890s questionnaires, strong blindfold players reported an abstract representation that ignored the colour or style of the pieces, and later experiments by Pertti Saariluoma found that what makes blindfold play possible is chess knowledge stored in long-term memory, used through visuo-spatial working memory, rather than any unusual gift for imagery.[^s3] Players differ in their aids: Reuben Fine found a blank board more hindrance than help, whereas George Koltanowski found an external board useful.[^s3] Fine also described interference between similar games as a hazard of simultaneous displays, and summarised each position with a few key statements to keep the games apart.[^s3]

Hearst and Knott devote separate chapters to the techniques of blindfold champions and to "the supposed health hazards" of the practice; they conclude that the dangers have been much exaggerated, and they note that a fairly strong amateur can learn to play one or two games blindfold.[^s2][^s6]

## Practical advice and pitfalls

Since a fairly strong amateur can manage a game or two, blindfold play is within reach of serious club players, and Réti thought it worth learning early.[^s6] The research points to where trouble comes from: recall depends on meaningful patterns, so masters remember real or legal sequences far better than illegal ones, and pieces that matter are remembered better than peripheral ones.[^s3] In practice that means losing track of a quiet piece far from the action, and confusing similar positions across games, are the usual failures; summarising each position in a few key ideas, as Fine did, is a known remedy.[^s3]

## Connections

Blindfold chess is a way of playing **chess** under its normal rules, like **correspondence chess** a form defined by the conditions of play rather than by new rules.[^s2] It **depends on visualization**, and it has been one of the oldest test cases for the **cognitive psychology of chess** since Binet.[^s3] Its records were set in **simultaneous exhibitions**, and it was **championed by** Philidor, Alekhine and Najdorf, three of the players most closely tied to its history.[^s1][^s6]

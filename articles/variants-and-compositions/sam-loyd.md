# Sam Loyd

**Samuel Loyd** (born Philadelphia, 30 January 1841; died New York, 10 April 1911) was an American composer of chess problems who later became the best-known puzzlemaker of his era.[^s1] He trained as a steam and mechanical engineer but eventually lived from his problems and puzzles.[^s1] The World Chess Hall of Fame calls him "The Puzzle King" and credits him with turning problem composition into a complex art; he was inducted into the U.S. Chess Hall of Fame in 1987.[^s2]

## Problem career

Loyd started composing as a boy. His first problem appeared in the *New York Saturday Courier* on 14 April 1855, and a first prize in a *New York Clipper* competition followed in October 1856.[^s3] Britannica says that by 17 he was regarded as America's leading composer.[^s1] He became problem editor of the *Chess Monthly*, the magazine associated with Paul Morphy; sources differ on the start date, with the Hall of Fame and MacTutor giving 1857 and Britannica 1860.[^s1][^s2][^s3] He also ran the chess column of the New York *Illustrated News* in 1860.[^s4]

The Hall of Fame notes that his most intense period of composing lasted barely four years, yet it produced his best-known works, the **Excelsior** and the **Steinitz Gambit** problem.[^s2] MacTutor credits a problem he published in the *Musical World* in 1859 as the first **retrograde analysis** problem, and records that he contributed five problems to the *American Chess Journal*, one asking how stalemate could arise in ten moves.[^s3] In 1877–78 he wrote a weekly chess column for the *Scientific American Supplement*, which he illustrated with woodcut portraits of masters.[^s2][^s4] Those columns supplied most of the 500 problems in his book *Chess Strategy* (1878), which also sets out his views on solving and composing.[^s3][^s4] Alain C. White's *Sam Loyd and his Chess Problems* (1913) collected his work after his death.[^s3]

Loyd's problems were valued for surprise and elegance. One 1875 journal compared them to Mozart's music, and Martin Gardner praised his gift for new and unexpected themes.[^s3][^s4] His prize three-mover below shows the style: its key looks like the worst move on the board.[^s5]

## Example: the "Steinitz Gambit" (mate in three)

Loyd's prize-winner in the Novelty Tourney of the Canadian magazine *Checkmate* is given above as the key position (White to move and mate in three).[^s5] ChessBase dates it 1903; Winter's notes cite the prize as 1904 and quote a critic who objected to its duals and untidy by-play.[^s4][^s5] The key is the outrageous **1.Ke2**, which walks the king toward a black army and allows roughly ten checks. White has a mate after every one. After 1...f1=N+ 2.Rf2+ Kxe4 3.Bd3# or 1...Kxe4 2.Bd3+ Kd4 3.Rf4#, the king's exposure costs nothing, and after 1...f1=Q+ White simply continues 2.Ke3.[^s5] The name points to the Steinitz Gambit of the Vienna, in which White also plays an early Ke2; Loyd said the originality lay in the king leaving safety to defy so many checks.[^s5] Alain C. White's biography, as summarised by ChessBase, reports that the problem needed two small repairs before it was sound.[^s5]

## Over the board

Loyd was a capable player but not a leading one. The Hall of Fame says that at his peak he ranked among the best in the United States, and that his taste for elaborate combinations held him back.[^s2] He played in the Paris tournament of 1867, where MacTutor says he did poorly; his win as Black against Celso Golmayo there is his best-known game, although Winter lists conflicting accounts of it.[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Puzzles and disputed claims

Loyd later moved from chess problems to general puzzles and games, and around 1896 he and his son began a widely syndicated puzzle column.[^s1] He claimed to have invented the Fifteen Puzzle, a claim Britannica calls false and that Winter's notes, citing Jerry Slocum and Dic Sonneveld, attribute to Noyes Chapman instead.[^s1][^s4] Winter also questions several chess stories credited to Loyd, including the short stalemate game often given under his name and his account of a game played as a boy.[^s4]

## Connections

Loyd is a central figure in the history of **chess problems** and **chess composition** in America.[^s1][^s2] He is the author of the **Excelsior** and an early pioneer of **retrograde analysis**, and his ten-move stalemate task links him to **stalemate** puzzles.[^s2][^s3] He worked on the magazine associated with **Paul Morphy** and named his best-known three-mover after the gambit of **Wilhelm Steinitz**.[^s1][^s5] He **played in** Paris 1867 and is honoured by the **World Chess Hall of Fame**.[^s2][^s3]
